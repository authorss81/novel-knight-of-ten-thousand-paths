## Volume 13, Batch 0001 — Chapters 601 to 610, "The Cheapest Thing on the List"

**601** — Embermonth 26, YR 320, a Friday and not a market day, the second hour, the room opened on a day it is shut. A carrier lost two days at a ford and brought the band; the copyist of about twenty-six reads the fourth sheet twice and says *it is not a return*. Four hands of writing, no seal, nothing on the back, and **Aren Kest at the top of the first page**. It stands a keeper down for nine days and shuts a road for the same nine days, the matter being under question, and names the road by its two ends. The woman of about twenty-nine says a name at the top is the whole of what makes a direction. The man of about thirty-four with a sheet says it names a district, not a road. The copyist says the second true thing of the day: **then it is a direction, and a direction is obeyed**, and nobody has to, and everybody will, because a world in which nothing with a name at the top is obeyed is a worse world, and that is true. Cost: the copyist's, 214.

**602** — Wolfmonth 2, a Thursday and a market morning, the fifth hour. The hearing stands, said in the street first. **The man of about fifty-eight with a badge refuses to call the sheet false**, because it is false only when somebody can show it false and this room has no way of showing anything false, and put a name at the bottom and it is slander today, leave the name at the top and it is a direction and no county can act. Then the second true thing: **a direction obeyed because of a name and not because of an office can be written by anybody in nine counties with a pen, and in thirty-one years it has never been done, and he wants it on a bare leaf with his name at the top of it.** The copyist will not put his name at the top, having made that point herself in the first week of a month, and writes seven words instead: *the man with the badge said it.* The man of about thirty-four with a sheet asks whether he is to send it and is told that is not the badge's office, and that is exactly why it will be done. Cost: the sheet man's, 232.

**603** — Wolfmonth 6, a Monday, the room shut, the counter under the weigh-house. Two sheets about the same four miles of channel, ten days apart in the writing and three weeks apart in the books, in two different series, and one of them is a lie. **The copyist says the rule is the wall and the wall is what a road is put behind**, so that one bad return cannot be set beside one good return and made to look like a pattern, and here are two of the sheets on it. Nobody in that room is permitted to notice that a road can be two things for three weeks. The woman of about thirty-four who keeps the key says out loud, once, that Aren Kest and the keeper of the room were in the lane together on Saturday and not on Thursday, the hearing stood on both, and nobody has said why, and she is not asking. The two sheets are laid side by side on the counter at the seventh hour, which is not a practice, and go back into two books. Cost: the woman of about twenty-nine's, 198.

**604** — Wolfmonth 11, a Saturday and a market morning, second hour to ninth, the culvert at the town's gate. A cart's off-side wheel, a hand's depth of silt, water across the made stone. A boy of about seventeen fetches the man with the limp and he clears it with about nine barrows and a hook. **Three courses of sill laid in lime with the middle course gone between two clean joints, and a new course laid dry across the bed** by men from the district, three weeks before, on an instruction on a sheet with a name at the top. A man of about fifty who lays stone: *we were told to keep the water off the road and we did.* At about the fourth hour his left palm is warm in the silt and he does not look at it. The only number taken off that street all day is the number of barrows. Cost: his, 245.

**605** — Wolfmonth 16, a Thursday and a market morning. The hearing stands and nobody is seated. **The man of about fifty-eight with a badge asks, in the room, in front of nine people, what roads are open.** Seven are named in plain terms with no figure and no distance, the seventh *and a small one, about which I am going to say only that it is small*. Nobody writes a list, because a list with a name at the top can be argued with and a list without one can be added to and taken from. **He answered for the ferry city and the fruit valley and said out loud that he answered for the two he has been in, and the badge said the true thing: you have not chosen, you have been the last one there, and a man who has been last somewhere cannot be argued with at all.** And he says the limit out loud and names nothing: seven, no eighth, and a thing he has run out of that does not ask his permission. The woman of about thirty-four who keeps the key says she has never once in four and a half years been asked which roads are open. Cost: hers, 249.

**606** — Wolfmonth 19, a Sunday, the ninth open crossing, the second hour, a lime kiln on a slope. **Nell Craddock, 40, runs the kiln alone and has crossed twice in two years and was sent by nobody.** A boy of about eleven gives the whole count straight off: nine open crossings, four shut weeks, the longest eleven days. **She will not be the first to say the number, and the reason is on the page: the first number anybody says out loud about a place is the number that place has for the rest of its life, and she is the one who walks it.** A woman of about sixty has held the far end for about thirty-one years and has never been asked what the road is. The finding arrives in his own mouth: **somebody local has to say it out loud in front of people who will remember, and be wrong if they are wrong, and be believed about everything else for the rest of their life.** He walks a quarter of a mile in front of the sledge and is not thanked. Cost: hers, 182.

**607** — Wolfmonth 23, a Thursday and a market morning. **Garvin Slade, 47, brings a page with seven lines, six marked, and the seventh with nothing against it and not struck out.** He is right about the strain, twice, out loud, and does not hide the cost of being wrong. **The block's truest sentence: a road with a figure on it is a weight; a road with nothing on it is not a light thing, it is a gap, and a gap is not carried by anybody, and a gap goes first, and I have never once in my life had to be cruel to close a gap.** The copyist says she could name the gap inside an hour and will not, because the three series are not derived from one another and making one out of another hands nine counties a single number anybody can check. **Aren Kest says the seventh has a woman on it who runs a lime kiln and a boy of about eleven who can say its whole count, and does not say the rest.** Six marks asked for and none given; the page goes back inside a coat. Cost: Slade's, 217.

**608** — Wolfmonth 26, a Sunday, the room shut, the counter underneath. The man of about thirty-four with a sheet cuts the direction to about nine lines, prints it in a district hand, and posts it on a Friday. **A gatekeeper with about eleven years at the ferry stone reads it out and does not check the name, and the page says why: the man is not in the district and nobody there can say who he is.** The keeper of the road is told at the gate, asked nothing, shown nothing, and goes home for nine days. **Nothing rotted, nobody was late, nobody was hurt, and no wages went anywhere.** The badge says the true thing: *it does not need to be believed, it needs to be delivered, and there is not one person in this county who has done a dishonest thing*, and then says out loud in the lane that he said in a room on the second of a month that it had never been done, and it has now been done, and it cost about nine hundred pence of paper and nobody's livelihood. Cost: the badge's, 260.

**609** — Frostmonth 5, a Tuesday, nine days up. The keeper of the road is back and the road is open and the grass is coming up in the middle of it. **She is not here to complain. She is here to say a number**, and the number is nineteen columns of days-passable with nine days taken out in a hand that is not hers, on a slate in her apron, and it is hers and stays in her apron. **The nine days cost her nothing, and the whole of the matter is that for nine days nobody in about nine houses knew whether she was stood down or had gone home for a bad week**, and the reason there was no arrangement is that the standing down came off a gate and the page has her name nowhere on it. She asks for one thing and is told there is no way to agree it. The badge says *I want you to do nothing, and I have never in thirty-one years wanted a man to do nothing.* Cost: hers, 225.

**610** — Frostmonth 7, a Thursday and a market morning four days off, the tenth open crossing, the sixth hour, the kiln again. She came again with a boy and a rope, and said she knew what the seventh one was and that she was not going to stop him, and **then asked him do you want it, twice, the second time in a working voice with a spade in her hand.** The rider: the second she knows, she can stop bringing him things; and there is a woman nine days off who has been stood down nine days for a page with his name on it and does not know it was his name. **He did not answer, and the chapter says why on the page: the answer he had was a yes, and a yes said on that slope at the sixth hour would have made him the seventh thing on the page she had just described.** The boy of about eleven stands four paces off with a sledgehammer up and will not remember any of it. **Nobody thanked anybody and nobody wrote it down.** Cost: his, 213.

## Volume 13, Batch 0002 — Chapters 611 to 620, "Three Blanks and Three Marks"

**611 — Hearthmonth the first, YR 320, a Sunday, the first and second hour at the counter under the weigh-house, the first of a month, 2,757 words, column 202 pence.** The room is shut and the counter is taking about four hundred and nine parish rolls a month. The man of about fifty-eight with a badge says the hearing stands in the street, says there is no stair open, and says it at a counter so that the counter knows it was said. Garvin Slade comes at the second hour with a page that is not the page: a bigger sheet folded in two, printed twice, with six ruled spaces against six lines and the seventh bare and not struck out. He says what is on it, and the one new sentence is that a mark in a space is not a vote but a statement that you will not stand in the way. The man with the badge asks whether the page names anybody, and the page names six places and one man and cannot be shown false. The copyist of about twenty-six says the thing that turns the morning over: it is not a form, a form has a head that says what the thing is for, and this one's head says who sent it, and it is the first thing in four and a half years she cannot tell you what it is. A woman of about fifty comes four days on a cart and says she would give the man a figure for her own road if he were asking about her own road and he is not. Aren Kest says the seventh can be named and has no figure and that a description is not a weight, and that the man may write down which road it is and it will not be a figure. The copyist writes one question on a bare leaf, *what a page with six ruled spaces and no head is*, and it goes in the drawer under the counter because the room is shut and she will not open a room on a Sunday.

**612 — Hearthmonth the fifth, a Thursday and a market morning, the second to the seventh hour in the room and then the lane, 2,403 words, column 215 pence.** The page comes back after four days with one mark in it, in the space against the waterworks country, put in by a woman who keeps a channel by hand for about twenty-two years. The man of about thirty-four with a sheet says a person can only agree not to stand in the way about a thing they can name in a figure. Then the second fact of the morning: the keeper of the room did not come down to the lane, and the copyist says it out loud and nobody agrees with her, and what she says next is the volume's finding, that nothing in that room depends on the woman who keeps it being in it and three series would go on being kept correctly for a month with her out of the lane, and that she has wanted a room like that since she was about nineteen, and that she is saying it in front of the woman. The man with the sheet then says the block's finding about the page itself: a man who will not put a mark in a space is a man who has refused, and the page has no way to hold a refusal, so the refusal comes back as nothing, and nothing in a ruled space is the same as a space nobody has reached. Aren Kest is in the room to the fifth hour and says a page that cannot be disproved is not a page that is wrong, and that a refusal that comes back as a blank means a road everybody refuses will have been shut by a printing press, and that the page is only dangerous for about nine days and after that it is a piece of paper in a coat. He goes down. She comes up at the seventh hour behind the copyist. They pass in the lane and neither says anything.

**613 — Hearthmonth the seventh, a Saturday and a market morning, the second to the seventh hour, 2,324 words, column 229 pence.** A man of about thirty-one from the road office comes four days on a made road with a letter and no paper entitling him to an answer, because his office has a page going round nine counties with a line on it for a road it cannot find. He asks the three questions a clerk is taught to ask, and is told no three times, and then says he will read his own correct reading of the answer before he puts it in his letter: a rule that keeps three series apart is a rule that says no two returns may be read together, and a page that wants six marks cannot be read any other way, so the rule is a wall in front of the only door there is and the wall is doing the work of the man who wrote the page. Nobody says he is wrong. The copyist is not defended and does not want to be; she says the rule was made for a different reason, that a pattern is a thing a district can be shown and the road will not care which half of it is wrong, and then she says the cost out loud, that four years of correctly kept series is why two warnings about the same four miles of channel sat in two books for three weeks, and that she laid them side by side once on a counter and put them back, and that there is no second practice in the room to replace the one she has just called a blind spot. The page comes back with two more blanks, three of six, and a man from a road office calls them refusals and the man with the sheet says he has three blanks and cannot tell. The badge-man asks whether he is going to fill the fourth head and is told there is no form for it and nobody may rule a fifth.

**614 — Hearthmonth the ninth, a Monday and not a market day, the second to the ninth hour at the culvert under the made road at the town's gate, 2,240 words, column 234 pence.** The frost has been and gone twice. The man of about fifty who lays stone comes at the fifth hour with a hod and says he had about nine days to decide whether to come at all and is glad he said that first. He goes under the arch and finds the course laid dry across the bed is gone, and under it a scour about a foot and a half across and a foot and a half deep at the low side, and the bed below it hard and clean, and the two lime-keyed courses where they always were. He says the middle course is the one that goes, because the top course has the road on it and the bottom course is keyed to the sides, and the middle one has nothing above it that would fall and nothing below it that would hold it, and nobody has ever asked him to look at a middle course. He digs to sound twice, lays three courses in lime, and refuses to lay a fourth, and says so before he starts rather than after, because afterwards you would have had a reason. The boy of about seventeen is paid a penny into his own hand. The man will not have the day written down anywhere, because in about nine years there will be a man who says it was always three courses and he will be right about the three and wrong about everything else. And he says the true thing of the afternoon: the worst job he ever saw was not one where the stone was wrong, it was one where the instruction was right.

**615 — Hearthmonth the twelfth, a Thursday and a market morning, the second to the seventh hour, 2,189 words, column 243 pence.** A man of about thirty-six walks four days with a paper in his coat and asks the room for a figure for the seventh road, because he has read that a road with a figure on it is a weight and a road with nothing on it is a gap and he cannot do anything about it for about nine days. The woman of about twenty-nine who keeps the room gives the correct answer in three parts: the figure is not the room's to give and not the room's to hold and there is no figure in here for most of the roads people ask her about; a road may be described only by its own answer, and if this room makes one series out of another for one man with a paper then every number it holds becomes a number that man is entitled to; and a figure about that road has to be said out loud by a person who walks it, in the place, in front of people who will remember, by somebody who can be moved off it, and there is nobody in that room who can be moved off it and the room is not going to ask, because the moment a room asks a person for a figure the room becomes the party that wants one. She then says plainly that the page will go round twice while he walks four days, that the correct position is not a comfortable one, and that the road will be shut inside a fortnight and there is nothing anybody in that room can do about it. He asks what to take back and is told to write that he asked, that the room would not give, and that the room was right. The copyist repeats that she could name which of the seven is the gap inside an hour and will not, and nobody defends her. The badge-man says there is no form in nine counties for asking a person for a figure or for refusing to give one, and the only place either has ever been written is a bare leaf in a drawer, and that is not a way of doing things.

**616 — Hearthmonth the fourteenth, a Saturday and a market morning, about the second hour in the lane, 2,381 words, column 251 pence.** The man of about thirty-four with a sheet asks Aren Kest to look at a thing outside, and shows him a copy of the forged page in his own one hand, made at the counter and carried to the district and kept in a satchel for about a month, and tells him in advance that he was not going to keep it and is not going to put it in the drawer upstairs. At the top of the first page, in the place where a name goes on a thing that is going to be acted on, is his own name, in a hand that is not his. He reads it twice. Nothing is at the bottom and nothing is on the back. He says the page is not his and is also nobody's, and the man with the sheet tells him the second half of that: a man who says a page is not his is making a claim, a claim has to be believed, and being believed is a thing you are given and not a thing you do, and there is no form in that room for it. He then says the third thing himself, that about four hundred people hold one true thing about him and about four four hundred hold another and he said no when he was asked whether he would put them straight. Then he names the one thing he could do and says he is not going to do it: he could stand in a lane on a market morning and deny the page in front of nine people, and a man with a limp denying a page in a market is a man telling nine strangers which of the two sets he is in, and he has not asked either set anything. The man with the sheet writes on the back of the copy, unasked, that the man named was shown it on that day and did not say the writing was his. The page has also come back with a second mark, from a man on the coast who keeps his count in tides and who put a mark in because he wants the arithmetic honest and because he is the only person within four days of the gap.

**617 — Hearthmonth the fifteenth, a Sunday, the third to the seventh hour at the counter, 2,206 words, column 262 pence.** Garvin Slade says he has been right about the water and is going to be right about a second, smaller thing: a direction that leaves a room on a Friday and is read at a gate on the Friday has no check in it, and a keeper has not been told and there is nobody to tell her. The fix is one day. The man of about thirty-four with a sheet then says what it costs, which is nothing about any road and the whole of his office, because his office is the day. He says a delayed thing has nobody's name on the delay: the blame moves off the gate onto the room, and the room has a door and a number, and he has a face. The badge-man says a rule needs a head and this room has none and neither has the district, and a rule that is right and has nowhere to go is worth exactly nothing, and this is the first time in thirty-one years he has watched one of them be right. Slade says he knew that when he said it and that nobody in nine counties has ever asked him what it costs. Aren Kest then says the argument out loud, in a working voice, to about four people at a counter: a mark is not a vote, a person can only agree to be held about a thing they can name in a figure, and in about nine days nobody will be able to tell a man who said no from a page nobody got to. He is asked what he would do and he does not answer, and the chapter gives the reason and it is not modesty. He says he is going to go and clear a grating because there are nine barrows of silt on that street and nobody has taken them.

**618 — Hearthmonth the sixteenth, a Monday and not a market morning, the second to the eighth hour on a made road four days down, 2,120 words, column 271 pence.** Ordinary work: a bridge over a ditch with about nine yards of parapet gone on the low side, three men and a boy of about seventeen and a cart and a load of stone, and the wages counted out on the sound parapet into nine hands. A carter carrying the district's paper stops and leaves a page on the parapet, and the man of about fifty who keeps the made road reads it standing up and marks a space in his own line without any speech, and says the reason when asked: because I can be found, because he has a figure in yards, because if he is wrong about anything on that page there is somebody he can be told about it and he can say so to his face, and a mark from anybody who cannot be found is worth nothing at all. A woman of about fifty arrives having walked about nine hundred paces and asks for a ditch, about nine hundred paces of it, nine days of three men, about four hundred pence of a county's money, and she has brought the numbers because he will not take her word for one. He says he will cut it in the next dry week, that there is no hearing and no paper, and that he is cutting it because the water is coming over a made road and the made road is his, and he would have cut it in the thaw about four hundred paces at a time over two years and got it wrong in about nine places. She says the one true thing: a page wants a mark and a ditch wants a spade, and only one of them has a head over it, and the one that has a head over it is going round nine counties. He tells the carter not to write the day down. Nobody thanked anybody.

**619 — Hearthmonth the eighteenth, a Wednesday and not a market morning, the second hour at the gate at the low end of a made road, 3,423 words, column 283 pence.** The man of about fifty-eight with a badge has come four days on his own account because a road he keeps is on a page, and says so first, and refuses to say the rest of it again at a gate. The gatekeeper of about eleven years reads the page out a second time to about nine people who did not hear it the first time, and does not check the name, for the same reason as last time. The woman of about fifty asks what her own line is for and is told that her line is the sixth, that the sixth is to be kept, and that the page says nothing at all about the sixth because the sixth is assumed, and that about nine people at a gate have been asked to agree to shut a road on a page that has never once said in nine counties that the road they are being asked to keep exists. She then puts a mark in the seventh space and says why, in a working voice: an empty space is a road nobody is carrying, and she would rather be argued with than be left alone on a page. She reads her count out loud at the gate: nineteen columns, one for each year, of the days that road was passable out of the days in that year, the last column unfinished, and a nine taken out of it in a hand that is not hers that she has never asked about. She says again that she would like somebody in that county to tell her which way her count runs. The man with the badge says nothing for half an hour and the chapter gives the reason. A carter waiting with his horse yoked asks whether this means a road can now be shut on a count, and she says no, and that a count shuts nothing and is the only thing anybody can be argued with, and that one time is one time and nobody may cite it, and the badge-man says *it is not a practice* and she agrees.
 **The block's fifth beat, added here by the verification and repair pass and in no other chapter.** The chapter already held the setup: a carter asks whether this means a road can be shut on a count, the woman of about fifty says no, the badge-man says then it is not a practice, and she says nobody may cite it. **A woman of about forty-three who keeps a road that is not one of the six is at the same gate.** Her line is the fifth.
