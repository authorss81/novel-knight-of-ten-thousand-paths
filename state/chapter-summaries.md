## Volume 09, Batch 0001 — Chapters 401 to 410, "Nine Words and a Road"

**Days 931 to 972, the twenty-sixth of Mudmonth YR 318 to the seventh of Longlight YR 318, a Monday to a Sunday, forty-two days inclusive, two market mornings at 936 and 957. The year does not turn inside Volume 09 and the whole of it is YR 318. Block 0001 is 22,935 words in ten chapters, counted file by file on a plain whitespace split with the title line included, and the manuscript is 1,312,189 in four hundred and ten chapters. The block's argument in one sentence: *a name that nobody has to answer to is the only kind of record that cannot be got at, and the block finds nine practices that nobody is keeping and one name that everybody knows, and it loses the name in a market by accident on the seventh day.* Nothing in the ten chapters crosses, nothing is explained, no System panel appears, no stage or ledger or qualification word appears, and no name is spent on anything but three working people.**

**Ch 401 · the twenty-sixth of Mudmonth, a Monday, not a market day · the kilns, two days west · the song, and the ninth line held.** A yard of four lime kilns, four families not related for about sixty years, sharing a yard, a water butt, a cart and a song on four different terms none of which is written down. **The fourth kiln is cold since the middle of Harvestmonth and is the first thing a buyer sees from the road, and about forty people know what the buyer thinks and none of them has said it.** **Brenna Tull, 44**, runs the second kiln, calls the protagonist *Aren* once, and has kept a figure on every one of the three things he has done in two years. The draw: a hole made small at the second hour, nine rounds over about nine hours, a bar, a hook, a wheelbarrow, and **a man who has done it twenty-two years can put a word on any one of about seventy and does not choose.** The song in full, nine lines, in parts, on the beat of the bar coming down on the rim — **Ask the far side / Two books / Never a person twice / Read it yourself / Say it in a room / Leave the name off / Anybody may come / Feed the stranger first / — nothing.** **Ivo Serrat, 17**, wheels barrows and has carried glass on that road for four years; he has about four hundred entries in a book of his own that are figures and marks and no words, he can count a barrow of bricks and cannot read, he has never shown it to anybody, and he says so before anybody asks. **The ninth line is ruled in his book and empty, ruled four times because the page wears, and a clerk of about sixty would say about four in ten of the nine hundred such lines in that book were meant to be filled in.** She will not sing the ninth in a yard with a stranger in it, and the reason she gives is that a road said once in a yard of four kilns is a road nine people have heard, and nineteen years of not saying it has been undone by a man standing still for nine hours. Cost: **nine loads of peat at 46 pence running to 414, eleven sacks of quicklime at 22, 436 pence, nine marks and four pence, and none of the four who drew it paid out of any part of it.** Ending: discovery.

**Ch 402 · the first of Greenmonth, a Saturday, a market morning · a back room over a cooper's, ten people, about nine hundred in the street · nine words and no column.** The room is taken for the day at four pence for a man of about twenty, who is told twice not to put a head on the sheet and rules nine lines for eight words. **The first of the eight is asked about four times and four people who have never met each other answer it, and the man of about forty-seven who stood with his back to the board says afterwards that he could tell they had never been in a room by the shape of the answers.** The eighth takes the room for forty minutes: a woman of about fifty at the salt warehouse has put a pot on at the back of her house since Embermonth of the year before last for a man with no shoes, has a figure, will not say it, and says why in nine words — *if I have a figure and you have a figure, then there is a number of me.* **A man of about thirty-four says it is eleven. Nobody writes eleven down, because the man of about twenty had the pen in his pocket, and he will be a man of about fifty in that town.** The ninth line is asked about four times and answered four ways — a name, a place, not a word at all, a line — and a man of about twenty-four with a load of glass says a line ruled for nothing is a line somebody meant to fill in and has four hundred of them in a book, and does not say where, and does not say he cannot read it. The man of about twenty asks whether he wants a column headed *who*; Aren says no, and gives the reason in about thirty words, and the man of about thirty-three with a barrow completes it: **nine rows and one of them would be a man and eight of them would be a thing a man does.** The man of about forty-seven argues the other side for nine minutes, unprompted, and it is not resolved. **A woman of about twenty-nine is in the room all day, is not asked anything, says she has nothing when she is asked what she makes of it, and no one asks her twice.** The sheet stays on the bench-board; the door is not locked and the room is let by the day. Ending: discovery.

**Ch 403 · the fifth of Greenmonth, a Wednesday, not a market day · the arch over a beck, nine miles off the kilns · a mark under a stone.** A single arch, forty years old, about nine feet of dressed approach on either side and nothing on the arch, which is right. **A road nine miles long called Coldharbour, and the harbour nine miles further on is a field, and the road has been the road to a harbour for about four hundred.** The beck is about eleven inches over the sill and the ring is nine feet up, and a man of thirty-two with a limp has to come down onto both knees first. **A mason of about sixty-eight is under there with a lamp on his belt: do not put a hand on the ring, because a hand takes the skin off it and in nine years there is a mark on that arch that is not the mason's mark.** **The mark is on the underside of the ninth voussoir from the left, cut into the bed — the face nobody sees, which is why a mason uses it — and the ninth is not special.** A mason counts a ring off his own hand in the dark, and a ring is wider at the haunch than at the crown, and that is the one thing everybody who has built one knows and nobody who has only looked at one does. **Saber Vahl, 68**, cut the mark forty years ago at twenty-eight, has never seen a man in that county who knew it, and has never once said so out loud. **A clerk came out twice and asked whether he had put anything on the stones, and he said no both times, because he wrote it down, and a man who says no twice is a man who has made a decision.** He will not do the top course: it wants two men, four days, a new stone and a barrow, and **a man who has not seen the stone in nine years does not put his mark on it, and my trade is arches.** His want, said once: there was an arch over a drain at a house in Cask in Longlight of the year before last, he was not taken, he was drunk, both men are dead, and **he is after a day's work at four shillings and sixpence and is not after anything else.** Then the fuse: **he has been saying that road's name to anybody who stops at that arch for forty years, about nine men, not one of whom he knows and not one of whom knows what he is, and there is not one piece of paper in this kingdom with it in it except a man's mouth.** He says it twice in a field beside a beck, and nobody is there to be asked about having said it. Cost: a cart at four shillings a day for two days, 96 pence, and the mason at two days, 108 pence, **204 pence in all, and neither of the two of them said a word to the other about a word.** Ending: discovery.

**Ch 404 · the tenth of Greenmonth, a Monday, not a market day · a room over a cooper's on a street of working yards · the plan, and the refusal, and the reason he cannot have it.** **The sheet is still on the bench-board on the ninth day and has been seen four times by a cooper's wife who does not move it because furniture is not swept round.** The keeper of records is **Mara Vey, 29, four years and a half**, down on the fixed-day cart with about four hundred sheets in a banded bundle tied at one corner with a cord, and she is the person in the room who knows most about what a copy is by about nineteen years. The plan is made in eleven minutes: **eight words into eight places, and the places chosen for what they are, and the sheet itself to go to the eighth and not be a copy of the sheet.** He has four places and two of the people will say no to a sheet of paper and the third cannot read. **He says in about forty words, standing up, that he will hold the ninth, and that about four hundred people went without a road for four months because a man of about fifty-eight kept a store for nineteen years and a clerk wrote his trade in a column — and that he would be doing it with a better hand and would be in nine counties instead of four.** She agrees in the first nine minutes: **he is the person least able to hold it, and I am the person everybody would look at second, and if the ninth one is in that room it is not a ninth one, it is a first one with a witness.** Four seconds of silence, the only silence in the chapter. **And then she refuses the second of the eight, which is *Two books*, which is the only one anybody could keep twice, and the reason is that she keeps a list of nine places, could produce it inside a quarter of an hour, and a person who can do that is holding eight copies in one hand** — and she asks for that to be said out loud, because she has had things said softly in rooms and quoted at her for a year and a half. Then the change of decision, out of a list of nine rooms she keeps and has never shown: **who is hardest to send a form to — a man on no roll, in no yard's book, on nothing that goes out on a cart, in no place anybody has a reason to be, and therefore the only person in four counties a form cannot reach.** She names the man of about nineteen on the bar, who is not in any of her nine rooms, and four lines are written in a book of his own, **and one of the four is hers and he wrote it down because he wanted the words he had written to be hers, and she looked at the four lines for about nine seconds and said one of them was wrong and would not say which.** The cart back is on the fifth morning. Ending: decision.

**Ch 405 · the fourteenth of Greenmonth, a Friday, not a market day · the head of a made road four miles west of Marl · a sheet with nine figures on it and a man who cannot read.** The bar: up Monday, Wednesday, Friday, Sunday, down three, up in about four seconds on a lever and a fulcrum, sockets cut by a man of about thirty with a mallet in about two hours. **He is paid nine pence a day for the three days in seven it is up, is not paid the four hours, and is not paid for the four months of the year when nobody comes, and a person in that position is one this kingdom has no rate for at all.** A hundred and seventy days gives about ninety-seven up-days and about seventy-three paid days, and **there are about seventy-three twists of paper in the left pocket of a coat that is not his and he has counted the twists and not the days.** **The morning: about nine carts a week, about a second and a half each, and no negotiation, because a man of about nineteen has a lever and nine feet of oak.** The man of about thirty-three with a barrow comes up, stops nine feet short of where he has come in about four months, and puts a sheet on the end of the bar. **He was told to leave it and not to say what it is, and he has been on that road about nine years and had never left anything for anybody and did not know until that morning that he could.** Then he says the thing that made him stop coming up that road in about nine words, and it is a ditch four hundred yards west that takes a loaded barrow down about two inches. **The sheet has nine figures, no head, no seal, no signature, and a date at the bottom in a larger hand.** He looks at it and says *that is a number* and *I cannot read them*, and the man of about thirty-three works it out in about four seconds. **I can count. I can count a barrow of bricks. I have been counting cartloads off that road for a hundred and seventy days and there is a man who used to take four and he stopped in Thawmonth and it was nothing to do with me.** Put his finger on the date, which is the fourteenth of a month, two digits, and says he knows which day it is because there is one of these most days. **About eight people in that town have worked out his four months ended on the twenty-fourth of a month in Thawmonth, four are right and four are wrong by a day, all four of the wrong ones are wrong the same way, and the reason is that nine men set the date in a yard and never said it out loud.** The barrow man had the day in front of him and could read it and did not read it out, because he was told not to say what the sheet was. The sheet goes flat on a bench under a stone, because the draught in that shed comes off the floor. **And a bundle has been in that shed for about a hundred and twenty days, unopened, and it is about the size of a small dog, and he has a sister of twenty-two four hundred miles off he has not spoken to in about a year.** Ending: cost.

**Ch 406 · the seventeenth of Greenmonth, a Monday, not a market day · a fold of ground with a byre, thirty miles south, about two hundred people · *Never a person twice*, and what it has grown into.** What the third of the eight means: **the person who keeps a thing and the person who uses it are not to be the same person in the same season, because a person who writes a figure and then spends against it will write a figure that spends** — it is about four hundred years old, it is about a forge, and it is why no man in about nine counties is both clerk of a store and the man who draws from it. **In this fold it has meant, for about a hundred years, that one man walks the boundary and nobody walks it with him.** His father died in a winter; he was eleven; he was the eldest of three; the boundary is two miles and a boy of eleven can walk it, and he has done it about thirty times. **Nobody asked me — you have been told it, I did not hear it until I was standing in a yard at eleven with about four other people in it, and a woman of about sixty-nine said you are your father, and that was the whole of the asking, and it was him asking me, and she did not wait for an answer.** **He is good at it and that is said early and plainly: nine stones of which four are old and two are about a hundred and forty years old and three are his grandfather's, a stream under water about four months in the year, no list of where, four errors in thirty years, and every one of the four caught before the season turned** — three by a man of about fifty who put a stone back and never said so, one by a girl of about eleven who told her mother. They walk about nine hundred yards of the two miles, which is half of it, in about a quarter of an hour. **A boundary walked is a thing you do with a man of about nine on your left and a stream on your right, and every nine or ten yards one of you looks back, and you look past him at about four feet off the ground, and the boy could do it before he could count to nine.** At about four hundred yards is a place four generations have all walked a foot to the left, and he points at it and will not go and look at it: **that is the one nobody argues about, and the two that are in the water are the ones that get argued about.** A boy of about nine, correcting an adult about his father's age and being right, then asking from thirty feet off with both hands in a hole: *are you going to tell him to do it.* **He has had nine years to decide and has got to the end of them about twice, and the boy is nine, and he has decided twice that nine is the age and has decided it twice this year — that the boy does not have to do it, that nobody in that fold has ever not done it, that he would be the first and his grandfather's people would have called him a soft head, and he has made his peace with that because there is going to be one soft head or there is going to be nobody in four generations.** Then the refusal, in about twenty seconds, on the practice itself: **if I take a sheet off a man with a stick and put it on a bench in this fold then I am a person twice, and I will be a person twice in a song and in a room, and there is no way to be the man who walks it and the man who has it, and that is the whole of what my father taught me in about nine words before he died.** **And on the first of Longlight he walks it alone and the boy does not, and about two hundred people in that fold know the boy did not walk it and not one of them knows why, and there is no form in this kingdom on which a man can write down why he walked a boundary on his own when his own son was in the fold with him.** Ending: cost.

**Ch 407 · the twenty-second day of Greenmonth, a Saturday, a market morning · a market town of about two thousand on the salt · the ninth line said in a market, by accident.** The market runs on two days in seven; a rope at one end marks the two; the bar at the other end goes up on five because the market trades on two; about two hundred yards between them, about nine hundred people, about four hundred of them about a cart. The argument is about dressed stone, a chalked corner, and the words *that is my corner* said four times. **The man of about thirty-three with a barrow puts his barrow down in nine feet of ground with nine carts going round him and sings the nine lines in about eleven seconds with a barrow wheel against his boot, and he sings it well, because a man who carries glass and lime has a voice that four hundred people in a market street have heard at seven o'clock for four years.** Nobody sings the ninth with him because nobody there knows there is a ninth line. **About forty people heard it, nine within about nine feet, thirty-one within about sixty feet of a nine-hundredweight load, and about nine hundred in a street of two hundred yards, and there is no wind in that street at the fourth hour.** He got the ninth line right, from a back room, from a man of about twenty writing on a sheet. He does not know what it is worth and never will. **A road's name that is said where a load is being settled is not a road's name any more**, and he says the fourteen words back flatly, as though reading a rate off a board, and adds that he has said nine lines about nine times in four years and did not think about it. **Nobody put it back, and it took about nine minutes to establish and no one wanted to establish it.** By the seventh hour a man of about forty-three said there was no such road and a man of about twenty-six said there was, and that his grandfather had a field by it, and it went nine miles, and it was nothing now, and it had been a harbour once — **and a name that had been a road became a road that a grandfather had a field by, in about four minutes, in the ordinary way, with nobody's voice raised.** Then a woman of about nineteen says it to a man of about thirty who writes it on the back of his own hand, and a man of about thirty-eight with a crowbar asks whose hand it is, and she says she does not know and is sorry. **He put a bar of soap over it in the evening and did not put anything over it in the morning, and the whole of the difference between a name in a song and a name in a county is about nine hours and a bar of soap.** And the man of about thirty-two with a limp worked out at the cross end of two hundred yards, badly, on a bad leg, that the plan had been right about everything except the one thing it was about. Ending: cost.

**Ch 408 · the twenty-eighth day of Greenmonth, a Friday, not a market day · a roadhouse nine miles on from the kilns · four feet of board with no head on it, and a boy who cannot read.** She is called nothing, has been there about thirty-one years, bought it off a man of about sixty, never shut the door in the daytime, about nine people a week. **The eleven o'clock is not an hour anybody chose: it is the first person off the made road, and about four people come in at the eleventh hour and are still there at the second hour of the afternoon, and the figure is four a week in Mudmonth and eleven or twelve in Harvestmonth, and that difference is the whole of what a roadhouse on a made road is, and there is no figure for it except a woman of about fifty and a plate.** The practice: **feed the stranger first — the argument for about a hundred years is whether the stranger is fed before he is asked what he wants or before he is asked who he is, about four places do it the first way and about five the second, and this one does both, in that order, and does not do it in a hurry.** The column: **four feet of a board, no head, thirty years, three hands, about three hundred and seventy figures, no rule about how often one goes on, and she cannot say what any of them means except that every one of them is a plate — and she will not read it out, because if she reads it out you have the figure and she has said it, and she has said a figure out loud twice in thirty-one years and a man of about forty came to the door with a sheet and wanted it in writing for a bench and she did not give it to him and does not know where it went.** Four shillings a week in bread and cheese, about two hundred and eight shillings a year, done in a man's head in about four seconds, and she says he is the second person in thirty-one years to do it and the first one did it in writing. **A man of about forty-three says he has never once been called a stranger in his life and had not noticed until about eleven minutes ago, and a man of about twenty-four says a line ruled for nothing is a line somebody meant to fill in and has four hundred of them in a book and does not say he cannot read it.** Then the best thing in the ten chapters, in about four minutes, with no speech: **she brings the board in off the kitchen wall and the boy of seventeen opens his book, and four feet of board and about four hundred entries lie side by side with no head on either, kept by two people who cannot read what the other has written, about nine miles apart on a made road, neither of them knowing ten days ago that the other existed. They find about nine marks in common, and the ninth is a mark neither of them can read, because the person who can read it is a mason of about sixty-eight about forty miles off.** The barrow man comes in at the fourth hour, is fed, and is told she heard him in a market on Saturday and it was a good song, and every person in the room stops for a second and a half. **The boy of about eleven with a pail is four hundred yards west on a shoulder at every hour of the daylight, is the reason the barrow man stopped coming up that road, is not the roadhouse's, and does not know.** She reads seven of the eight out loud in the road and does not read the eighth. Ending: discovery.

**Ch 409 · the first day of Longlight, a Monday, not a market day · a field four miles off a made road, a stone in a hedge, a figure twenty-one years old · a clerk's two kinds of minute and no third.** Longlight is when a boundary oath-stone is renewed: the neighbours walk it and a figure is cut into its face with the year and the stone goes back in the hedge. This one is about three feet and undressed and eleven years overdue. **It is not the stone the records speak of, and nobody in that field has ever heard of the other one, and there is about nine inches between them.** About nine people walk about two hundred yards of line in about forty minutes; **the first of them is a man of about sixty-seven who farms the near field and was forty-six when the figure was cut, and he is the reason it is eleven years overdue and nobody has ever asked him about that.** **A clerk of about sixty keeps two kinds of minute for about nineteen years: a walk, which is who came, in what order, which side of a hedge they stood on; and a figure, which is what was cut and when. He is not going to keep a third, and he says so in about eleven words: if I wrote down the names to go with them then in about nine years there would be a man in a town two hundred miles off who could tell me that a field of about nine acres had nine people in it on a Monday in Longlight, and he could not tell me one thing about the hedge.** Tovan Kest is the man of twenty-five; the yard sent a barrow, two men and a length of board, and he agreed in about nine seconds because a barrow going four miles for a day is not a job anybody has to think about, which is the whole of his opinion and he was right. **He did not come to see the man of thirty-two with a limp and was not asked to and was not asked why he was not, and they had about four sentences together in about four hours and all four were about load.** *Put a board under the near wheel.* *It is soft for nine feet and then it is not.* *It is soft for nine feet going and it is hard coming.* **And that is all that happened between the two of them, neither of them has said one word about any year, and about four hundred people in that town would not believe it either way.** The mason of about forty-three and an apprentice of about fifteen: **a mason does not offer, because a man who offers to do that has told you he does not know which of the two of you does it, and the clerk cut the year with the mason's tool and the assistant held the head and did not swing it, and the mason said afterwards it was good work and the clerk said it was the same work he has always done.** Nine inches of play in a hole, four inches to the lane, and the number is in the clerk's head and not in either minute: **if I lose it then a field of about nine acres and a hedge of about forty yards will be argued about in about eleven years, and the arguing will not be about the hedge — it will be about which of nine men said what, and I have watched what it turns into, and it turns into the thing itself, and the hedge goes.** Cost: **a mason at three shillings and sixpence, 42 pence, two men at six pence, 12 pence, 54 pence, and a clerk paid by a county and not by a field.** Ending: decision.

**Ch 410 · the seventh day of Longlight, a Sunday, not a market day · a town of about four thousand in a fen county · eight words into eight places, done by somebody else.** **Eight places: four he has been in and four he has never been and is not going to be told about.** The other four were named on the fourth of Greenmonth in about nine seconds, off a list of nine rooms the keeper of records keeps and has never shown and could produce inside a quarter of an hour, **and that is the whole of what she gave, and it is the largest thing anybody has given anybody in about nine days.** The paper: **a quire at two shillings and sixpence, thirty pence, two sheets between eight copies, a quire about twenty-four sheets** — and the four feet of board in a kitchen that has been kept for thirty-one years cost nothing and is worth more. **The cart was loaded on the Saturday: about a hundred and four sheets, banded in dozens, a band is a hundred and eight and about a hundred and four is a band with four taken out of it, tied with a cord, and eight of the hundred and four are the same eight words, and they are in the middle of the load, and the man of about thirty-three with a barrow loaded the cart and said he had seen those eight lines before in a market in a road, and would not say where, and tied the band and went on loading.** **The room does not know which eight they were and the man who loaded the cart does not know what he said, and there was no plan in it, and nobody on that cart is a keeper of anything.** **The man of about twenty was not in that building and does not know and will not find out for about eleven years, and he is the only person alive who has seen the whole of it twice.** Four of the eight have already sent word and none was angry and **three refused the paper and did the thing anyway**: the woman of about forty-four will not have a sheet in a kiln yard because a sheet can be picked up and she is right and nobody has the answer to it; the man of about forty-one said the thing already said at length and a boundary will be walked by one man and not by the boy; the woman of about fifty read seven of them to an empty room and did not read the eighth and the paper went back on the same cart in the same band. A fourth, a man of about thirty-one with a canvas bag, about nine days' ride away, says there is a better place to put the words and will not say where. **A fifth word came back from a road, and it is the only one of the five that asked a question** — *whether the first of the eight is still being done anywhere* — and the keeper of records read it standing up in about four seconds and then sat down and did not answer it for about a minute and a half, which is the only time in about nine days that anybody in that building was not doing what they were doing. And **the man of about thirty-three with a barrow is going to have to ride that cart in about a fortnight to find out whether a sheet with eight words on it got to a man in a byre, and he has not been asked to and has offered, and there is no form in this kingdom on which a man volunteers for a delivery.** The ninth copy is under a stone on a bench; the first is in nobody's hand on a beat of about seventy times a round; the second is nowhere; the fifth is in a drawer; the seventh is nine days of a cart ride from a man who does not know he is going to fetch it. **And a man of about thirty-two with a limp is holding none of the eight and none of the ninth, is on no list that any of the nine places is on, and has no form anywhere in nine counties with his trade against any of the eight, and it is the first thing he has wanted in about four years that he was not also the person to pay for.** Ending: decision.

### The ten endings, as a list

1. **Ch 401 · discovery** — a ninth line ruled four times and empty, and a woman of about forty-four who says the price of not saying the ninth is nineteen years and has just had it undone by a man standing still.
2. **Ch 402 · discovery** — eight words on the back of a form with no head, on a bench-board, in a room that is let by the day and whose door is not locked.
