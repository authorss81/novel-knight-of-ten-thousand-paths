## Batch 0001 — Chapters 1–10, "The Nineteenth of Mudmonth"

**Ch 1 · the sixth of Mudmonth, late afternoon · Aren.** Flood stage at Sedge Reach. Aren counts eight load points on a four-bay deck, chalks the piers, and argues four parties with money at stake into one more day: light loads through Thursday, no carts, half-fare boat passes for the millers. Journeyman Bind on a batten under the third bay. The cracked Bellweather bell is still on the Old Toll cradle and the bridge guild wants it off the roll at six marks. He tells everyone he bought it as scrap bronze for the tools a foundry will not recast for him, which is true and not the reason. Friendly people repeat the fever account to his face; he does not correct them. He gives the barge master the ninth and the truth instead of a date, then signs a hold on the bell in his own name, good to the guild's sitting on the twenty-fourth, with the tool-chest as surety. Six marks he cannot raise, against a chest worth three and a half. **Change:** a private decision four other people have to live with. **Ends:** he sleeps with his back to the wall.

**Ch 2 · the eleventh, early morning · Aren.** The bell is grown into its cradle; the split lip knocks the yoke every time it moves. Wenna Koll wants the crossing kept, because eleven of her people are stranded by the road everybody is arguing about. Tovan offers the honest six marks and wants the metal gone before noon. The guild's removal notice is dated this morning, eight days before the anniversary, and the clerk is already on the road. **Aren refuses the money and signs the guild's bond for six marks, with Tovan as the witness he did not choose.** He also **names the tool-chest himself, before the clerk has finished asking** — he had the decision in his coat when he walked onto the bridge at eight in the morning and worked out half a second afterwards that he had made it already. That is the choice the chapter is for: not the tool, which the clerk would have suggested, but a man volunteering to be the answerable one before anybody offers him the form. Tovan puts the true thing on the river: *you were the one they asked to hold it.* He puts the bell back and works his own sill, which is the only paid work in his life. After the bell is back on the cradle and both men have their hands full and are standing clear, it gives one dull note by itself, out of the split, and the whole bridge hears it and every one of them explains it. Wenna: a woman from the salt loft has been asking the ferrymen which repairer signed the sill. **Change:** he has chosen the paper that makes him answerable over the money that would make him free, and told nobody why. **Ends:** he cuts lime out of the sill and finds himself listening for it.

**Ch 3 · the twelfth, afternoon · Mara.** The Bellweather Claims Archive, two flood-safe rooms over a salt merchant, in a room with its rule written on the wall in fair copy: an anchor is recorded on a sworn tradesman's word and not on a clerk's. She needs Aren, and the registrar at the Crown registry has already quoted her four shillings for a roll that is with the marshal's emergency office. She shows him what she cannot argue with: **Ferris Oat's thirty-one days of brine weights with the arithmetic intact**, and Bess Alder's parish list that splits four thousand back into two hundred dead at Alder Cross. **Exchange:** custody entry, claim number, brine-weight extract, and her signature on a county filing, in return for an **undated** promise that he will not ring the bell. He signs the whole of it having already chosen the nineteenth, and does not tell her the bell rings by itself. She notes the shape of the silence in her day-book. Once, coldly, in the margin: *Vey, Hanna, 9,* of Hushwater — no relation, matter closed, and it must never be opened again. **Change:** hostile cooperation is now a contract, and she is not fooled. **Ends:** eight days, one undated promise, and a renewal in the province on the second of Greenmonth.

**Ch 4 · the night of the eighteenth into the nineteenth, ninth hour · Aren.** The storm. A carter's man drowned on the seventeenth and the ferrymen go out to ring for the drowned on the cracked bell, because it is the only bell on the reach anyone will leave alone. He breaks the promise. Three days of waiting, three things in his body rather than his head — a place, an anchor, a witness he does not have — and the road-crews' saying he has never credited: three days out and three days back. The bell is lifted by its headstock, never its body, and the crack must not go face up. Oathlight dull red inside the split. **Gatehouse:** *A road needs a living hand to open it.* The seam comes into the rain as weather, not light. He steps through off his own repacked sill, thinking for four seconds that he is going to fix it. **Change:** the roadmark, and the verse of his mother's river song that will not land on its note again. **Ends:** a whole, sound bell going across a hundred roofs in a city that is working.

**Ch 5 · path time, cold morning · Aren.** North-bank steps of a great arched bridge, in the wrong place, with no papers and a bar. The toll-man wants a farthing for coming up steps he did not cross. Children ask what he is here for. The bridge carries a washing line over the middle span every morning, and a four-bay timber gate bridge eight hundred feet off gives him the geometry of the arch, the cutwaters, the repointing, and a hole under the second pier. Then he says the name, and the street goes quiet, and the question behind every face is *is the fever back.* **The fever was real here**: two hundred and eleven dead of the low quarter, a local ledger, a woman with her shutters shut. He says he is not here to fetch anyone and nobody is owed to him — and then catches himself already arranging his face to hear their grief. **The elapse turns.** He learns the fish-scale argument from a woman hanging washing: the table tells you what the river has done, the fingers tell you what your boat will find. He writes the roadmark in his notebook. **Change:** he came for survivors and wants their story, and the shame arrives before the understanding. **Ends:** the bench sends a ferrywright.

**Ch 6 · path time, midday · Aren.** Third slip, a hull on the blocks, a garboard split nine feet aft of the knuckle. **Sera Quen** says out loud that she will not be grateful, because a debt owed to a grateful stranger is a leash, and that a steward maintains and a keeper is a word for a man somebody else has appointed to own it. She refuses his method twice: his anchor is the hull's own frame, the doorway beside the wound. **He pushes anyway, because he is better than the room, and the working fails the way workings fail** — the force goes into the anchor, a seam runs three feet, and a hull-hand of eighteen opens his hand on the iron. Quen teaches it properly: a narrow bound, a four-inch block in compression, the old bollard stone as the anchor, **Aren as the counterweight, not the anchor**, and a witness who would have refused, said out loud. It is then established that the first thing happened on this slip cannot be denied and is in the yard book under her hand. She warns him that a motion does not travel. **Change:** relief at not being the best man in the room, immediately followed by the knowledge that relief is not welcome. **Ends:** a note from the bench — *The bell remembers the night* — and a warning that the bench does not want money and wants its answer withheld until it has its terms.

**Ch 7 · path time, evening, and the six days that follow · Aren.** The guild hall over the arch, the bench on the crown stone, an oath-stone in a lead-lined trough under cold water, renewed every Longlight, cut on the nineteenth of Mudmonth, YR 301: *no order, writ, or office shall close it while hands remain on it.* **The third bell** is the crossing-closed bell, rung from the crown once and long, and it cracked on the third stroke of the refusal — so the hinge is the night the guild refused the writ, and the bell is still in the founder's yard on two trundles, unrecast. A writ came to the north gate on the fourteenth of YR 301, was read in this room by a man who was told to read nothing else, and was refused on the deck at the second hour of the nineteenth; the tender found two hundred people on the deck who nobody had ordered, and its captain sat on the toll stone in the rain for a day and a half with a written duty and no instruction to use it, and a second boat brought a written recall on the twenty-second with a name he refuses to read aloud. The guild kept the **counterfoil**; the signature is on the other half, in a register, in **toll-house hands**, and the toll-house steward is already trying to buy a foreign stranger with a trade road. A second man puts the **Hushwater crossing book** on the table before the terms: eleven crossings, nine names, a cooperative that counts ferry hands and hearths and not souls, a best estimate of near sixty, and the whole argument that *dependent places* is where a person is made to disappear. The bench's terms: their refusal, in his hand, to whoever sends the next order, and a promise to return. **And the volume's hinge, stated by Aren in a plain sentence so a reader can check it:** it is **one bell**. Nine stone, split lip to shoulder, and the founder's mark on the shoulder is the Bellweather founder's mark, which he read in a Merefen scrap yard on a wet morning before he ever came up the north steps. It is under a tarred cloth on two trundles in that yard **and** it stood on an iron cradle on a bridge abutment in his own country, and he rang it there in the storm, and both facts are true, and he has tried for six days to make them agree and cannot. The Crown's own clearance entry in Merefen reads *unclassified salvage*, with no city and no river on it, entered in **YR 302, a year after the writ**. Nobody in the batch explains it and no one may. The newest fact: an eight-inch crack in the **outer ring of the second pier**, not growing this month, whose honest repair is a new cutwater — nine months of the bench's capacity against a debt pledged to the ninth of a grandson. He volunteers for the thaw work, and on the fourth day walks four miles of perfect raised causeway north into reed, and turns round at the old tide mark. **Change:** investigator to participant, given work he would have taken anyway. **Ends:** the only iron in that city with his work in it is a pin, and he has no way home.

**Ch 8 · the twenty-seventh, midday, market morning · Aren.** He arrives on his own sill into a fever, a clean rectangle in the moss, and Rell's watch. **Nine days, not three.** The market is on because the fourth-day market of the twenty-fourth was lost with the road, and the Tuesday barge is lying off with no berth because the wharf road is four hundred yards of bridge road. The sealed order is dated the **sixteenth** and delivered on the evening of the eighteenth, before the storm and before anything had happened, and it names *the road-keeper of Merefen, and the holder of the Bellweather hinge.* The bell went off the abutment at first light on the nineteenth and the cradle has been empty for eight days. Rell believes the bell brought the fever, says so, and will answer for the seizure. In front of forty witnesses Aren says the whole thing twice: the city is standing, the bench refused a writ, the bell cracked ringing the refusal, and he was twenty-one, sent with a partial order, and he never reached the inner city, and for three years he gave the account that the fever took Bellweather while his own notes sat in a coat. He cannot sing the verse. He puts his palm on the pale cross in the sill. **Gatehouse:** *What you carry back will not remain only yours.* **Change:** a private certainty traded for a public record he does not control. **Ends:** he cannot get up the steps.

**Ch 9 · the twenty-seventh to the thirtieth · Tovan.** A fortnight's scour repair done in three and a half days by the wrong brother; six hours of it his own error, learned about at the bottom of a crib at the fourth hour. A chalked tally on the back of his hand and eleven days' pay he cannot give. Three clients want a date; he gives them the ninth and refuses to be asked a fourth time in front of his crew. The **unnamed factor** for the western-channel stone merchant has eleven tons of paving setts stranded on a bank, a quarter of his year's margin already gone, and a want of his own that has nothing to do with a bell: to be a man who has not yet eaten it. He decides to cut new stock at the western end so the stone becomes somebody else's problem for a season, and says he will come back on the first and ask again, in the yard, not in front of the crew — "a concession and not a favour." **He stays addressed by office; the six-name cap is not spent on him.** The guild clerk will take three and a half against six and the chest is worth three and a half. He goes up to the room. **The refusal, in his own voice and with no appeal in it:** he will mend the bridge, he will not hold the road, he will not keep this, and he will not be the man holding the span when it goes. And the estrangement gets a shape, said once, for the first time: he was ten when Aren left, he wrote at eleven about a roof and an apple tree, and there was no word back for two years, and he did the roof himself over three summers and has never said so until now. **Aren's fever-talk corrects itself twice in one speech:** the number two hundred and eleven came from **the ferryman on the north bank**, and the woman on the gallery is the one who asked *How would you know?* — they are not the same person and neither is the woman on Ferry Lane. Aren cannot answer the refusal, feverish, and the day ends. He is glad, at four in the morning, that the answer to *will you say it if I say it* came to him in a room where nobody could hear. **Change:** the loyalty survives and the availability does not. **Ends:** he pays what he has, tells the crew he will be there as long as there is a bridge, and tells almost no one.

**Ch 10 · the thirtieth, the day the fever breaks · Aren.** The salt-loft rooms, and **Lysa Fenn**, magistrate of Merefen, who has come out from the provincial town at Mara's written request, and who tells him — unasked, and to Mara's pointed silence — that she took a filing from him eleven years ago, that it is a different document from the bell, and that she will not discuss it in this room. **Mara's terms are the scene:** a filed exhibit, a chain of custody, a date, and a second witness — and she will not file a crossing claim on his word. The chain has a hole in it the size of a bell and the hole is the document: it is Crown salvage, and an exhibit in Crown custody is Crown property, so the Archive's rule of one set of records outside Crown custody is invoked for the first time in four years and earns itself. Lysa takes the exhibit **beneath** his account, so that the first thing a reader sees is a seal and a date and not a man explaining himself, sets the seizure hearing for the **fourth of Greenmonth**, and refuses to hear the crossing first. A second paper calls him road-keeper again and summons him to the provincial town on the ninth of Greenmonth; there is no such office in the charter, the county list, or the crown's schedule. He asks Lysa what a keeper is and she asks him. **He hands over the counterfoil with no condition and keeps no copy**, and signs the sworn account: the partial order, the failure to reach the inner city, three years of the fever account, eleven years of field notes in a coat, and both of his omissions, to Mara and to his brother. Lysa Fenn states the honest answer to the seizure now, in advance, as **three days from the day written on the paper to the morning the bell came off the abutment** — *not* eight (the empty cradle when he got back) and *not* nine (his absence); the three days are what make the order indefensible, because it predates the storm. The town's own ledger comes to **eleven marks and four pence**, itemised, and Mara refuses to let it share a page with the exhibit. She straightens an iron clamp with the motion of the narrow Mend, Lysa names it a mend and records that she does not know what she saw, and both of them notice. **Change:** the road he has held alone for eleven years now belongs to somebody else's filing system. **Ends:** the anchor-glass slip — two Steppe discs, four marks, money before cutting, **and the four marks are Tovan's** — and a man who can do nothing about any of it except be in Merefen when it is needed.

## Batch 0002 — Chapters 11–20, "What the Fever Cost Everyone Else"

Block 2. **Zero Gatehouse lines in the batch, by budget** — both of Volume 01's two were spent on the ringing and the return. The System's absence is the pressure: the road in him is open and is not usable, and nothing in Merefen answers when he puts his hand on the stone. POVs Aren ×5 (11, 13, 15, 18, 20), Mara ×3 (12, 14, 19), Tovan ×1 (17), Lysa Fenn ×1 (16, the only time in the volume anybody holds her view). Six names, all pre-established: **Jos Rusk, Bram Ottery, Halven, Pei Tarrow, Sarn Oyelaran, Ilvane Quarren**.

**Ch 11 · the first of Greenmonth, mid-morning · Aren.** Two days out of the fever room and the town has decided it does not need him. He wants a job he can be paid for and gets a shear pin, eight pence and a shilling, from a brother who owes him eleven days and will not be asked. The scour hole on Sedge Reach is the work that is actually waiting and it is unaffordable: eleven tons of stone, a fortnight with the road shut, a crew of four paid by somebody, and the trust has nothing until Longlight — **it is arithmetic, not cruelty.** He finds the guild's **bench mark re-cut by hand in Mudmonth and a quarter of an inch low**, takes a level off it twice, writes the figure, and is told a road warden does not put papers in against the bench's own mark. Free, unwanted, correct, and worth twenty years of levels. **The factor** for eleven tons of stranded setts gets the true price of his own mistake and a date he is not promising. The Old Toll sill's cross is a **working scar, dry inside a streaming bridge** — a scar, his, and the sill will take one more and not a third. He puts his hand flat on it and **nothing answers**: no warmth, no voice, no seam. Then the glasswright: **Master Ilvane Quarren**, sixty-eight, will not sell to a man who cannot say who will be standing on the stone when it goes, and says his real want out loud — the Steppe road to be a road and not a queue, to be able to stop, and a bridge somewhere with his name on the seat and the man who set it alive. **Ends:** four words on a market board with no rule over it — *Bell taken by the crown on the 19th. Nobody asked. Ask Kest, he was there* — and a note from the salt loft: *A man has been in the outer room three days running… he says it is about the crossing.*

**Ch 12 · the first of Greenmonth, afternoon · Mara.** A notice to file before the second, and a man on the bench in the outer room who has been there three days. **Jos Rusk**, 51, Hushwater ferryman, survivor, has come to file and is not here about money. **Anwen Rusk's crossing book**: nine years, every crossing, in a hand other men checked, and Rusk says out loud what it cannot do — *a hand that is regular proves a boat went across, on a day, in a direction, with a cargo; it does not prove why a village was not there in the morning.* **Mara's personal refusal**, made in front of a claimant: she will not head a claim *Bryn Rusk, 11, lost on the last ferry out*, because a child at the top of a page is the easiest possible shape for a piece of paper to take and it is the shape that loses. **She puts the cause in dispute in writing — *the record does not show the cause* — and tells him it will cost him**, because a claim with a wrong cause can be disproved in a year and a claim with the right cause cannot be proved at all. He is not argued with; he is heard, and the hearing is written. The **Culling Order** is named once, in his mouth, with no member attached, and Mara holds the word in a room a magistrate can see and a clerk cannot. The last page: eleven lines, and the eleventh in a **different hand** — *Persons, 11. Not landed, drowned channel.* **Ends:** very quietly, *Has anybody told the ferrymen?* — and she has to say no.

**Ch 13 · the second of Greenmonth, mid-morning · Aren.** The finding is read aloud in the trust room, by a county clerk who refuses to improve it. Clause one: no claim to Bellweather survives. Clause two: the crossing and its dependent places **was not built**, and is not maintained. Clause three renews the county's emergency power for a year, to unnamed officers. Aren does the thing he is good at — he reads the sentence as a structure and finds the load path, and tells a room that *not built* is a statement about masonry and not about a city, and that if the Crown asks the sentence to hold one more weight it will be asked about a bridge and not a fever, in a room where there is a mason. **He gets one thing wrong**: he names the marshal's office in front of eleven people on the morning a finding was read, and a clerk leaves having decided to write a memorandum, which is the cheapest weapon in the Crown's armoury. Lysa Fenn will have to put him nearer a bench. **Mara's correction**, in front of eleven people, is the chapter's spine: her thirtieth-of-Mudmonth exhibit is now a document about a thing that never existed, and one clerk's word on a wrapper can call it *unverified* in eleven seconds; and *its dependent places* fits a city of four thousand and seven villages and does the work of both. **The clerk's parting shot** — the office that holds the file is the one that will be asked what the file is for — names the real enemy without appearing. **Ends:** a printed fee schedule from the Crown registry, signed by a registrar, and his name.

**Ch 14 · the second of Greenmonth, afternoon · Mara.** **Halven**, registrar at the provincial registry, travels with a locked chest between his boots. He is not a villain; he is a man with a post, a family, a legal exposure and a price, and the price is **a charge for a search**, which is how a Crown registry keeps itself from being blamed. He brings the Crown's Bellweather salvage and clearance volume and, unasked, discloses a **gap on a shelf in the fourth press** where the *Anchors, schedules and dispositions, Alder Vale* book — the one book in the registry that names objects — was entered in YR 309 and is not there, off the roll, by no hand of the office. Mara writes his sentence word for word and makes **Sarn Oyelaran** attest it in her own hand, because a copyist's note is a fact about a book and a keeper's note is an argument about a Crown. **The money fight, in front of Halven:** she pays the four shillings to hold the date, cannot pay the search, writes Lysa Fenn for an order to inspect a register without fee — **eleven days** — and is honest about both facts, including that a keeper of a public register has no jurisdiction, cannot refuse a fee without a magistrate, and is therefore unassailable. Halven's want is money; Sarn's want is **a post of her own**, and Mara writes a four-line warrant for a third post in her own hand in the day-book, not in a file. **Ends:** Sarn stops in the light, reads a whole page instead of ruling it, and says the Bellweather clearance of **YR 302** is out of place in a bound order, in a hand the same age as its neighbours.

**Ch 15 · the fourth of Greenmonth, the ninth hour, market morning · Aren.** The hearing, in a salt-loft room with a wedge in the door and thirty-one people on the stair. Three documents on the table in order of age: the guild's notice of the eleventh, the sealed order of the **sixteenth**, the claim exhibit of the thirtieth. **Captain Rell** under oath, correct, frightened, and right that a Crown paper delivered to an officer competent to execute it is not refusable. **Lysa Fenn says the three days out loud in a public room** — the sixteenth to the morning of the nineteenth, off a paper written before the storm — and rules the seizure **lawful**, refuses the bell, and does not restore the guild's trust, because a municipal trust cannot be affirmed over a lawful Crown holding on the same stone. She records the authority as unproducible and warns the room that being told the law was on its side is the most expensive thing that will happen in Merefen this month. **Aren testifies about a bell and nothing else**: nine stone, a cradle grown into the stone, a bar through a headstock loop, a split outward from the sound bow, a founder's mark he read in a Merefen scrap yard. **The landing is Bram Ottery's statement**, written unasked at his own table, entered with the hour: what the watch did and why and who stood where — and it names the man who has been telling the market since the seventeenth that the bell carries the fever, **who is in that stair**, saying it again, and the court cannot un-sell it in a morning. Ottery's statement costs him: Rell relieves him of the sergeant's post and of nine and three months' accrual, in a corridor, and there is no promotion in it. **Ends:** the chalk on the toll-house board says lawful procedure and abeyance, and the rest of it happened in a stair.

**Ch 16 · the fourth of Greenmonth, afternoon · Lysa Fenn.** The only time in the volume anybody holds her view. In the provincial town, out of the assizes' way, she writes the finding in four sheets and signs it, and understands what she has done: **she has made herself a party.** Clause five is the mechanism — the authority is unproducible and the officers unnamed, so any office that acts again is on notice that this court asked and was not answered, and the asking is dated. She leaves a private minute saying the finding is not a victory. Her clerk brings her the thing she has been circling: the third clause renews the county's power annually **to officers nobody anywhere can name**, and a power nobody can name is a power nobody can ask about — and the clerk has brought it from a filing room, where it does not belong. **The emergency power is the hearing; the bell is the peg.** She names the eleven-year-old salvage filing once more and refuses it again, and says out loud, for the first time, the want she has wanted for a year: **to make the law useful to people who cannot afford a title or a patron**, on a salary the reeve's office calls a discourtesy, held by her family, never once appointed to anything. She stamps the inspection order — returnable on the fifteenth, eleven days — and hands Aren the finding to carry to the keeper. **Ends:** a magistrate who has gained a question and a name, and a town told in chalk that the law was on its side.

**Ch 17 · the fifth to the seventh of Greenmonth · Tovan.** The **fifth is a stated cut**: the market cleaned up, the pitch off the cobbles, nobody new, no new paper, the bell still where it is. And the town has started joining two half-sentences: *he's the one that talks, and he's the one that was away.* The work finishes — the thing a bridge carpenter dreads more than a flood. **The factor** puts four marks into the yard against a month of face-stone cutting, in the yard's chest, which is the crew's wages, and Tovan understands in about four seconds what he has done. **The guild clerk**, who is polite and therefore the worst of it, is sent to ask the brother what the road warden means to do, and gets a two-part answer: the bench wants to know whether the bond is a risk to the roll, and that is all it wants. **Pei Tarrow**, 52, freight on the Old Toll channel, mother a Hushwater Tarrow alive at sixty-eight four miles out, comes to the yard unasked to say **he is not a symbol** and to ask for freight. He has no book, no list, no dead. Tovan gives him the work, the wages, the same day off as the other three, and two rules: nobody asks him about Hushwater, and nobody gets an answer about Aren either way — because the yard is the only place in this town where a man can be paid for a day's work without being asked what he thinks about a bell. **Tovan's own want at last, in his own voice:** a paid yard and a bridge season and a brother who is not a road. **Ends:** a boy reports a bell on the far bank on the seventh, in daylight, in a dead calm, with nobody near it — and the tower has a line of sight down the old raised road.

**Ch 18 · the seventh of Greenmonth, the ninth hour · Aren.** The report in a cooper's bill: an hour, a direction, a sight-line, and a boy of thirteen who then went up the tower and looked and saw nothing. He goes up and looks himself. **The silence, written as deliberate:** he names Bellweather-Living, the arch, the bench, the stone under the water, the third bell, the boy of seventeen, the full minute — and gets nothing. Not a warmth, not a fold in the air, not a word. He tries the cradle iron: cold, and the only thing in it is a Crown paper. Then he **finds a working that does work** — a promise the width of a shake into a scaffold upright, dull red, free — and the two facts are the same fact: oathcraft is a promise made to an anchor, the sill is a door and not the thing behind it, and **the road in him is nominally open and is not a journey.** Eleven days and not one word sent or received, and a bench of nine holding an arch on his word. He writes the length of his own waiting into the notebook so he can never afterwards say he did not try, and then does the thing he has not done since the sixth of Mudmonth: sits down on the abutment and counts nine load points on a bridge he is not going to enter. **The choice at the bottom of Weir Street:** the watch, which would make a sound into a reason, or the Archive, which would make it a document worth nothing for five years and impossible to argue with. **Ends:** he writes the address of the salt loft on the wrapper. He has a door, a glass sill, four marks, and a witness to find, and he has none of the four.

**Ch 19 · the eighth of Greenmonth, the salt-loft rooms and the yard · Mara.** The renewal's damage, made concrete: a second column in the day-book headed *entered under the finding of the second, and therefore a year weaker*, and — under the wall rule about a sworn tradesman's hand — **a new rule she has never added before: no combined figure is entered in this office.** Sarn brings the Crown volume again: the YR 302 clearance entry is **out of place, in a hand the same age as its neighbours**, and Mara spends three days deciding she is entitled to the word *altered* and not *out of place*. The decision: **she does not publish it.** Sarn argues, publishes-be-damned, and is right — and then does the thing Mara did not ask for and writes her own attestation in her own hand, with the date, the hour, and no opinion. **A registrar's fee, understood:** the four shillings bought a *date of search*, not the ledger, and the gap on the shelf stands until the order returns on the fifteenth. **The magistrate's clerk**, who has walked four miles up a hill to serve a finding, comes to ask whether the seven villages are *dependent places* — because if he enters them as such they fall with the crossing, and if he enters them as names they are not parties to a finding about a bridge. He asks for a figure, and **Mara says why, out loud, in front of a court officer, that this office never adds**: a total is one number and can be disproved once, and nobody has to look at the reason; a name can only be looked for. He enters them as names, in the county's spelling, with the figures separately and *sum not stated* in the total line **and a space left where the eighth figure would go.** **Jos Rusk** returns as promised, with the market's version of the eel-man's stair and the lesson of it — a record got him further in a week than nine years of being right. The wrapper's **eleventh line** needs a tradesman's attestation, and the only man whose hand a court will take is the man two Crown papers already call a keeper; Mara says what it costs him and says the true thing, which is that a man nobody can read can be destroyed at leisure. **In the yard**, she wants the quarter-inch bench mark entered in her book, Tovan refuses to put a clerk's figure on a factor's paper, and she reads the cutting contract standing up and understands both ways to have four marks: a bond is faith, a debt against a contract is a document. **The bell report is filed as reported** — an hour, a direction, a sight-line, three witnesses, and no conclusion, the only page in the building nobody can improve on. **Ends:** she sends a slip down to a yard with a figure on it, and has never in four years sent a man a note that was not in a file.

**Ch 20 · the eighth of Greenmonth, evening, the yard and the Old Toll sill · Aren.** **The decision chapter.** The clerk comes into the yard, not to the bench, and offers the obvious thing — a second bond on the roll against a chest worth three and a half — and **Aren refuses it out loud, in front of his brother and four men, by handing the clerk his own sentence back**: *a man who signs six and has three and a half is a man who has signed on faith, and faith is not a currency we take.* The clerk, who is a careful man, names the alternative: **a debt, with a rate, a term, and a third party's name on it.** Tovan offers it without being asked, and says so in the room so that the story in the town afterwards will be half wrong. **Aren puts a mark and a quarter of his own on the bench and the advance is two marks and three quarters**, entered as a debt and not a surety, cleared at eight pence a day, carried on if the cutting fails, and never once in a sentence with his brother's name in it. **The clerk has to describe the surety, and the market's question arrives:** how many. Aren gives the villages by name, refuses the Crown's four thousand beside them, and says the keeper's reason out loud in a yard and credits it to her and to the magistrate's finding of the fourth — *a total is one number and can be disproved once; a name can only be looked for; the emergency power is the peg and the bell is the hearing* — and the clerk enters **no reason given** and a blank sum line, and prefers to be argued with. **The shed, at the seventh hour:** the fire is lit, there are two journeymen, and **Quarren's own shelf is the answer** — a pair of Steppe discs that came down in the winter at the count price, a year annealed since before he paid for them, standing in a cold rack since the first of Frostmonth with nobody's name on them. The slip is not a lie; a price list assumes a man comes in today. He asks the question again — who will be standing on the stone — and **Aren answers with a brother who has said he will not promise him anything, and no idea who is at the other end**, and Quarren says that is the answer he can sell glass to. He also says what the pair costs him: his shelf is empty, there is no fresh Steppe glass north of here before Longlight, and that is thirty years of a complaint answered with his own stock. **Nine shillings** is being quoted in Merefen off his own board, and a third of the price. **The sill, at the last light:** the hand on the cross gets nothing, again, deliberately. Aren cuts the seat himself, in a single block of the fourth course, square to a struck line, in fifty minutes. Quarren dresses the discs and says it is the first seat he has seen square in a hundred, sets them edge to edge across the two lines of the cross with the maker's mark under the near disc, and then **hands over a page out of his own account book headed *setting of anchor-glass, seat in cut stone, and how it will fail*** — the first customer in thirty years to ask how it fails instead of how it holds. **Tovan witnesses the setting and refuses to witness the road**: he will put his hand and the hour on the fact that a thing was set, and he will not put his name under a sentence about a road, because he has not believed in one since he was seventeen. **Aren's undertaking**, read aloud on the bridge and signed by three hands — a maker's account, a carpenter's sight of a thing set, a date and an hour — and it says plainly that **he has no person to witness what he is about to do, and has a record instead**, and that the record is not the road. A copy goes to the Archive's press, outside Crown custody. **The bill, named aloud in the street at the eleventh hour:** four marks, two marks and three quarters of it repaid at eight pence a day; a market on the ninth with no road warden in it; **the ninth-of-Greenmonth summons unanswered, which is a gap in a provincial ledger and a fact about him in a year**; and no person on either end of the road. **This is the second of three crossings this season,** and the first one cost nine days against a saying that allows three, three days of fever, a market that did not happen, and a bell under a Crown seal that the Crown was within its rights to take. He goes home with the ninth twenty-four hours off and closing. **Ends on the decision, not a cliffhanger: he is going, and the reader can name why and what it costs.**
