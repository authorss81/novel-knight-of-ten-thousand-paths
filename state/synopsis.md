## Back cover

A fever took the city of Bellweather, and a captain of twenty-one lost it. Aren Kest, stripped of rank and oath, has spent the years since repairing bridges in a fen town. Then he buys a cracked toll bell for two pence, and it opens onto a Bellweather that never died: working people, a mortgaged arch, a council that will not take orders, and nobody who owes the man who came back a thing. To clear his name he must cross into living versions of his world, where nothing resets, nobody is a rescue, and every road costs him something he cannot get back. The marshal who taught him to hold a bridge now offers his old rank back.

## Synopsis

Neyra's magic is oathcraft: a bounded promise made to an anchor, paid for in time, blood, material, and memory. Under Morrow Abbey lies the Gatehouse, a road-engine built by a dissolved assembly to keep one living history from destroying another. Its consent rule has since been edited, so the machine meant to stop one authorized ending now carries one.

Sir Aren Kest is thirty-two. He was the youngest captain in the Lantern Guard, given a bridge at twenty-one, and he lost it. The Crown says a fever took Bellweather and says he failed, false about the cause and true about the failure, and for three years he repeated that while keeping nine leaves of field notes in a coat he no longer opened. He lives over a cooper in Merefen, competent and unappealed to.

The cracked toll bell on his cradle gives him a roadmark and a crossing, and on the far side stands Bellweather-Living: the city survived the nineteenth of Mudmonth, buried two hundred and eleven of its own dead, and has no intention of being restored by the north. Its people are not a rescue. They have work, debts, a council that refuses outside command, and their own account of that night.

The pressure is specific: he is trusted with decisions nobody else consented to, because he can see which wall is load-bearing. Each crossing is a local crisis paid out of his own body: fever, a roadmark, the remembered pitch of a verse his mother sang, a limp, one ear going quiet, days he cannot account for, and a bleed that leaves him unsure whose grief is his. He learns mend from a ferrywright and loses a live contact for good on a forged witness.

The opposition is administrative: a Culling Order closing roads with writs, notices, and sealed anchors; a Council turning one emergency precedent into law; and Marshal Edric Hunn, who taught him to hold a bridge and now trades selected truth and conditional rank for the road.

The mystery is the mechanism: why a destroyed city is alive, who signed the order, why he can cross at all, what his mother's roadmark names, and what the Unwritten Wound is where a living possibility is forced out. Each answer hands over another, and the last movement turns the book onto its own subject: whether a road, an archive, or an authority can be left open by one man willing to suffer for it. A lone protector becomes a witness, then a custodian, then a man who can be asked instead of obeyed, while Mara Vey, the archivist keeping the record of the erased, keeps the argument honest enough to cost them both.
