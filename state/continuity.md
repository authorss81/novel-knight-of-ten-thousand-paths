## Hard canon

- World: Neyra, a late-medieval river-and-road world. Years count from the **Quiet Reckoning**; the story opens in **YR 312, Mudmonth**.
- Protagonist: Sir Aren Kest, thirty-two, a former Lantern Guard captain and current bridge repairer in Merefen. Estranged from Tovan; still idealizes Edric.
- Starting mystery: the Bellweather Sundering, **YR 301, Mudmonth**, erased Bellweather and seven villages. The story opens on the eleventh anniversary, in the same season. The Crown blames Aren’s failed bridge defense.
- The seven villages: Alder Cross, Saltbrim, Wrenlow, Cobbett, Nine Elms, Stonebarrow, Hushwater.
- Deeper answer: Marshal Edric Hunn and the Lantern Council ordered a forced cull to contain the Unwritten Wound; the order sacrificed a living city.
- Gatehouse rule: a living path cannot be reset or forcibly closed while conscious life exists inside it. Closing a route removes Aren’s access, not the world.
- **Local custody rule (added in bootstrap):** roads are opened by named local custodians, not by Aren. Two consenting custodians with paired anchors can open a road without him, and path communities can open roads to each other without the main world. Aren is a translator and temporary common anchor, never a necessary mechanism. The ending must show roads working without him.
- Path capacity: one at Stage 0/First Stitch, then two, three, five, seven, and ten at later stages. The final Unowned Road stage removes exclusive personal access.
- Path costs: roadmarks, memory loss, path bleed, lost time, failed workings, social obligations, and permanent local consequences. No path is a disposable map.
- Crossing budget: about three attempted crossings per season at any stage; **three days of fever after a return, at Stage 0 and at Stage 1 alike** — the first crossing in Volume 01 pays the same three days — growing to a month at Stage 6. A return stitch is consumed by use.
- Final enemy: Edric Hunn, using the established First Witness protocol to activate the One Road. No new final enemy is planned.

## Canon established by Batch 0001 (Chapters 1–10, prose on the page)

32. **The province's renewal is dated for the second of Greenmonth, not the anniversary.** The nineteenth of Mudmonth is a Sunday and the Bellweather Claims Archive is shut on it with the blinds down. A claim entered before the second of Greenmonth is a claim of the old year; anything after it is entered under a finding that nothing survives and is weaker for a year. The nineteenth is human grief and the second is a clerk in the provincial town copying a finding out of the same book he copied out in 301. **This is the volume's administrative clock and Block 2 runs on it.** The exhibit is dated the thirtieth of Mudmonth, three days ahead of it.
33. **The "three days" is how the warning reaches a roadwarden, and the saying is the same rule.** The Gatehouse's warning at the opening is three days; Volume 01's two-line voice budget is spent on the ringing and the return, so what Aren actually receives is the road crews' saying — *three days to open an old road, three days to come back through it, and a man who has not three days to give should not begin.* **The warning, the saying, the nine days and the six-day drift are all unchanged.** Open question 5 ("what did the warning mean by three, and how is it nine?") stays live and is answered in the middle volumes: nine is six path days plus the three the rule allowed for the crossing itself, and the drift is the six.
34. **Aren crossed in the clothes he left in, and the field notes rode in the breast pocket of that same coat.** They came back with him and they are the physical reason Ch 10's account can be specific about eleven years of silence. He has never handed them over and has never lost them. Do not move them to a chest, a box, or a room.
35. **The bar went through with him and came back.** It is the four-foot iron bar from the Old Toll cradle operation; it is his tool and it is the only object he carried into Bellweather-Living. It lies on the Old Toll setts where he set it down in the fourth second of the return.
36. **The Bellweather fever was real inside Bellweather-Living.** Two hundred and eleven dead of the low quarter in Mudmonth, YR 310, kept in a local ledger. **The number is given to Aren by the ferryman on the north bank**, who also names the woman on Ferry Lane who keeps her shutters shut and the man at the tanner's who has not been out in daylight. **The woman on the gallery is a different person** — she is the one who asks *How would you know?* and who says nobody there has ever wanted to see a stranger's hand. Ch 5 has three separate women in it and Chapter 9 once gave the number to the wrong one; keep the ferryman as the source of two hundred and eleven and keep the Ferry Lane woman distinct from the gallery woman. **The number is stated a second time in Batch 0003, in Ch 27, by Hollis Dree out of the city's own ledger in a public hall; that is a second source and not a substitution, the ferryman remains the first, and nobody else in Bellweather-Living may give it.** The Crown's fever account is therefore a **true thing carrying a false one**, and this is the moral complication the case is built on. It is not the Unwritten Wound, nobody in the path uses any word like it, and the path's ledger and the Crown's four thousand are two different documents about two different events.
37. **The third bell is the crossing-closed bell, and it is the hinge — and it is one bell, not two.** Rung from the crown at night, once and long, to close a crossing. It cracked on the third stroke on the night of the nineteenth of Mudmonth, YR 301, when the Bellweather Bridge Guild refused the severance writ on the deck — so the refusal and the damage are the same event, and the cracked bell is the anchor and the hinge at once. The path's bell stands in the founder's yard on two oak trundles under a tarred cloth, unrecast and unringed for eleven years. **The main world's bell is that same bell:** it was lifted out of the river at Merefen and entered on a Crown clearance roll in **YR 302, the year after the writ**, as *unclassified salvage*, with no city and no river written on it; it was sold at that clearance for two pence on the first of Mudmonth, YR 312; and it stood on the Old Toll cradle until the watch took it. **Chapter 7 adjudicates this on the page**, in Aren's own flat sentence to the bench: nine stone, split lip to shoulder, the founder's mark on the shoulder is theirs and he read it in a Merefen scrap yard before he ever came up the north steps, and it is in two places, and he rang it in one of them, and he cannot make the two agree. **This is settled fact and is not an open mystery, a time-diverge, a ghost, or a twin.** The bell is an anchor, and an anchor may be in more than one place at once; the Crown's blank clearance entry is the proof that the north has had it and does not know where from. Do not stage a second bell, a copy, a replica, or a mistaken identity, and do not let any character explain it inside Volume 01. Nobody in Volume 01 uses the word *hinge* about the bell except the Crown paper.
38. **The path's immediate problem is on the page and is not Aren's to solve.** An eight-inch crack in the **outer ring of the second pier**, river side, that opens in the cold and shuts in the warmth and is not growing. The ring stone is sound; the mortar behind it was raked out from inside by water coming up under the arch. The honest repair is a new cutwater: nine months of the bench's whole capacity against a debt pledged to the ninth of a grandson. The bench has ordered the pier marked twice a year by two men and a clerk. This is the seed of Block 5's failed public oath and must not be resolved before Chapter 41.
39. **The Old Toll sill is spent, and anchor-glass is the gate.** The packing was lifted and set down out of line, and there is a pale, dry, warm cross the size of two hands in the stone where the working struck it from the other side. Two Steppe discs, cut and annealed to bridge pins, **four marks the pair, ready in a fortnight, money before cutting.** No return stitch can be prepared, and no second crossing can be attempted, until that glass is paid for, cut, and set. This is the batch's forward resource and it is a money problem before it is a magic problem.

Tovan's own tally in Ch 9 (oak, lime, stone and cartage, nails) is the material line of the same column and the two must continue to agree. ## Canon established by Batch 0004 (Chapters 31–40, prose on the page)

93. **The batch's day map and the weekday chain, and the two errors in the Batch 0004 prompt itself.** Ch 31–32 the **twentieth and twenty-first of Greenmonth** · Ch 33 the **twenty-eighth** · Ch 34 the **second of Longlight** · Ch 35–36 the **ninth and tenth** · Ch 37 the **fifteenth** · Ch 38 the **eighteenth** · Ch 39 the **twenty-first** · Ch 40 the **twenty-second**. Weekdays, anchored on the **audited** fact that **the sixteenth of Greenmonth is a Friday** (item 91, and the Market Square board line *Road warden in the cart on Friday* depends on it): **20 Tue, 21 Wed, 28 Wed, 30 Fri; Longlight 1 Sat, 2 Sun, 9 Sun, 10 Mon, 15 Sat, 18 Tue, 21 Fri, 22 Sat.** The Batch 0004 prompt said "the twenty-eight a Tuesday" and gave the Longlight weekdays as 2 Fri / 9 Fri / 10 Sat / 15 Thu / 18 Sun / 21 Thu / 22 Fri. **Both are arithmetically incompatible with the audited Greenmonth chain and both were rejected; the prose follows the chain above and the prompt is wrong, not the chapters.** The lowest water of the year is **on or about the twenty-sixth of Longlight, a Wednesday**, which is the bench's marking day and the day the third crossing is pledged to.

94. **A crossing with no owner is a crossing with no crew, and a crew is not an owner.** This is Batch 0004's most reusable idea and it is Toban's, in his own voice, on the Old Toll deck. A **trust in abeyance means the crossing has no owner; a crossing with no owner has no crew, because a crew is hired and paid by somebody**; and a crew "can be hired by anybody with a box and can go home at noon" and "never once decided anything about this bridge." The distinction: **the owner is the thing that answers, and answering is the expensive part.** Nobody in Merefen had ever had to make the distinction because for two generations the owner was a bench, and a bench has a book and therefore has an answer. The trust's instrument gives the bench power over a crossing and no power over a person, which is why Chapter 37's absent man is entered as a protection and not as a default.

95. **The tolls of the Old Toll crossing are suspended, and the direction bars correspondence and *entertainment*.** **Dace Millward**, 61, a creditor in this town thirty-one years, **holder of the tolls for two years by purchase from an abbey in Rainmonth** (not nineteen years — the trade is thirty-one, the holding is two). The Crown's letter is dated the **twentieth of Greenmonth, YR 312**, arrives at the **seventh hour**, is addressed to *the holder of the tolls of the Old Toll crossing* and does two things: **suspends the tolls from the first of Longlight**, and directs that the holder "shall not permit, and shall use his office to prevent, any passage, **entertainment**, or correspondence" between any person claiming to hold or have held the crossing and any other person, on the crossing or elsewhere in the county, upon the subject of the crossing. **The town is not required to be told of the second half of it** (item 101). Millward's figures, fixed: **nineteen marks a year** in a good year, **eight marks and ten shillings** the winter half, and a wharfage that needs four hundred yards of road. He obeys, says so, wants the obedience on the chalk, and the toll-clerk writes it: *Crown letter, twentieth of Greenmonth, YR 312, seventh hour. To the holder of the tolls of the Old Toll crossing. Suspension of the tolls from the first of Longlight. A direction as to correspondence. Received by the holder. Held in the drawer. **The tolls are suspended by the Crown and not by this town.*** It then carries three witnesses' additions, including *read aloud in this office, the seventh hour, in the presence of the holder, the toll-clerk of this office, and Aren Kest, road warden of Merefen*, and Aren Kest's hand went under the clerk's and not over it.

96. **The quarter-inch datum, and the guild's man's argument for putting it on Crown's paper.** The Sedge Reach bench mark was re-cut **by hand three times in thirty-five years** — Mudmonth of YR 312 (the sixth), Mudmonth of YR 297, and the twenty-eighth year — every time because the instrument was away, every time in a month when the mark was proud, and **always downward**, so every level in the county taken since the twenty-eighth year is out by not less than a quarter of an inch **in the direction that makes a deck look sounder and a crown look higher than it is**. **The quarter of an inch is in the stone and the nine inches is in the heads:** a man who takes a level off that mark writes a round number, because a bench minute says *re-cut in hand* and not by how much, and the next man takes it off him and rounds it again because he cannot check it either, and nobody alive will find a nail's width on a mark a bench has dressed by hand. **Four generations of men rounding it in their own favour is nine inches, and it is the heads that go on building for a hundred years.** This is the chapter's thesis and it must not be re-derived as a physical figure: **the stone has lost about a quarter of an inch in thirty-five years and the arithmetic of the records degrades faster than the stone does.** The three figures are in Aren's notebook, which is in a coat, and that was the position until Chapter 34: **the guild's man refuses the guild's roll and consents to the county's schedule**, on the argument that "a thing that is ours is a thing we are answerable for and a thing we cannot be corrected in. **So keep the figures you can be got at.**" The figure is now on the county's sheet of the second of Longlight, in a remarks column, with a date, an hour, and a witness, and says on its face that the bench was asked for eleven days and declined. **The guild's man still refuses the guild's roll and must go on refusing it** — the bench is not wrong and has not been alleged to be.

97. **The Crown's four jobs, and the column headed *keeper*.** Longlight is bridge season and there is more work in the county than in four years. Four jobs: a **schedule of the season's inspections** (forty-one structures, a penny a mile and a shilling a day and the county's cart — **taken**), a **courier** carrying a sealed Crown packet four days each way (**refused**), a **copying post at three shillings a week** in the county survey office, initialling Crown returns (**refused**), and a **culvert at Four Elms** for a farmer (**taken**, a shilling a day and the farmer's materials). The reason, in his own hand: **a schedule is a record and a post is an office**; "a schedule asks me what I saw. A post tells me what to do next." **The county's schedule has a ruled column headed *Keeper* on every line** — an ordinary administrative column, ruled by the county's surveyor — and a man who signs in it on forty-one structures becomes, when the book is copied into a provincial office, a keeper of forty-one crossings. He cannot correct the form and he cannot correct the market, and the reason he cannot correct the market is that explaining the other sense of the word would mean explaining a column heading on a county sheet in a market. The one job he takes is signed at the foot of the sheet with the reason in his own hand **and with the statement that one of the two reasons is the money**, "because a man who takes Crown money for a month and cannot say in his own hand why is a man who has already agreed to something and does not know it."

98. **Petitioning, subpoenaeing and petrifying, in that order, and why the third works.** Mara's chapter, in a filing room, in her own words, and it is the batch's most portable piece of machinery:
- A **memorial** is a paper a person writes who cannot sue. It asks for nothing in particular and it dies of its own weight in about five years. **This office answers memorials by being dull at them and never argues with one in writing.**
- A **petition** is the same paper with a man's name on it and a court at the head. It is a door, and a door has to be answered.
- A **subpoena / returnable order** is a paper with a date on it. It names nobody's wrongdoing, it can be made without a summons, and its whole force is the date. **The first one came off a provincial post on the twenty-first of Greenmonth, stamped on the eighteenth, returnable on the ninth of Longlight, with a recital naming an office the county cannot produce.**
- **Petrification** is a **writ of enquiry into custody**, and it is the one that works. **The writ does not want to succeed.** It is returnable on the **ninth of Rainmonth** and is worth nothing on the ninth of Rainmonth, and what it wants is for the ninth of Rainmonth to arrive: **pending the enquiry the office may not move, copy out, or part with any record held by it.** Nobody is hurt, nothing is taken, nothing is shut, and a record office cannot make a copy of anything for thirty days. **The bar takes effect when the writ is stamped and not when it is served or returned**, and the stamp was the second hour of the ninth of Longlight — so **Mara's entire margin was the six days between the twenty-first and the twenty-seventh of Greenmonth and she spent them on Chapter 32's schedule of holdings, and nobody told her they were the margin because the writ did not exist yet.**
- **A printed space is the most frightening object in this county**, because a blank gets ruled through and dated and a ruled line is read as an answer, and the person who answers for it is the one who signed the return. Petch's grandfather's rule.

99. **Rowan Petch, 26, four years in the filing room of the provincial court, and the particular.** Mara's exhibit wrapper of the thirtieth of Mudmonth carried, in a small hand in the margin, *the seal is that of the marshal's emergency office, that office having produced nothing to this court.* The provincial court holds a copy of Mara's file and not the exhibit, and a clerk writes a **particular** for every exhibit the file does not hold. Petch wrote the particular **on the ninth of Greenmonth** and in it **put the words *the marshal's emergency office* into a file of the county's superior court** — which is the office the finding of the fourth of Greenmonth formally asked for and was not answered, on a dated question. He disclosed it to Mara **of his own motion, unasked, on the twenty-first of Greenmonth**, asked for his disclosure to be entered in her day-book with the hour, and **asked for it to say he did it on his own motion and not on a fee**, which is the sentence a clerk needs written over his name and which somebody else always has to write. He has been in Merefen **three times**: the twenty-first of Greenmonth, the ninth of Longlight, and the eleventh. He paid nothing and is owed nothing; the only thing he wants is to have been careful once while somebody was watching. **He appended his own memorandum to the endorsement of the Archive's return, of his own motion, not entered in the file, because "there is nowhere else in this building to put it."**

100. **The bench minute of the fifteenth of Longlight, and the gift the county gave the town for free.** The bench of the Merefen bridge guild **sat on a Saturday, on the reeve's calling, and the Saturday is minuted.** **Aren Kest was entered absent — at his own request, made on the thirteenth, not served, and the bench recorded that it had been informed he stands named in a provincial ledger in respect of the ninth of Greenmonth and had declined to consider it, and no reason is given.** (This is the **fifth** *no reason given* in a guild roll in four months; the clerk said in Greenmonth that he hoped somebody would find them all and read them together, and has begun writing the dates in the margin beside them.) Tovan said the true thing and did not vote because he is a party, in a public room, and the clerk entered that it was the **second** time a man had stood up in that room and said the true thing and declined to vote on it. The resolution, carried by eight hands and the reeve's: **the Old Toll crossing is kept in repair by the bench out of its own materials and its own men's time, and not out of the trust, and not at the charge of any person holding tolls, and not on account of any person named in any paper of the Crown** — and the bench records that it is not the owner and does not claim to be. The Crown's superior court had, on the ninth, sent a returnable order to the bench requiring it to name the officer holding the object; **the county refused to execute it on the eleventh** ("no officer of this county has authority to execute an order of the superior court, and the addressee is a bench of a town's guild and not an officer of anything") and **returned it**, and in returning it had to write the matter into a printed space, which put **the office of keeper of the Bellweather hinge into a Crown's clerk's hand in Merefen, on the back of a refused paper, with a date** — and the town read it out in a guild hall and minuted that it had taken no notice of it. **A Crown's refusal to use its own court against a town's bench is now a document the town can read out loud and be right about. It is the only piece of Crown paper in Merefen that helps the town, and it got there by accident.**

101. **Captain Rell, the commendation, and the instruction.** The Crown's office nailed up on the watch-house door, dated the **eighth of Longlight**, a commendation of the watch of Merefen with a grey seal and the hand-laid-on-a-book device, praising the execution of a lawful instruction in the matter of an object **"held in use among a company of persons"** — a phrase out of the seizure order of the sixteenth of Mudmonth, about a bell on a cradle that four hundred people walked over every fourth day, read in a provincial office as *in the middle of people*. **Rell is not flattered: he calls it a man being picked, nails it up himself with four nails, and says the notice is the only one he has put on that wall in nineteen years.** The instruction, dated the **ninth of Longlight, second hour** — the same hour, in another building, a writ of enquiry was stamped on the Archive, and the coincidence is in the letter and not noticed — received on the **tenth at the second hour** on the county's own horse and carried out on the **tenth at the fifth hour**, sealed, from the marshal's office: the object is held lawfully and the office is content it continue; **the watch shall prevent contact between the object and any person, and prevent any person approaching, examining or speaking with any person concerning it**; and **the watch shall prevent the assembly of any body of this county in respect of the object, and shall report.** It is **not a threat, names no law, requires no court, and the town is not required to be told.** Rell carried it out in one day, moved the bell at the fourth hour **from the storehouse by the fish weir into a room in the watch house itself**, did not renew the storehouse seal and left its door open in the day, and **told Aren on a step in the rain that he had made the chain of custody unbroken on purpose and that a record that is unbroken is a thing somebody can use.** He was relieved of a sergeant and of nine months and three weeks' accrual and has not been thanked; **his defence is that the one gap in the chain is nine minutes long on the twenty-seventh of Mudmonth and a man who was under an arch and told four hundred market people a city was standing.** The instrument **is returnable on the fifteenth of Longlight and Rell fills in the return himself, with his own hand, and has to write the office of keeper of the Bellweather hinge into a form, because the form asks what matter the object is held in respect of.** The bell is now in a room with a lock and a man in it at all times, **which is a Crown's room by the end of a year whatever anybody says.**

102. **Edric Hunn's first letter, and the fifth piece that is not in the chain.** It arrives at Merefen on the **eighteenth of Longlight, fourth hour**, in **county brown paper** with a **county postmark** and a **grey wax seal of a hand laid flat on a book** — a letter does not need a seal, and the seal says the sender wants the first person who opens the envelope to know. It is **eleven hundred words, dated the eighth of Longlight**, and it is **accurate**: it accounts for every day, every room, every hand and every paper since the first of Mudmonth, including the bell report of the seventh, the account filed beneath the finding on the thirtieth, the bonds and the notice and the valuation, the four conditions and the arrest entered against him for saying a motive, the passage in the hall on the eighteenth day of the other world, the field book on the bench's table, and the four-hundred-leaf register with its press-mark. **It contains no offer, no restoration, no rank, no shield for the Archive, and no access to a record.** Its one act of generosity is the four sentences that kill eleven years: *At twenty-one years of age you were the best man in the room. That is not a compliment; it is a load-bearing fact.* The whole letter is written in the third person about him; **exactly one sentence in it has a subject, a verb and a second person in it — *You will have no further contact with it*** — and it requires no court. It closes by asking, courteously, **for the list of those to whom he has spoken of the matter.** **The letter contains no person's name but his own and the sender's** — every other participant is *a man of Hushwater*, *a woman without a name*, *a road warden of Merefen*, *a creditor's man* — and Aren works out in a quarter of an hour that the only book in this county with that in it is the Archive's attendance column, and that Ch 32's petition's second column is a list of four descriptions and no names. **The names are the last thing the office does not have, and the letter is the Crown being entirely honest with him for the first time in eleven years.** His reply is five hundred words over four days, in the flat hand, and it is **not a refusal and not a defence**: a receipt by seal, date and hour; **the date** (the middle of the year, at the lowest water, and nobody in this country will be able to tell him where he is until he is there or is not, and that is deliberate); **a fact** (the object is lawful here, the magistrate's finding is in a county file, he signed six marks and cannot pay, and he is not going to get the bell back); and **a condition** (what the instruction costs: a bench that cannot meet, a toll-holder's winter half, a man with a bar to be watched in a county of nine men who watch roads — and, unasked for, **the dated question of the fourth of Greenmonth**). He gives no names. He signs with his name and no title. **The letter went to the post on the twenty-second at the seventh hour in county brown paper with a tallow seal, and reached the provincial town on the twenty-fourth, and a provincial clerk's memorandum of the twenty-fifth asks who else he has told, and answers itself: not this county; the question requires another office, and that office has a woman in it, and that office has a form.**

103. **The fifth piece, and why it cannot be lodged.** The Crown's letter is the only document in his possession that is the other side's own account of him, and it is signed and sealed. He cannot put it in the Archive's press (a writ forbids the office to part with records, and a Crown's own case against him in a room over a salt merchant is what the writ was written for); he cannot give it to the magistrate; he cannot put it in a guild roll. **It stays in a locked room over a cooper and there will never be a chain of custody on it, and he has written that down in the fourth piece.** **He did not show it to Mara**, and the cost of that is exact: she would have to enter it within a year and a day or refuse to, and a keeper who applies her own rule to a man she has an opinion about is finished, and he knows she will find out inside a week — and she finds out on the day it arrives, off a postman going past her own window at the fourth hour — because a wax seal the size of a crown piece is not a thing you can walk up a street with twice a day. **He was right about the speed and had no way at all to do anything about it, which is the point.** **This is the second time in a month he has decided what a keeper of records may not be told, and she had written him a line on the third of Longlight, dated, saying *do not write to me about this*, which he read as permission and which was the opposite.**

104. **The report, and the two readings, and the trade fact that makes both of them useless.** **Harl Mounce**, 34, a barge mate of fourteen years on the northern road carrying stone; laid four days on a towpath at the toll-house at Gallow Ford on the **second of Longlight** while two men and a boy with a book took a number off a mark cut in the coping of the **second pier**; a steward of thirty years told him **eleven**, and **eight** at the last marking, in a wharf conversation, and neither number is in the book of any person. He is not a liar and he has the sequence, the date and the words wrong, in the direction that makes it worse, and he said *going* four times and the steward never said it once. Mara entered it on the twenty-first at the fourth hour, as reported, with **five limits**, and the fifth is the load-bearing one: **the same description — a mark cut in coping, two men and a book, a rule, a number twice a year, the second pier — is a description of a Crown's office at a bridge in this country, and is also a description of a bench marking a ring on a pier twice a year at the lowest water in a city this county's own finding says was never built, and no person has said the two are the same bridge and no person in this county can say they are not.** She therefore **did not ask him which**, and entered the day and the hour at which she decided not to, against nobody, with no opinion. The market's second version, thirty mouths: *Bridge in the other world is going. Kest has been there. Kest is not saying* — and the joke that has hardened round it is **Ask the keeper. The keeper'll have it written down with the hour on it**, which is now the same word doing three jobs in one town: a keeper of records, a road warden, and an office a Crown's clerk read off a roll.

105. **The trade fact that makes the true version worse than the false one, and it is Aren's.** **A ring stone opens in the cold and shuts in the warmth, and a bench marks a ring twice a year precisely because a ring has two readings and not one.** So eight and eleven are almost certainly not the same season, and a crack in a ring that measures eight in the warmth and eleven in the cold **has almost certainly not moved three inches** — and a bench that has entered two numbers a year for four generations and has never once compared them **cannot tell from its own book whether the ring is standing still or going, and has never needed to, because until a writ came down off a river eleven years ago nobody in this country was marking that ring for anything but a bench's own order.** The number he wants is the one four years ago, in either season, and nobody in this country has it. **And the report cannot be acted on by anybody: not by him (no seat, no glass, an instruction, and a woman who will write *not here* in a book), not by a bench (no jurisdiction over a stone four days off), not by a magistrate (can order a production, cannot order a man to look at a ring), not by the keeper of records (who has written down that she does not know).** This is the batch's engine, and it is a man being competent and arriving at nothing.

106. **The chain, the three places, and the rule it obeys.** On the **twenty-second of Longlight** Aren Kest makes **a chain of four pieces in one order with a date and an hour against each**: a sworn account of the thirtieth of Mudmonth with the finding of the fourth of Greenmonth beneath it; the bench minute of the fifteenth of Longlight with the returned order on it; the report of the twenty-first with its five limits; and **the fourth piece, his own account of the nineteen days in the city four days off, in nineteen lines with a day against each, which is in nobody's hands but his and always has been.** The spine of the chain is not his and not Mara's: **it is the phrase *the office of keeper of the Bellweather hinge*, which is in three of the four pieces, is in none of them by itself, and reached Merefen free on the back of a refused paper in a county clerk's hand.** Three places, and he chooses a **building over a person** and says why in a room: **a person can be arrested for a document and an office can only be closed, and a closed office still has a door.** (1) **The toll-house**, Dace Millward's roll and chest, entered in seven lines in the toll-holder's own hand with an eighth of his own, because a creditor's book is a book of what a man is owed and it is the only book in the county a court in a hundred years will order a thing out of. (2) **The Archive's press, as a lending and not a custody** — the chain is the property of a person, the office may produce, exhibit, read and hand it to a court, and asserts no claim to it, and **it will be listed in the writ's answer on the ninth of Rainmonth in a fair hand**, which is the cost Mara named before she agreed to it. (3) **The yard's week-book, as a fact and not a paper**, because **Tovan Kest's rule, said out loud in a gateway in front of four men on the twenty-eighth of Greenmonth, is that nothing that belongs to a man goes in this yard's book and only a thing set.** The second witness to the chain is **Dace Millward, who is not his brother and not a clerk**; the second place-holder is the woman upstairs; and the yard holds the fact that a paper exists and cannot produce the paper, which is the correct division and is the first time the rule has been tested.

107. **The money, closed, and the cost named in somebody else's accounts.** **Aren Kest has eleven shillings and fourpence in a jar on a shelf** on the twenty-second of Longlight — the county's schedule at a shilling a day and a penny a mile, and a culvert at Four Elms at a shilling a day with the farmer's materials, less three weeks of eating; the two shillings for the room comes out of the cutting contract and not out of the jar. **Four marks is sixteen shillings, which is the anchor-glass, and he is four shillings and eightpence short of two discs, and there is no fresh Steppe glass north of Merefen and the Steppe road comes down in Frostmonth.** The figure has not moved in a fortnight and it is the smallest number in the book and it is not the point. **Millward's cost of lodging the chain, said out loud, is that his roll now has four things in it that are not tolls** — a suspension, a direction about correspondence, a town's charter and a chain of eleven sheets — **and a man in a counting house reads that roll once a year in Harvestmonth and has read it for eleven years and has never once stopped at a line, and this year he will stop at four, and the year after it will be three times, because that is what a book does: a book with three things in it that are not business has become a record, and a creditor does not want to be a record.** Tovan's figures, also closed: the wages on a load of nine tons are a fortnight and four days for four men at the yard rate and a boy at half, **two marks, thirteen shillings and sixpence**, against the factor's **four marks** on that load, and there are four more loads before the contract is done. **The Old Toll's maintenance is three marks and about six the campaign, twice a year, seven marks and no farthing**, and the figure in every conversation and in the bench's own resolution is **four marks and six a year**, because four is what goes in a book, and Tovan has caught himself rounding and has stopped.

108. **The batch's six-name cap: four spent, two unfilled.** **Dace Millward** (Ch 31, 35, 40), **Iven Sarrat** (Ch 31, 33), **Rowan Petch** (Ch 32, 35), **Harl Mounce** (Ch 39). The guild's clerk, the guild's man with the levels, the salt merchant, the toll-clerk, Wenna Koll, Sarn Oyelaran, Jos Rusk, Reeve Aldis Fenn and Captain Rell are all addressed by office or by established name and cost nothing.

## Relationship state at the end of Chapter 40

- **Aren and Mara:** the batch's real movement, and it is a reversal of the movement of Chapter 30. In Chapter 30 he told her what a crossing cost in the body and gave her a document; in this block **she priced being right for the first time in four months and said so out loud**, entered a claim against herself on her own account, made a second keeper, sent a town's charter out of her building to a creditor, told a watch captain a fact he could only get from her, and then **took a paper off a filing room table and put it into a press as a lending and listed it, in advance, in the answer she is going to give to a writ in a court on the ninth of Rainmonth** — which is the cost of being right the fifth time, and she named it before she agreed to it. **He did not show her the Crown's letter and she did not ask, and she has worked out that he has it and has written down the fact of its delivery and not its contents in the day-book with the hour on it, and the reason is a rule on her wall: a copy is a second thing that somebody can lose and a fact is a thing that can be looked for.** He came up her stair with four things and no paper and she told him that was the most careful thing anybody had done in that building in four years and then told him to go away and write the fourth one. They are not closer and they are not further. They are the only two people in this county who are both holding documents about the other.
- **Aren and Tovan:** the refusal is intact and has been given an institutional form. **The yard's rule now exists: nothing that belongs to a man goes in this yard's book, and only a thing set** — invented in a gateway in front of four men on the twenty-eighth of Greenmonth because of a stair rail, tested in Chapter 40, and the test produced the correct division, which is that the yard holds the *fact* that a paper exists and cannot produce the paper. Tovan has entered his brother's unasked handrail in the yard's own book, in his own hand, with a date, an hour and a rule over it, **and has not told his brother, and is not going to.** He has said the true thing in a public room and not voted, for the second time in five months, and has said that the man who says the true thing in this room has to live in this town. He has caught himself rounding a figure for four weeks to be believed sooner and has stopped. He has learned that his brother's freedom is the reason his gate has anything behind it and has stopped defending the gate. **The estrangement is not healed; it is two brothers holding the same two pages of the same book and neither of them has mentioned it.**
- **Aren and Captain Rell:** a working relationship that is neither an alliance nor a war, and it is the batch's most careful piece of craft. Rell is the Crown's man in this county and is frightened, correct and unrepentant, and has been shown to be right on every count by a Crown's own commendation he nailed up himself. He told Aren on a step in the rain that he had made a chain of custody unbroken on purpose, and he asked him for one thing in trade terms and got it. He asked him not to come up the street again, and then told him that every man standing still in this business is standing still because he went up, and that he is the first man Rell has met who can see that about himself, and that he has no idea what to do with it. **They will meet again and it will be about a form, and neither of them is going to enjoy it.**
- **Aren and Edric Hunn:** **the devotion is over and it ended in a letter and not in an argument**, because the letter was accurate and the accuracy came from a list with no names in it and the list came from the Archive's own method, and because the one generous sentence in it — *you were the best man in the room, and that is a load-bearing fact* — is the reason a valley died, said kindly, by the man who used the room. He wrote back in the trade's terms with a date, a fact and a condition and no refusal and no defence and not one name. **He is now known to the office in a shape that fits him exactly, and what the office has stopped asking for is whether he will stop, and what it has started asking for is who else he has told.**
- **Aren and Bellweather-Living:** **thirteen weeks, and not one word, in either direction, and the silence has become an account rather than a mystery.** There is a mark in a diary that will be taken on Wednesday at the lowest water by two men and a clerk, and a woman who keeps that road will write in a book that he was not there, and there is a crack in a ring four days off that is either eight inches or eleven or neither, and a report that may be about a bridge four hundred miles the other way, and the seat has been used twice, and there is no fresh Steppe glass north of Merefen before Frostmonth. **He has a third crossing of the year in him and four shillings and eightpence, and the crossing is still being saved, and the reason it is still being saved is that a chain of four dated pieces is in a creditor's chest and a press and a yard's book, and nobody in Bellweather-Living knows that any of it exists.**
- **Mara and the Archive's own position:** she is a party under a writ, she has a second keeper, she has a lending she must disclose, she has a claimant's name in a provincial file, and she has begun to price being right. **She has also, for the first time in four years, been wrong in public about a thing that is her whole life** — the town has attached a report about a bridge in another world to her office through a joke, and the only correction available to her is a document nobody reads, and she has entered the fact that she does not know and the hour at which she decided not to ask.
- **Reeve Aldis Fenn and Lysa Fenn:** the reeve has called an extraordinary bench on a Saturday and got a resolution out of it, and has minuted that she did not vote because a reeve does not vote on the guild's own matter and has wanted to for nine years, and has told the crown clerk to come in a room and be right. **Lysa Fenn has no page in this block at all**, and her finding of the fourth of Greenmonth is carrying more weight than any document in the county, and the reeve's minute and the magistrate's minute still do not agree and were written three days apart.
- **Mara and Sarn Oyelaran:** Sarn is now a **keeper of records with a key and a warrant in the day-book**, and she refused to write the one sentence the whole case wants, out loud, in a room, and gave the reason in trade terms. She is the person the office is beginning to need rather than use.
- **Mara and Jos Rusk:** he has his name in a provincial file as an attendee, twice, in his own name, and she told him before anybody else and did not apologise. He is not grateful and is not going to be, and he has said that he will not tell the five ferrymen until winter and will write to her first with a date, and that a paper cannot stop what he is about to do, and that this is the argument for it.


## Batch 0005 pre-write resolution — the Fallowmonth chain, entered before the prose and not to be drifted

111. **Longlight runs to the thirtieth, as Mudmonth and Greenmonth do, and Fallowmonth opens on the first.** Carried forward from the audited chain in item 93 (**the twenty-sixth of Longlight is a Wednesday**, which is the lowest water of the year, the bench's marking day, and the day the third crossing is pledged to) and from **Chapter 40, which is a Saturday on the twenty-second of Longlight**. The chain is therefore closed: **Longlight 22 Sat, 23 Sun, 24 Mon, 25 Tue, 26 Wed, 27 Thu, 28 Fri, 29 Sat, 30 Sun. Fallowmonth 1 Mon, 2 Tue, 3 Wed, 4 Thu, 5 Fri, 6 Sat, 7 Sun, 8 Mon, 9 Tue**, and the rest of the month on the same frame (**10 Wed … 16 Tue, 17 Wed, 18 Thu, 19 Fri, 20 Sat, 21 Sun, 22 Mon, 23 Tue, 24 Wed, 25 Thu, 26 Fri, 27 Sat, 28 Sun, 29 Mon, 30 Tue**). **Fallowmonth the second is a Tuesday** — the day he comes up out of the Old Toll sill, and the day the second pier of the great arch goes. **The first Friday of Fallowmonth is the fifth**, which is the yard's factor-money day in the new month. **Do not drift this.** Path-side weekdays are the path's own and the two calendars are not one clock: the far side's own day-names may be used in a Bellweather scene and a report that carries one of them into Merefen is therefore *wrong about the day* while being right about the night, which is the batch's fourth report and its last pull.

112. **The third fever's dates, resolved.** The established pattern is fixed twice: **item 25** and **Chapter 30** — *begins on the night of a day, breaks on the third day, three days, never a fourth.* First: night of the twenty-sixth of Mudmonth, three days, over on the thirtieth. Second: night of the fifteenth of Greenmonth, three days, broke on the eighteenth at the sixth hour. **Third: he comes up out of the sill at first light on the second of Fallowmonth, the fever runs the second, third and fourth, and it breaks on the fifth of Fallowmonth at about the sixth hour.** The Batch 0005 prompt's phrase *the twenty-second to the twenty-fourth* is a **slip in the prompt** for the second to the fourth of Fallowmonth and is rejected, for two reasons: Chapter 40 is a Saturday on the twenty-second of Longlight and he was in Merefen on that day, and Chapter 49 opens on the ninth of Fallowmonth, which requires the fever to be four days over. **Three days is the figure, on every page.**

he has been right about the fever eleven times and wrong about the names every time and has known it for two years; a stranger came off a road in the first week of a rolling and gave the county a second instrument, and he is glad of it and has been ashamed for about four years and both are true.** **Pernel Quist does not sign, and her two reasons are both hers and neither is a matter of principle: if she is one of the three then every restoration in this county is a thing a woman in Kilnholt did — and she has known that since the sixteenth day of a rolling, when a stranger asked her a question in a room with a stove in it and she said no in one word and he wrote down the reason, and the reason was that she is the anchor; and the third hand is four hundred miles off and behind a price, and a roll signed by two hands and a promise is not a roll in three hands, it is a roll in two hands with a third hand written on the end of it in a fair copy, and in about four years a magistrate is going to ask which of the three it is and the answer will be that one of them is a letter, and she will not sign a thing that has a letter in it and has been right about that every single time in nineteen years. And: I am not signing on Thursday. I have not said no. Do not put it in the book that I said no, because I have said no twice in nine years and both times I was wrong, and a book is a worse place to be wrong than a corridor and a better place to be right, and I have not decided which this is.** It is the fourth page of the minute and the longest thing in it and she corrected one word and the word was *worse*. **She has not signed and Chapter 89 is where she signs, and the reason she gives there — a roll that can be wrong is not a weaker roll, it is a roll that has to be read twice, and a roll that has to be read twice cannot be read by one man in a morning — is NOT spent in this batch and must not be spent in Chapters 81 to 88.**

210. **The second notebook entry, four months after the first, in the same vocabulary, in tons, and he does not act on it.** **Chapter 80, the twenty-second week, in the back of the notebook, in the flat hand, on a low stool at the low end of the rack: he does not write down what the rite is for, because he has not worked it out, and a man who writes down why a thing is good in the same book as what the thing is has written a sentence instead of a working — and that sentence is the one he wrote in a filing room in Longlight and Petch refused to hold for him, and it is the shape of the sentence that put a bell in a file four hundred miles off.** The parts, in the trade's own words, and **the anchor, the witness, the bound and the counterweight are the four from the nineteenth day of the first time here, and the witness line is identical and he says so: a witness that is also the anchor is not a witness, it is a second reading of the same hand, same as the last time, I have written that sentence twice now in two counties and it has been right twice.** The counterweight now also carries **six men out of a county of nine hundred and forty-one hearths whose names are six lines with nothing against them and no reason on them, and there is no column in this county for what happened to them and there is not going to be one.** The bound is the same and he says the same thing about it. **The figure is the same: two tons each, off a barrow of setts, with no reason of any kind; ## Relationship state at the end of Chapter 80

- **Aren and Mara:** the batch's movement is that **she returned a figure of eleven to the province on the ninth of Frostmonth and he was twenty-five yards from the door and was not sent for, and neither of them knows that the other would have understood what the office's three lines are.** He is four hundred miles off inside a clay county in his fifth month and cannot be reached; she has the three lines in her own day-book with the hour on them and has entered that she does not see what they are, **and the two of them are the only people in the world who could each have said what the other needed to hear, and they are not in the same room, and that is not an accident of the plot: it is a binding agreement, a bench minute, a letter in a file, and a crossing that opened on a market morning.** **Nothing has been lied to. He gave her the numbers on the fifth of Wolfmonth and this batch adds no word between them at all — there is no meeting, no letter, no message, and the last thing she wrote about him is on a page in a day-book and the last thing he wrote about her is that he is not going to tell anybody in a clay county a word about any of it.** The not asking is unchanged and the wound is unchanged and **the Chapter 92 breach is now planted for the third time in his own hand and is not resolved and may not be.**
- **Aren and Tovan Kest:** **named out loud, in a yard, on a market day, at about half past four, for the first time in four years** — *there is a road in this county that was opened on the ninth of Embermonth at the fourth hour and nothing came, and it did not fail, and I do not know when it will open and I have not asked, and I have a brother in it and I have not sent him a word because I do not know the address* — and he told four men in a gateway that if either of them ever tells anybody in this county that he said it he will deny it in a form. **And the sentence at the gate of the fourth of Hearthmonth, which is the whole of the brother beat in this block: the yard is a fortnight, and when you come back it will be a fortnight or it will not be, and if it is not then I will have had to put a man off, and it will be Tarrow, and I will not be able to pay him and he will not be able to ask me, and I am telling you now so that on the day you come back and I say nothing about it, you will not think it was your brother.** The estrangement is not healed, nobody has said the word brother in a scene, and the arrangement of four months standing — the fee and the hour and not the reason — has not been re-litigated and has not been improved on.
