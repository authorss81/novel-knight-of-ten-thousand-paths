## 1. Volume identity

Volume 01 asked who has the right to decide about a living city that survived an official cull, and answered *nobody, and here is the sheet with two names on it.* Volume 02 asks a harder and much quieter question, and it is the question a records office is built on: **what does a record owe the person it describes?**

The answer is a sentence a man in a clay county says in his own trade's words, and it is not a moral sentence when he says it: *a person who is struck off the roll has to be able to answer for himself, and if he answers he goes back on it, and if he cannot answer then he was never on it and we have been keeping a list of things.* Ash has been severed from the world by a writ nobody in Ash knows was a writ, because there was no bench there to refuse it and no bell to crack ringing the refusal. The county's answer to four generations of that was to make itself into records: fired clay tiles, one to a person, carrying a pressed device and a hearth and a trade and a hand — permanent, unfalsifiable, and, in the county's founding conviction, a thing that cannot be taken from you if it is written down. Then a person began to go back to being clay, and the county's answer to that was to fire them: to press their mark into a fresh tile and fire it hard, so that the mark is the only durable account of them. **A person who has been fired is alive, employed, and is no longer consulted.** That is Ash's law, signed by three magistrates, administered by a decent man who wants the softening to stop before the spring, and it is the Bellweather Sundering with a kiln on the front of it. The volume's job is to make the resemblance arrive about forty pages late and then be impossible to unfeel.

The engine is **records being bought and sold, not a structure failing.** No arch goes down. No glass breaks. Nothing is forged that was not already true. What happens is that a registrar in a provincial town is offered a legal shield and pays for it with a book that is not on its shelf; that a Crown office issues a form with a column headed *Keeper* and a marginal instruction that manufactures a keeper out of a word; that a county's one physician wrote down, four years ago and in a drawer, the true medical fact that the disease travels along the marks that are read most, so that the cure is three disagreeing copies of every person; and that a magistrate signs it because it works.

At the centre of it a man who can cross and has begun to count the people he has, discovers that the count is not the constitution, and finds that the thing which outlasts him is **a furnace, a form, and a bench in a fen town that votes.**

**The County of Ash is a different kind of community from Bellweather-Living**, and the difference is the whole reason it is a volume and not an echo. Bellweather-Living refused. It has a bell that cracked ringing a refusal, a bench of nine, a creditor's office with four conditions on the wrapper, a counterfoil, and a word for a promise it made and broke. **The County of Ash has no bell at all, because nothing was ever refused.** It has a tile works, a roll, a bench that is small and mostly wrong, and no word for *refused* that does not also mean *lost*. Its roadkeepers are called **the hand** and they are tile-cutters, because a road in Ash is edged in fired tile and the kerb is a record. Its historian is a stock count. Its archive cannot be copied out of, because it is fired. And it is the first community in the book to reach the right answer for the wrong reason and to be unwilling to give up the wrong reason: *a thing written down cannot be taken from you.* **It can. What cannot be taken from you is being asked.**

## 2. Central pressure

Four parties want four different things out of a record, and the volume's engine is that all four of them are reasonable.

- **The Office of Provincial Registration** wants a **complete register.** It holds the press registers, the shelf registers, and a provincial file containing a woman's name; it was asked for its authority on the fourth of Greenmonth, YR 312, and has not produced it; and it has discovered that the cheapest lawful way to act on a road it cannot find the owner of is **a count of persons instead of a list of names.** A count is lawful, needs no court, names nobody, and cannot be refused without refusing a figure.
- **Ash's rollkeeper and magistrate** want **one correct roll.** Two hundred names struck in eleven months, a physician's finding four years old that nobody acted on, and a spring coming in when the clay is dug and the rolling is heaviest and the last two winters have been the worst.
- **Aren Kest** wants a second route he can use, and has written in a notebook that the cost of a crossing is greater the better he gets at the work on the far side, and has told one person, and is going to tell nobody else, and is going to be gone for eighty-eight days.
- **Merefen** wants its road, its market days, its bench, and its eleven marks and four pence: a trust in abeyance, a suspended toll, an instruction in force that bars the assembly of any body of the county in respect of the object, a copying post at three shillings a week, a yard with about five weeks left in it, and **a bell on a bridge that is not his and that somebody else rang on the twenty-ninth of Fallowmonth.**

**The volume question:** what does a record owe the person it describes — and who is answerable when the answer is *the record was right*?

## 4. Starting power state, and the shape of Stage 2

**Stage 1 going in.** One route, one carryover, one road-marked palm, a return that must be prepared, and no ability to make the far side answer.

**Stage 2, the Second Door, reached at the climax by qualification and not by award.** The terms are given on the page in Block 1, on a printed instruction in the hand of the **Office of Provincial Registration**, and the reason they can be given at all is that the Office is quoting a figure from a source it cannot name — the same hole that has been in this case since the fourth of Greenmonth.

- **Routes held: two.** Bellweather-Living, and the County of Ash.
- **Carryover: two, and he cannot tell which path gave him which.** One is the hand's habit of reading a handrail in the dark, which is eleven years old and is not a working. The other is **a way of standing** — weight slightly back, hands loose, the stance of a ferrywright on a slip — and he cannot say whether it came from the third slip of Bellweather or from a kiln floor in the County of Ash, and he has been doing it since the ninth of Embermonth, and a woman in Merefen noticed it before he did and has not asked him about it, because she wrote in Fallowmonth that she was not going to ask him again.
- **One message, and it must be a list.** Carried on a scheduled day, in a fair hand, to whoever is keeping the road agreed with it. **A list can only be checked, and that is the reason and not a decoration.**
- **One object, under a pound,** and it must be something the far side can use and the near side can do without.
- **The second route does not answer when it is called.** The first one does, on a schedule, by a bell. The Second Door does not carry two schedules, and the far side decides, and **there is no procedure for a far side that has not decided.** So the nine months of Ash come down on a day like a letter, in a form, on a schedule, and **he does not learn what happened on the day it happens and cannot ask and cannot be told.** That is the volume's real cost and it is a silence, and at Stage 1 in Chapter 30 the same silence cost him eleven days and the book treated it as a mystery. At Stage 2 it costs him the middle of his own case, and it is a choice he made: a route that answers on demand is a route somebody has to stand at a bridge for at all hours, and he is the somebody.

**The drift, stated in Block 1, in an office's hand, and wrong.** The road crews' saying is three days out and three days back. Chapter 47's notebook has already given the Gatehouse's own figure for a held road as **three weeks**, twice, and never in writing. In Volume 02 it is in writing for the first time, printed in a marginal instruction of the Office of Provincial Registration, and the Office cannot say whose figure it is, and the figure does not change. **The error changes.** Crossing A: warning three weeks, actual absence twenty-six days, **error five days.** Crossing B: warning three weeks, actual absence **eighty-eight days**, **error sixty-seven days.** The warning has not failed and the reader is given the arithmetic in a notebook and a day-book and is able to check it. The lesson, which is the volume's argument in a number: **the error is not in the warning. The error is in him, and it scales with the work, exactly as he wrote in Greenmonth that it would.**

## 5. Major locations

### 5.1 Merefen and the county (main world)

- **The Old Toll crossing** — four hundred yards up the road, narrower and older, out of cart use for two generations. Trust in abeyance, tolls suspended, fourth course open the width of a hand and scarred, a dressed block and forty yards of tarred hemp through the abutment on a splice cut out of a cooper's bin, a bell on the abutment with a band of forty-one rivets and a dull red seam in it that was closed from both sides at the same hour by two people four hundred miles apart. **The road is a line and two hands. There is no third seat and no glass.**
- **Sedge Reach Bridge** — four timber bays, the town's crossing, and in Volume 02 it is the failure. The scour under the nose of the third pier is a washtub and the honest repair is eleven tons of stone and a fortnight with the road shut, and **the pier is uninspected from the ninth of Embermonth because the man who would have inspected it is in another county.** The bench mark is a quarter of an inch low; the guild's man with the levels still refuses the guild's roll and is right to.
- **Weir Street** — the coopers, the foundry, Toban's yard, the rooms Aren rents, the salt loft.
- **The guild hall over the wharf** — the bench of nine, a long room, a Saturday sitting when a thing is important enough.
- **The salt-loft rooms** — the Archive, the press, the day-book, the weighing book that is not a record and is the salt merchant's.
- **The provincial town, two hundred miles off** — the Office of Provincial Registration, the press registers, the fourth press, the provincial court, and Rowan Petch. **Name the office and the form. Do not name the people in the office, with one exception: Sabin Dain, who is a named person with a want and not a clerk.**

### 5.2 The County of Ash (the path)

A clay country on a river the county calls the Kiln, in ash-wood and peat. **Eight villages and one market town, Kilnholt, which is the county town and the seat of the assizes.** The county's own count is **nine hundred and forty-one hearths on the roll and about two thousand and a half people**, and the county's official figure is the hearths, because a hearth is what a fire is and a fire is what a person has. On the ash is the road.

- **The Ninebridge** — nine spans over the Kiln, and **a road in Ash is a record: every span is edged in fired tile, one tile to a kerb stone, the device impressed, the kiln's mark on the back.** The road is four hundred years old and the tiling is sixty. **The hand of the Ninebridge** is the person who keeps it, and the person is a tile-cutter, and the word is *the hand* and not *steward*, and there is no Ash word for *refused* that does not also mean *lost*.
- **The kilns.** Four of them, at Kilnholt, Ninebridge, and two villages. A rolling kiln is fired for nine days and takes a cord of wood a day, and **the ash-woods are the county's second industry and are the fuel for the first**, so a county that triples its tiles triples its cutting. **The kilns are an employer and they can close, and in Thawmonth two of them close, and that is Volume 02's labour failure and it is not a metaphor.**
- **The Roll of Persons** at the assizes. Nine hundred and forty-one hearths, and the number of tiles fired on the roll to date is **two thousand, nine hundred and six**, against about two thousand and a half people. **There are four hundred and fifty-six more tiles than there are people, and somebody has been fired twice, and nobody has ever counted, because a county counts fires and does not count itself.** The first chapter on the far side is a man doing a stock count in a kiln ledger.
- **The List of Persons to be Struck** — about two hundred names, eleven months old, kept under the county's own law by the rollkeeper, and the offer is to burn it in the market square at the assizes, publicly, in front of witnesses, **because a struck person cannot be read, and a person who cannot be read cannot carry the softening.** Burning the list stops the disease. That is the argument, it is correct, the magistrate believes it, and he is right about the fever.
- **The tiles themselves.** Seal-tile, six inches by four, fired clay, an impressed device, and on the back a hand and a mark. A **fired** tile is permanent and cannot be argued with. A **soft** tile is a mark in clay that has not been through the kiln; it is carried for a child until the seventh year; and it can be **re-pressed**, which is one thing nobody in nineteen years has wanted, because re-pressing is the one thing a record must not be able to do. **Ash's founding act is the decision to fire everything, made in YR 308, four years after the severance, by a county that had lost the register and could not bear to lose the people. It protected the county for nineteen years. It is the disease.**
- **The softening.** The county's own word. A person loses the sequence of their own day, then their trade, then their hearth, then their own name; a soft person cannot be witnessed by anybody, including themselves, and the county's law requires a person to be witnessed by two others to stay on the roll. It is not an infection and it is not a curse. **A person is going back to being clay.**

## 6. Major factions

Each has a want that survives Aren leaving the room. None is a monster and three are better than he is.

- **The Rollkeeper's office at the assizes.** Wants **one roll, correct, and the county has never had two.** Two rolls is not redundancy; it is an admission that the first one can be wrong, and this office has never admitted that anything it wrote could be wrong. **Pernel Quist**, 44, rollkeeper, nineteen years in the job, is the volume's best bureaucrat and she is fighting the truth with a filing system.
- **The kilns and the ash-woods.** Want the spring's clay dug, the rolling done, and the wages paid. **Three times the tiles is three times the wood is three times the cutting, and the ash-woods are the fuel, and in Thawmonth two kilns close and about forty men are out.** The kilns can do the arithmetic faster than the magistrate can.
- **The struck.** About two hundred people, alive, employed, and **not on the roll** — which in Ash means not a parish, not a market stall, not a marriage, not a burial in the county's ground, not countable. Their want is not money. It is to be *asked*. **Ostry Vaile**, 58, kiln-worker, struck eleven months, who has said in a room that he would rather be left alone and uncopied, which is the anti-Archive position and the most defensible sentence anybody in the volume says.
- **The bench.** Small, quarrelsome, mostly wrong, and the Ash version of a bench: it has no writ to answer and no bell to ring and it is the only body in the county that meets, because a county that has been cut off still has to argue about a road. **It is not a rescue and must not be written as one.** It argues about the price of tile.
- **The county's one physician.** **Hesper Lound**, 63, who wrote the finding four years ago: **the softening travels from a mark to the person who reads it, and from a person to the marks of that person's household, and it travels fastest along the marks that are read most, and the marks that are read most are the roll marks, because a clerk reads the roll every morning.** The cure is therefore not fewer records. **The cure is records that do not agree with each other**, because a thing cannot travel along three lines that disagree about where the line goes. This is a medical finding. It is true. It has been in a drawer for four years. It is the seed of the resolution and it is not a moral argument, it is a physician's.
- **The Office of Provincial Registration.** Wants a complete register and a lawful basis for acting on it, and it has found one that needs no court and names nobody. It is not the marshal's office and it is not under him, and it is more dangerous than the marshal's, because it is *doing its job*.
- **The counting house of the tolls of Bellweather-Living.** A creditor's office with a bundle of string, four conditions on a wrapper, a four-hundred-leaf register, a frightened clerk's fair copy in it, an index with nine entries, and a grandson of nine whose name is on the front of the book. **Alis Merrow wants to know whether the arch he mortgaged is an asset or a lie**, because a second severance in the same hand four years later is evidence that a writ can *work* — that a city can be removed and the debt with it — and if a writ can work, four generations of refusal were a strategy rather than a virtue, and his counting house has been sentimental for eleven years. **He is right, which is the problem, and his office is the volume's answer.**

## 7. The mechanics this volume runs on

1. **The road is a line and two hands.** A return must be prepared by two hands, a date, and a witness at each end who is not the person keeping the road, or the road does not open. **If the witness is not there the road does not open and it does not fail, and nobody is to say afterwards that it failed.** That distinction is Sera Quen's, it is the engine of the whole volume, and Ash's terms are written in it.
2. **A record is a person who is not present.** It has to be shown, not told. A person struck off a roll in Ash is alive, is employed, is spoken to in a market, and does not exist for any purpose that requires a number. Bellweather-Living's crossing book has eleven crossings and nine names and no souls; Ash's roll has nine hundred and forty-one hearths, about two thousand and a half people, and two thousand nine hundred and six tiles. The second of those is the county's whole problem in one line.
3. **Copying is not neutral, and it is direction-shaped.** A copy is more stable than the original and the original is what is lost. Mara's archive, the toll houses' four conditions, the Hushwater woman's refusal, the roll's two thousand nine hundred and six — all of them are this and none of them is a metaphor. The county's four-generation founding conviction is the sentence the volume exists to correct, because **a thing written down can be taken from you. What cannot be taken from you is being asked.**
4. **A form is a machine for making an office out of a word.** **Form R.C. 4, Return of Contact**, of the Office of Provincial Registration. Ruled columns: *Object*, *Date*, *Person having knowledge*, *Keeper*, *How often*. It is undated on the copy that came, and **a form with no date on it does not have to be answered and therefore does not have to be tried.** The marginal instruction on the reverse, in the Office's own hand, is the volume's planted horror and **nobody in the volume understands it**: *where no office of keeper is found in the county schedule, the person having knowledge shall be entered under the column headed Keeper, and the entry shall not be construed as the creation of an office.* Volume 02 may put the private-keeper clause on the page in an office's hand. **Volume 02 may not let anybody understand it.** Volume 03 is where Aren learns what it is being read as.
5. **A count is a list with the names taken off.** The Office's second instrument of the volume is not a writ, a seizure, an order or an instruction. It is a **return on the Archive's own record asking for a count of persons rather than names**, which is lawful, needs no court, and cannot be refused without refusing a number. **This is how a road gets closed without a keeper ever being named, and it is how Edric's question — who else has he told — is answered without a single person being named.** The Crown escalating in kind rather than in paper.
6. **A refusal is cheap or it is not a refusal.** The line, the two hands, the date, the witness at each end. Extended to records: a record held by one person is a favour; a record held in one building is a custody and a thing a court can order produced; a record held in three places of three different kinds is a thing a court has to answer three times. **Three different kinds of holding, because one kind of holding is one kind of failure.** Ash's three-hand roll is a creditor's office solving its own collection problem, and that is why it works.
7. **A cull offered as a kindness is still a cull.** The List of Persons to be Struck is administered by a decent magistrate who wants the softening to stop before the spring. He is right about the fever. He is wrong about the names. **No one in the volume may call him a villain, and no one in the volume may be able to say he is wrong about the fever.**

## 8. Escalation sequence — five blocks, Chapters 51–100

### Block 1 — Chapters 51–60: The Form and the Registrar

*Early and deliberately bureaucratic, because the Crown is a machine and the reader has to see the teeth before they close. The Stage 2 terms, the drift arithmetic, the count, and Halven's trade are all in this block.*

**Ch 51 · the first of Embermonth · Aren.** Three days after the bell. **A second Form R.C. 4 comes back from the Office of Provincial Registration, and a person comes with it.** The person is **Sabin Dain**, 33, an officer of that office whose title on her own form is an aid to the register, and who **is not a member of anything**, and who wants — in her own voice, early, in her own trade's words — **that no family in this country is ever again removed from a record by a name nobody entered.** She is Volume 02's mirror of Mara Vey, and the volume says so on the page exactly once and never again. She fills in the *Keeper* column, because the form says to, and she reads the marginal instruction out loud in a gateway in front of four men, because a woman who is about to be difficult reads the difficult part first. She has not come with a threat. She has come because **a form with no date on it does not have to be answered**, and she has found that in eleven months this office has sent nineteen to this county and has had an answer to none, and she would like a date and a person's name, in that order, and she will go back and get her office to send a dated one. **She is right, she is helpful, and she is the vector, and the reader should be able to see it by Chapter 55.** The printed instruction on the reverse also carries **the Gatehouse's own figure for a held road — three weeks — as a standard of measurement, in an office that cannot say whose figure it is.** That is the drift plant, in Block 1, in an office's hand, where a reader can check it against Chapter 47's notebook.

**Ch 52 · the second · Mara.** The four-hundred-word list sent north on the twenty-seventh of Longlight was addressed *to the city of Bellweather, and to any person keeping the road agreed with it*. **It has been answered twice.** The first answer came from Bellweather-Living in eleven weeks and is four lines long and is about a bell. The second came in the fourth week, in a hand that is not a clerk's, and it is about a man who has been struck off a roll of persons and cannot be found because he is alive and is not consulted. Mara reads the second answer four times, does not know what a roll of persons is, looks it up, finds that a roll of persons in this country is a thing a Crown clerk would use for a village, and then finds nothing further, **because there is no county of Ash on any map in this county.** She enters the second answer in the day-book against no person with the hour, writes that the office cannot tell whether it is a second road or a forgery, and **sends it to nobody** — for the reason she wrote herself in Greenmonth about a claim with a cause in it: *a man can enter a claim against the office. A county cannot be served.*

**Ch 53 · the third · Aren.** The yard, a factor's stone gone, a county town's wall going, **a fortnight in it and spoken for.** Toban's count, closed, in the yard, in a gateway, with four men: a yard cannot be hired for a season it does not have, and a man who has been the reason there is any work in it for five months has been gone a month and has sent no word, **and that is not a moral failing, it is a fact about a river, and the yard's whole argument is that the road is not a trade and the yard is.** The second Crown instrument of the volume arrives at the same hour and **it is not a paper that threatens anybody:** it is a request for a **count of persons** who have had knowledge of a declared object, in a county, in a form, with a date on it and a space for a figure. Toban's answer, invented in Greenmonth and now load-bearing a third time: **the number of the lines, the hour, and the fact that this yard inspects no crossing in this county because this yard builds them and does not inspect them, and a figure for a thing it has not got — because a count of persons is not a thing anybody in a yard can supply, and the honest answer to a count is a fact and not a number.**

**Ch 54 · the fourth, a market day · Mara and Aren.** **The count.** Reeve Aldis Fenn calls it in the guild hall over the wharf, because a bench has to decide in Frostmonth what it is, and a bench cannot decide about a bridge without knowing who can be asked to stop it. The question is the count's own question: **how many persons in this county can say no to the Old Toll crossing, and be believed, and go on living in this town afterwards.** The answer is **eleven, and it is not four**, and the eleven are read out with a date and an hour and are **not entered in the roll**, because a roll in this town has a *Keeper* column on it now. Bram Ottery is one of the eleven and says so himself, and says he has not been paid for anything since the fourth of Greenmonth and does not intend to be asked. **Mara's answer, in her trade's words: *a count is a list with the names taken off, and a list with the names taken off is the one document I have spent four years refusing to make.*** Two other things happen in this room. **A man comes up the stair with a form in the ordinary way, and it is the eastern-counties claimant; the phrase Culling Order is used once, in his mouth, with no member attached to it, and is not used again in this volume.** Mara takes the form and it is not this office's war, and she says so out loud, and a reader who has waited eleven years for her to take it will understand that the volume is about the fact that she did not.

**Ch 55 · the fifth · Mara and Halven.** **Halven's shield.** The marshal's emergency office offers him an endorsement on the same printed instruction that carries Form R.C. 4, which discharges the writ of enquiry on the reeve's office and ends the question of what a registrar did with a gap on a shelf in YR 309. The price is the ledger. **Mara recognises the offer because Aren refused the same offer in a room with a clerk in it eight days before, and the record of that refusal is in the same county file, and nobody can undo it, and she writes that down.** Halven takes it. He sells the *Anchors, schedules and dispositions, Alder Vale* — the volume that is not on the fourth press shelf, whose removal is dated the nineteenth of Mudmonth YR 301 with no name and no office — to the Office of Provincial Registration, in a room, for an endorsement with a number on it, and the book is gone before the day is out. **He discloses it to Mara, unasked, in his own hand, in a form that cannot protect him, and asks that the disclosure say he did it on his own motion and not on a fee.** His want, in his own voice, is the sentence Volume 11 will need: *I want there to be one document in the world in which it says I did it.* **And what is in the book is this, and only this:** a schedule for **three bells on the Alder Vale crossing**, of which two are entered removed with no office against either, and the third is entered *retained* — and the retained one is the bell on the Old Toll abutment in Merefen; and a schedule of dispositions whose county column runs past Alder Vale to eleven other counties, **one of which is Ash, and the disposition word against Ash is the word the county of Ash uses for a person who has been pressed into a tile and fired.** A Crown office did to Ash in YR 305 exactly what Ash is now doing to its own people, and nobody in Ash knows it, and the reader can see the word and cannot be sure what it means, and neither can anybody in the room.

**Ch 56 · the sixth · Mara and Tovan.** The second writ of enquiry is discharged and the third is answered, and **the Archive's holding of the second answer is a problem and not a triumph:** the four places cannot hold it, because it did not come through the channel and cannot be dated in the channel's terms. It goes into the day-book and **into a press that is under a bar**, and a form asks whether the office holds any record relating to a declared object in a second county, and the honest answer is yes, and the honest answer is a name on a form, and the name is a county that does not exist. And the lease: the salt merchant says the second floor has a term on it and a landlord, and the term ends in Rainmonth, and Mara costs the option of a second building in a market that has lost a market day, pays it, and enters it — the smallest paid item in the business, a second time.

**Ch 57 · the seventh · Aren.** **Rowan Petch comes down the road with a question he has been asked and cannot answer**, and the question is the volume's best one: *the office of which I am a clerk has been asked to say what the county file is for.* He must write something and he has four days. He asks the only man in the county who reads a press register for a living, and the answer is that **a press register answers what was entered on the shelf**, and the fourth press's register says a book was entered and is not on the shelf and was removed on a date with no name, **and that those are two different questions, and the office that asked has asked the wrong one, and the wrong question is the one that gets answered.** A day's hard walk is six leagues and the provincial town is further, and Petch has four days, and he goes — on his own time, at his own cost, asking his own office a question he has to be right about. **This is the second time in the book he has done that, and it is why he is one of the four who can see the index.**

**Ch 58 · the eighth · Mara.** The reading of the fair copy of a fair copy, in her own room, with the second keeper. **The index has nine entries and eight are Alder Vale and the ninth is not, and the ninth is dated YR 305, and the column headed *returned* is empty for it, and there is a separate column headed *answered* with a date in it, and the date is eleven days after the tender.** Eleven days is a clerk's answer and not a river's. **The writ at Alder Vale was refused. The writ at Ash was complied with, and the compliance is a document, and the document is in a book in a city that does not admit to the Crown that it exists.** The name is not on the index, and the register does not leave the city, and the four conditions say it is not produced in court and has not been asked for and will not be. **Mara enters one fact and refuses three inferences, and writes the three refusals out, and the refusals are the volume's first statement of its argument:** a date can be looked for, a press-mark cannot be argued with, a hand cannot be produced, and a county that is not on a map cannot be asked a question. And she does the thing the eighth ruled line requires: **she does not fill it in, and she writes down that she looked and wanted to, for the second time, and the two lines are a year apart and are read together.**

**Ch 59 · the ninth, a market day, the fourth hour · Aren.** **The scheduled crossing, made properly, and the far end is wrong.** A line of tarred hemp, a date, and a witness at each end who is not the steward, on a named day written in four books in two countries. The far end answers. **The far end is not Bellweather-Living: the weather is wrong for a river city, the steps are clay, the bell on the coping carries a mark that is not the Bellweather founder's mark, and the woman at the far end is not Sera Quen and does not give her name.** She says a county. She asks him one question, and the question is *are you the one who sends people*, and he says no, and she says that is what the last one said too, **and the road does not open**, because the terms of a road are that the far end decides, and the far end has not consented, and a road that opens onto a place that has not agreed to be a road is a door. **He does not go through, does not argue, and stands on a bridge in a market town and lets a woman in another county refuse him.** He comes back and says one sentence: *she did not open it and she was right, and I was not the man who sent anybody, and it made no difference, which is how I know she was right.* And the toll-clerk on the north steps, the witness at the Bellweather end, says the thing the reader needs: **a road that is refused is worth more than a road that is open, and a county that refuses a road has something to refuse it with — and this county has nothing.**

**Ch 60 · the ninth of Embermonth, the fourth hour · Aren, and the crossing out.** **He goes anyway, and the going is the block's turn, because the far end has answered once, and answering is a consent to be asked again.** A return stitch prepared with two hands, a date, and a witness at each end, **for the first time in the volume by a person who is a keeper of nothing: Bram Ottery, unemployed, unrewarded, standing as the witness at the Merefen end of a road he does not keep and has no office in, whose reason is that a man who has written down a true thing and been punished for it would like once to stand somewhere while a true thing was being done.** Path time **twenty-three days**. Main-world absence **twenty-six days**; he comes back on the **fifth of Wolfmonth** with a fever of three days and a way of standing that is not his.

### Block 2 — Chapters 61–70: Twenty-Three Days

*The far side. A different kind of community. No arch fails. A stock count, a law, and a list.*

**Ch 61 · Ash, the first day · Aren.** The north steps are clay steps, the water is a river called the Kiln, the town is a kiln town, and the air is full of the sound of something being fired for nine days. **The county's introduction to him is a stock count, and the stock count is the chapter.** Nine hundred and forty-one hearths on the roll, about two thousand and a half people, and **two thousand nine hundred and six tiles fired on the roll to date, and the tiles are fired in fours, and nine hundred and forty-one does not divide into anything.** A man with a ledger and a thumb black to the second joint says the county has always counted fires and has never counted itself, and then says the number out loud, and then says a second number, which is four hundred and fifty-six, and then stops, and does not say what it is, and Aren works it out in a minute and a half and says it out loud in a kiln shed, **and a room of nine people stops what it is doing.** The room is a room of **the struck**, and one of them is a man of fifty-eight who has been struck eleven months and who says the number is right and that he knows what it is, and that it is his mother, and that she has been fired twice, and that the second time was in the winter, and that she was not asked. **The volume's first four hundred words about a path are about a stock count and an arithmetic error, and neither is about magic.**

**Ch 62 · Ash, the second to the fourth day · Aren.** **The Roll of Persons at the assizes, and Pernel Quist, and the law.** A tile to a person: a pressed device, a name, a hearth, a trade, and a hand, the last of them pressed by the person themselves in wet clay, so that the mark is theirs and not the office's. A person is on the roll if two others witness them and their tile agrees with the clerk's entry. **A struck person is not struck from the roll; they are struck from the count, and the difference between those two things is the county's whole moral life and nobody in the county can state it.** A struck person is alive, employed, and **is not consulted** — cannot witness, cannot be witnessed, cannot be married into a hearth, cannot be buried in the county's ground, cannot be given a name at a kiln. Quist wants one roll, correct. She is hiding nothing. She has nineteen years of a filing system that has never been wrong, a physician's finding from four years back saying her system is the vector, and **she has the finding in a drawer and has read it and has concluded that a physician's finding about reading is not a finding about a roll — reasoning correctly, and wrong, and the chapter must let both of those be visible from inside her head.**

**Ch 63 · Ash, the fifth day · Aren.** **Magistrate Colm Arreld**, 54, of the assizes, in a room with a stove and a clerk, and the list. He is not a villain and does not use a word like one. He wants the softening to stop before the spring, because the spring is when the clay is dug and the rolling is heaviest and the last two winters have been the worst. **The instrument is the List of Persons to be Struck: about two hundred names, eleven months old, and the offer is to burn it in the market square at the assizes in front of witnesses, because a struck person cannot be read and a person who cannot be read cannot carry the softening.** The county's own law provides for it; the clerk has the law open on the table and it is four lines and it is signed by three magistrates. **Aren is asked what he thinks, by a decent man who wants his opinion — the first time anybody in this book has asked him for advice instead of a service, and the moment the whole volume turns on.** What he says is: *I would want to know what happens to the tile.* And the magistrate says: *the tile is fired, and then there is no name on it, and then there is a hearth with a fire in it and no reason anybody can give for the fire, and that is not a worse thing than a name that cannot be read.* **And Aren, who has spent five months being the best reader of a room in a book about not doing that, finds that he has nothing, and the reason he has nothing is that the magistrate is right about the fever — and that is the moment the volume stops being about a man learning a lesson and becomes about a method that works.**

**Ch 64–65 · Ash, the sixth to the tenth day · Aren and Ilsa Soke.** **The hand of the Ninebridge.** A tile-cutter of forty-one who keeps nine spans, four hundred yards of tiled kerb, and a road that is a record, and who is the person who answered Aren's question in Chapter 59, and who is **not** a steward, because the word here is *the hand*, and the reason there is no Ash word for *refused* that does not also mean *lost* is that nothing has ever been refused here. Her want, in her own voice: **that a road in this county is the only thing in it that is not an argument**, because a road is tiled and a tiling is a record and a record does not have an opinion, and she has spent nine years being asked to mend a road that is being cut up by people who will not say which half they want. She gives him work on the terms that he is useful and not a visitor, and the work is **re-pressing a soft tile**, which is a thing a child does and which he is too big for, and which takes four days to learn, and which is the single craft the volume needs from him. **And a Bind does not travel and a motion does: the motion he carried back from Bellweather in Chapter 10 — a hand setting a thing true against a thing out of true — is the motion of a man re-pressing a soft tile, and it is on the right side of the world for the first time, and it belongs to a county and not to a city, and nobody in either world has taught him anything.** He does not get stronger. He gets a second use for the same hand, which is the whole of what Stage 2 is.

**Ch 66 · Ash, the eleventh day · Aren.** The struck, and the argument. Two hundred people whose want is not money but to be asked. **Ostry Vaile**, 58, kiln-worker, struck eleven months, who has said in a room that he would rather be left alone and uncopied, and who says the sentence the book needs: **a person who is struck has to be able to answer for himself, and if he answers he goes back on it, and if he cannot answer then he was never on it and we have been keeping a list of things.** He is right. He has been right for eleven months. **Nobody in the county has ever put that sentence in a form, and the reason is not authority: it is that a form requires a column, and the column is *struck*, and there is no column in the world for *answered*.** And the four hundred and fifty-six, and the woman fired twice in the winter without being asked, and the second time, in the winter, **by her own household, because a soft tile can be re-pressed by anybody with a thumb and a wet cloth.**

**Ch 67–68 · Ash, the twelfth to the eighteenth day · Aren.** **Hesper Lound, the physician, and the finding that is four years old and has been ignored.** Softening travels from a mark to the person who reads it and from a person to the marks of that person's household, **and it travels fastest along the marks that are read most, and the marks that are read most are the roll marks, because a clerk reads the roll every morning.** The cure is not fewer records. **The cure is records that do not agree with each other.** Three copies, in three hands, in three places, none authoritative alone, a person restored when two of the three agree and removed when two of the three agree otherwise, and no single office able to do either. It has been in a drawer for four years. **Aren does not solve it in this block and is not permitted to.** What he does is refuse the list — and refusing the list is wrong, and he knows it is wrong, and the chapter makes him say the sentence the rest of the volume is built on: *if I tell this room not to burn that list, I am telling a county with a fever in it to keep a list, on my word, and I am the man who put the list there by arguing about it in front of witnesses, and I have been doing this for eleven years and I am not going to do it again in a hat.* He asks for a week. **Asking for a week is the most expensive thing he does in the volume, and the first time in the book that he asks a community for time instead of taking it.**

**Ch 69 · Ash, the nineteenth day · Aren and Ilsa Soke.** **The terms, and the road.** Five terms, in the county's own hand, **on a tile and in a book, because a tile cannot be copied out of and a book can — and the county has understood something in four years that Mara Vey has understood for four.** 1. The road is not to be made to carry a person. 2. No name is to be read out on the far side of the line by anybody from the north, and no name is to be written in the line's book at the north end. 3. The north is not to send anybody who can be asked to come and look. 4. **A person who is struck may answer, and if a person who is struck answers, the person is restored.** 5. The road is kept by a hand at each end and a witness at each end, and the witness at Ash's end is chosen by Ash. **Term four is the whole volume and term four is what kills the road.** The mechanism is on the page and it is not magic: restoring a struck person requires the roll to be re-read, re-reading a roll is a working on a scale of two hundred, and a working at that scale needs an anchor, a bound, a counterweight and a witness, **and a bound is a promise, and a promise about two hundred people to one office is a shape the Gatehouse has refused before, and the shape it refuses is the shape a severance writ has.** He does not know that. He is a bridgewright and he can see a load path, and he is about to put four hundred tons into a bed of river gravel — **and the volume says so, in his own trade's words, in a notebook, four months early, and he does not act on it.** That is Chapter 69's whole content and it is the volume's most damning page: **a man writing down the mistake in the correct technical vocabulary four months before he makes it, because he has written the mistake down before.**

**Ch 70 · the fifth of Wolfmonth · Mara, and the return.** He comes up on the fifth with a fever of three days — running the fifth, sixth and seventh, breaking on the eighth — and with **a way of standing** that is a ferrywright's on a slip and that he cannot source. The second answer is on the desk in Mara's fair hand with the hour on it, and the third thing he cannot tell her is the one that matters, and the block ends with **the fever, the silence about the way he stands, and a page in the day-book in which she writes down that she has noticed and that she is not going to ask, because she wrote in Fallowmonth that this office is not going to ask him again.** Her reason, written under it, is the sentence the rest of the volume is built on: **the not asking has changed from a protection into a thing she is doing to him, and the difference is that a protection is entered in a file and this is entered in a day-book, and she has noticed the difference, and she has noticed it late.**

### Block 3 — Chapters 71–80: Ordinary Work in Merefen

*The main-world engine, the recovery anchor, and the failure that is not a bridge. The Crown escalates in kind. The election.*

**Ch 71 · the ninth of Wolfmonth, a market day · Tovan.** A page in the yard's book, a factor's stone gone, a wall job going, **a fortnight in it.** The volume's labour problem, stated in the yard with four men in it. Pei Tarrow asks to be put off two days instead of three. A man comes from the west channel who has heard the warden is abroad and does not care. **And a Crown's man comes up Weir Street with a form in a leather bag, and Tovan gives him the number of the lines, the hour, and a figure for a thing the yard has not got, and says the thing is the same answer to a Crown's form as a fact instead of a name, and does not say whose idea it was.**

**Ch 72 · the fifteenth of Wolfmonth · Mara and Rowan Petch.** **Petch's answer, and the wrong question.** He brings back what the press register knows, and it is a bomb with a slow fuse: the office is now asking a different question, **and the different question is about the file, and the file has a woman's name in it.** And Sabin Dain's second letter, which is **dated** — the first dated form in the case — and which asks for a **count of persons** rather than names, and which is signed.

**Ch 73 · the twenty-first of Wolfmonth · Aren and Lysa Fenn.** Lysa Fenn's first frightened morning in the volume. **There is no instrument she can produce against a count of persons.** A count is not a writ, is not returnable, names nobody, and cannot be refused without refusing a figure, and a court that refuses a figure is a court that has said a number is not a record. She goes at it the only way she has, which is procedurally, and the procedural answer is that a returnable paper with no person named in it is a paper with no party, and a paper with no party **expires**, and a thing that expires is a thing nobody has to answer. **It is the first time in the book that a technicality of her own trade beats an instrument, and she is quietly delighted and frightened in the same hour, and Aren watches her be both and understands that this is what competent looks like on a woman who is not him.**

**Ch 74 · the fourth of Frostmonth, a market day · the bench.** **The frost, the bench, and the first of the two visits.** A bench of nine keeping a crossing it does not own, four marks and six a year, a chest with two marks in it, the second of the two visits voted in Longlight. Ordinary work, in daylight, with a cold chisel. The guild's man with the levels puts up a specification of two leaves and **does not open at the eighth line this time, because the eighth line is a seat, and there is no seat, and the number of uses of a seat that is not there is not a thing this bench may know either.** Tovan is in the room, is not a member, says the true thing and does not vote — for the fourth time in six months — and the true thing this time is about the third asking: *a bench that is generous decides, and a bench that is asked is being told what it is for, and a bench that is asked three times and says yes three times is not a bench, it is a subscription.*

**Ch 75 · the ninth of Frostmonth, a Monday, a market day · the election.** **A road warden is elected in this room in Frostmonth.** Reeve Aldis Fenn is re-elected to a bench that has no rule to keep her off it, and the vote is eight hands and a reeve's, and it is the shortest minute in the roll and the first time in eleven years that anybody in that room has been put in an office by a vote rather than appointed. **And Aren Kest is elected a road warden of the Merefen bridge guild on the ninth of Frostmonth, by eight hands and a reeve's, and the clerk asks whether he accepts, and he says a thing that is in the roll and that the volume ends on: that he accepts the vote and not the office, because a vote is a thing a town gives a man and an office is a thing a man is held to, and he does not intend to be the man who holds this crossing.** He is on the roll and he is elected and he will have a vote on a question that is not about him. And the count is read out again, on the record, and it is **twelve** — because Bram Ottery is on the roll now, elected, having stood as a witness — and **a count that goes up by one when a man is given a vote is a count measuring the wrong thing, and nobody in the room can prove it, and the chapter does not pretend otherwise.**

**Ch 76 · the ninth of Frostmonth, the afternoon · Mara.** **The count reaches the province.** The Archive returns a figure for a returnable form and the figure is **eleven**, and eleven is lawful, and eleven is not a list, and **the eleven are in no order and there are no names and every one of the four that are in a file is in it**, and the form's *Keeper* column on the copy that went is blank, because a form with a figure in it does not need a name in it. **This is the answer to Edric's question, and it is a good answer, and it is made by a keeper of records, and it is made for the Crown, and nobody in the room notices what has just happened until Dain's reply — which is three lines, and dated, and reads: *received. The office notes that a count of persons is not a list and proposes a count of persons for the county as a whole.*** The second sentence is the volume's political engine and it is a sentence and not an instrument, and it arrives four days later, and it is a lady's writing. **Aren is not in the room and is the only person in the book who could have said what it is, and the reason he is not in the room is that he is in another county, and the reason he is in another county is a bench in a guild hall over a wharf and a binding agreement with a bench of bridgewrights four hundred miles off.** The engine is now a document being filled in on both sides by careful people.

**Ch 77 · the fourth of Hearthmonth, a market day · the crossing out.** He goes at the fourth hour, and the preparation is the volume's set piece: **two hands, a date, a line, and a witness at each end who is not the person keeping the road.** Merefen's end: **Tovan Kest, the hand**, and Bram Ottery, the witness, and the toll-holder's clerk, and **Reeve Aldis Fenn with a sheet and a rule.** She is on the bench now and she is voting on a thing she is witnessing, **and she refuses to do both, and is minuted.** The gate is not opened from Merefen: the far end opens it, on the scheduled day, and the county's terms are the gate, and Ash's hand is Ilsa Soke and Ash's witness is a person the county has chosen and Aren has never met. **Nine months and six days.**

**Ch 78–80 · Ash, the first twenty-two weeks · Aren.** The long middle in three beats. **(a) The argument**, in a room with the bench, the kilns, the rollkeeper and about two hundred of the struck, in which the standing is not *we are right* and not *you are wrong* and is **a price**, and the price is three times the tiles. **(b) The kilns close**, two of them, in Thawmonth, because there is no wood and no money, and about forty men are out, and the county's alternative is a list, and the kilns can do that arithmetic faster than the magistrate can. **(c) The three hands** — the rollkeeper's office at the assizes, the hand of the Ninebridge, and **a fourth place that does not exist yet and has to be built out of a letter and an old press-mark**: a creditor's office, which is the only institution in this book that already keeps three kinds of holding for a reason that is not political. **Alis Merrow's counting house can hold a roll of persons in its press for a farthing of salt, because it held a chain of eleven sheets for a creditor who did not want them.** He is a man in another world and the only person who can do it, **and the price is the eleven sheets and a letter of conditions, and the fact that a roll of persons in a creditor's press is a thing that can be produced in a court in a country that does not exist — and Merrow's whole life's argument is that this is the worst possible thing, and he is going to do it anyway, and he will say so in a hall.**

### Block 4 — Chapters 81–90: The Nine Months

*The block where the volume fails, and fails in a way that is not a bridge. **The volume's second severance is not an arch. It is a form, and the hand that filled it in is his.**

**Ch 81 · the sixth month · Aren.** **The mistake, in the correct technical vocabulary, four months after he wrote it down.** The three hands are working. The struck are answering and being restored, and it works, **because it is the physician's finding and because two of three is a rule a person can check, and a person who can check is a person who has been asked.** Two hundred names. Then four hundred, because the work goes faster than the argument and the argument is always slower than the work. **Aren proposes, in a room — not a bench, a room, to a magistrate, to a clerk, to a rollkeeper — that the re-reading be done as a rite: the struck person re-presses their own soft tile in front of two witnesses, the two witnesses' marks go onto the two other tiles, and the office does not read the roll, it watches people answer.** It is the best idea anybody has had in the volume. It is consent made physical. It is also, in the trade's terms, **a working at the scale of four hundred people with one anchor and one bound, and the bound is *these people are the people*, and the counterweight is the rollkeeper's office, and the witness is two hundred of the struck.** He cannot see the load path, because he is not looking at a structure; he is looking at a document, **and the load path of a document is made of other documents, and the load goes into the weakest one, and the weakest one is a copy of a copy, and the copy of a copy is in a counting house in a city that does not admit to the Crown that it exists.** He says it out loud, in a hall, to about sixty people, on the fourth hour of a day in the sixth month of his own time there, and four words into a fifth sentence **a clerk in the counting house stops him and enters the arrest against him, with the day and the hour, in a book four hundred miles off.** It is the second arrest entered against a stranger in a year, in two countries. Nobody in the room understands what it means. Aren does, and says nothing.

**Ch 82–83 · the sixth month onward · the softening and the struck.** The rite works for eleven weeks. And then it does not, **because the softening travels along the marks that are read most, and the rite is the most-read thing in the county, and a man's own name re-pressed in front of two witnesses in a hall four times a day is the fastest line there is.** Vaile is restored in the ninth week, and he is at the kiln, and he is well, and he is **read** — his name, in a hall, by a stranger from the north, in a list, four hundred times, because a list of restorations is a thing a clerk reads every morning, and the clerk is a man, and the man is a man, and the marks that are read most are the roll marks. **Ostry Vaile softens in the ninth week and is alive, and employed, and in the roll in three hands, correctly, and does not know which kiln he fires.** The roll has him. The roll is right about him. **He is not dead and he is not struck and he is not consulted, and no instrument in this county or in Merefen will do anything about it, and the only person in the book who is not satisfied with that is the woman who reads for a living — and she is two hundred miles away in a fen and has not been told and will be told by a form.** No named supporting character is killed. Nobody is killed. **The volume's catastrophe is a person who is still there.**

**Ch 84 · the seventh month · Aren and Ilsa Soke.** **He works it out, and it takes him four days, and he gets it exactly right.** A record that a person can contradict is not a record of that person. A record that cannot be contradicted is a *claim* about a person. The county's founding act — fire everything — was an attempt to build a record that could not be argued with; it has protected them for nineteen years and it is the disease, **and the cure is a record that a person can change, which is a soft tile, in three hands, in three places, none authoritative alone — which is what they have already built, and it is being read too fast to survive.** And then the other half, which he gets first and cannot use: **it is not the three hands that is killing Vaile. It is him.** A stranger from the north, standing in a hall, reading a list of two hundred names aloud, four times a day, for eleven weeks, in a county where nobody had ever asked for a list to be read aloud, because everybody there could already read. **He stops the list. He stops it alone, in a room, without asking, by walking into a hall and taking a page out of a clerk's hand** — and it is the old move, the eleventh time in the book, and **it is right, and it is him deciding for a room, and Ilsa Soke says so to him on the spot, in her own words, and does not say it unkindly.** It is the first refusal in the volume and it is a professional refusal and not a civic one.

**Ch 85 · the eighth month · the coping at Ninebridge.** **There is no bell in the County of Ash and there has never been one, and the absence is the loudest fact in the volume.** Nothing was ever refused here, so nothing was ever rung, and a county with no record of a refusal has no word for one, and a roadkeeper in Ash is *the hand* and not a steward because there is nothing in the county's history for a steward to answer. **And there is a bell.** On a coping at Ninebridge, a hand's width, with a rope on it and no yoke — and it is not a bell, it is a **tile**, and the tile carries a mark, and the mark is **a hand laid flat on a book.** Nobody in the county knows what it is. The man who put it there is dead. It has been on that coping since before the roll was begun, and it is the only object in the County of Ash that anybody put there for no reason, **and it is the device that is in grey wax on a post in Merefen, on a storehouse door by the fish weir, on the back of a Crown's letter, and in a magistrate's finding in her own hand.** The reader has had it since Chapter 10. **No character in this volume explains it and none may. It is a thing on a coping, and it is the volume's last planted mystery, and it is planted on the last page of the Ash material and not in the middle — and the reader who has waited eleven months for an explanation is going to get a tile.**

**Ch 86 · the eighth month · the child.** **A soft tile and a river.** The child is **Bel, four years old, of Kilnholt**; a child's tile is carried soft until the seventh year; the mark on it is a thumbprint, and it was not pressed for her — **she pressed it herself, in the ordinary way a four-year-old makes a mark on wet clay, on a board in a kiln shed, and the mark is a river.** A river with a bend in it and a mill on it and a weir. The county has no such river. The woman who fired the tiles in that kiln last spring can give the date and can say she saw a child do it. **Aren is shown the tile by a woman who will not let him touch it, and he recognises the Sable in a mark made by a four-year-old's thumb in a county four hundred miles from the Sable, and he does not say the name of the river in the room — because a name in a room is a thing that goes into a book, and he has watched one bell-shaped seal go into a file from a single stone, and he has been the man who said the name in a room five times.** The county's answer, from the rollkeeper, is the most frightening sentence in the volume: **we have been looking at the wrong tile.** And the reader is given the arithmetic that makes it true: a soft tile can be re-pressed, so **a fired tile is the only record in the county that a person cannot change, and a record a person cannot change is a claim about a person and not a record of one** — which is the volume's argument, arrived at by a stock count and a child, and not by a philosopher.

**Ch 87 · the ninth month · the bell's absence, named.** The county has no bell because nothing was ever refused, and the tile on the coping is the only object anybody put there for no reason, **and the chapter's work is to make a room of Ash people say out loud that they do not have a word for what happened to them in YR 305, and that they have a roll instead, and that the roll is nineteen years old.** A woman of about sixty says the sentence that closes the argument: *we did not lose our register. We lost our refusal. A register is what you have instead.* And Ilsa Soke says the road sentence, in her own words: **a hand answers for a road and a hand can be asked, and there has never been anything in this county that could be asked, and that is why there has never been a hand that had to answer.** Both of these are the volume's argument in a path community's own voice, and neither is a paraphrase of anything said in Merefen.

**Ch 88 · the ninth month · the terms, and the price of term four.** **The negotiation that saves the county and closes the road.** The strike of the two hundred is ended by putting Ash's own terms in force: a struck person may answer, and answering restores. Restoring four hundred people requires re-reading the roll. Re-reading the roll is a working at a scale the Gatehouse has refused before, **and what Ash does about it is the county's own answer and not Aren's: the re-reading is broken up and made local and made slow, and it is done in the eight villages and the market town separately, over nine days, by the county's own people, with no office reading anything and no stranger in the room.** It works. It is slower than the rite. It is slower because it was designed by a county that had been cut off and not by a man with a bar, **and the whole of the volume's argument is in the difference between the two plans and the fact that only one of them can be run by people who are not him.** And then the road: the crossing out was made on a line, a date, two hands and a witness at each end, **and the witness at Ash's end was a person the county chose, and that person was struck in the eighth month, and the county did not replace him in time.** The road does not open on the scheduled day. It does not fail. **It does not open, and nobody is to say afterwards that it failed** — and the second route is simply not there for the rest of the volume, and he is four hundred miles away with no door, and the last nine months come down as one scheduled message on a named day in the form the Second Door allows, in a fair hand, being a list.

**Ch 89–90 · the ninth month, and the climax.** **Ch 89 is the county's day and it is not a battle.** The magistrate signs the three-hand roll. The rollkeeper refuses for two days and then signs, and her reason is the volume's most professional sentence: **a roll that can be wrong is not a weaker roll. It is a roll that has to be read twice, and a roll that has to be read twice cannot be read by one man in a morning.** The kilns reopen on a schedule of two thirds and about thirteen men stay out, and the bench argues about the price of tile and wins a reduction and loses a vote. **And the thing that is actually new is the four hundred and fifty-six**: the two thousand nine hundred and six tiles, the ones that were fired twice, and the discovery that a fired tile can be *re-ground* — that a county can take a claim about a person and turn it back into clay, and that this takes a kiln, a cord of wood, and a decision. **Ash begins to un-fire its own founding act, at a cost, on a schedule, with a bench, and it is the slowest and least dramatic and most permanent thing in the book.** **Ch 90 is the climax and it is a form.** The Second Door's terms are on the printed instruction in front of him in a filing room, and the fourth of them — one message, and it must be a list, and one object under a pound — is met by Ash with a **scheduled list**, and the list is the county's own answer to term four, and it is four hundred words, and **it is not a message, it is a bill, and it is itemised, and a clerk in Merefen can check every line of it.** The stage comes. It comes the way Stage 1 came: not as a reward, in a room, with a witness, and the room is a filing room and the person who witnesses it is the only other person in the county who can be asked. **The second route does not answer when it is called, and the volume's cost is stated in a fair hand in a day-book with the hour on it.**

### Block 5 — Chapters 91–100: Eighty-Eight Days, and a Roll in Three Hands

*The return, the concealment, the breach, the settlement, and the ledger.*

**Ch 91 · the second of Greenmonth, a Tuesday · Aren and Mara.** He comes up on the second of Greenmonth with a fever of three days — the second, third and fourth, breaking on the fifth at about the sixth hour — **and he does not tell her what it cost.** Not a lie. He tells her the numbers, because he has never not told her the numbers: the path interval, the main-world absence, the error against a three-week warning in a marginal instruction, the four pages of his own account. What he does not tell her is the morning. **He has lost the exact sequence of an ordinary morning with Tovan** — six or seven things in an order, a particular Tuesday, in a room over a cooper, in a yard on Weir Street, eleven years of it — and he can still do every one of them and cannot do them in that order, and he does not know the order. **And he cannot say which path gave him the way he stands.** Both are in a notebook. Neither is in a form. **And that is the concealment, and it is the whole of it, and it is the first time in the book that he has decided what a keeper of records may not be told and it is the third, and the first two were about a letter and cost him a filing room and a year.**

**Ch 92 · the fifth of Greenmonth · Mara, and the instrument that finds it.** **A keeper of records finds a cost with the one thing she cannot stop using, which is a date.** He tells her, in a room, on a working morning, that the Ash magistrate's clerk sent the schedule of the eight village re-readings on the nineteenth of his own month there, and the fair hand writes it down. She does not ask him anything. She goes to the page and compares two entries against the day-book: **two dates in his account are path dates and not main-world days, and he has given them to her in the wrong frame, twice, in one morning, without noticing.** A bridgewright would not have noticed. A keeper of records cannot not. She enters the fact, with the hour, and enters the two wrong dates, and enters that she did not ask, and enters *why she did not ask* — and the reason, written out in full, is the relationship milestone the series requires: **she wrote in Fallowmonth that this office is not going to ask him again, and the not asking was a protection, and the not asking is now a wound, and a wound she is choosing not to open is worse than a question, and she has noticed the change and has written the day she noticed it and it is a year late.** She does not ask him. **That is the breach. It is a working trust breaking over a thing she refused to do, and the volume does not resolve it and Volume 03 does not resolve it, and Volume 04's chosen intimacy is bought with it.**

**Ch 93 · the ninth of Greenmonth, a market day · Lysa Fenn and the count.** **The second count, the county as a whole, is answered by a filing room with a figure in it and no names in it**, and the figure is lawful and expired, and the count has become **the mechanism by which this office can be closed without anybody's name ever being written down anywhere.** Lysa Fenn's finding cannot touch it. The county's chamber goes into a room and comes out with something worse than nothing: **a resolution of the county council that the Bellweather Claims Archive is a body corporate, on a form whose printed space under *Function of the office* has been filled, in the founding's own words, with the space above the ruled line left empty** — and a space that a form has for a thing's name and not for a thing's purpose is the only reason the resolution is not what it would otherwise be. **A body corporate can be sued. A keeper can only be dismissed.** And nobody in the room says the sentence, and the chapter does not say it either, and the reader can.

**Ch 94 · the county town · Sabin Dain, and the reader being ahead of the plot.** Dain comes down the road herself, in daylight, and asks to be recorded, and is recorded, **because she has learned in eleven months that being recorded is the most frightening thing anybody can do to an officer and it is the only thing that makes an officer answerable.** She is right about the count and she is right about the archive and she is right that a filing room with a woman's name in a provincial file is a thing a person can be ruined with. **And she is the one who says the sentence the volume's readers have been waiting for and it is not a threat and it is not an offer: *the office is not asking you to tell us anything. The office is asking you to confirm a figure, and a figure has no name in it, and in four years there will be a number in a file with no name attached to it, and that number will be about this county, and nobody will be able to say whose hand made it.*** She is a woman with a form and a good filing system and a horror of unnamed things, which is exactly Mara's position mirrored, **and in Volume 04 she will join an order that has learned to remove names rather than complain about them, and the reader should be able to say at Chapter 90 that they saw it at Chapter 55.**

**Ch 95 · the sixteenth of Greenmonth · the second severance closes, in the wrong country.** **A clerk in the counting house of the tolls of Bellweather-Living, who is a grandson of the clerk whose fair copy is the third hand in a four-hundred-leaf register, reads the index and says that he can tell the die and cannot tell the hand.** The YR 305 line carries **press four, die eleven, and a later impression of the season** — and a die is a die, and a press is a press, and a press-mark does not lie about a hand the way a name does. **The reader can check it and Aren cannot, because the index has no name and the register does not leave the city.** Alis Merrow reads it, and puts it in his own counting house's record, in his own hand, with a date and an hour, under his own law, in his own city, and **the name of the signing office goes into a record in Bellweather-Living and not in Merefen** — a fair copy, signed, made at his own cost, entered as produced by a creditor's office in a matter in which no court in this county has jurisdiction. It is the first document in the series held by a person in a path. **And it is a fourth place, and it is the worst of the four, and he says so in a hall, and his reason is the volume's own: *a creditor's press is the worst place in the world to keep a roll of persons and the only place in the world where nobody can quietly take it out of the book, and I have been arguing that for eleven years and I am now the man who is doing it to my own city, and I would like the argument minuted and the minute put where a Crown's man will find it in about nine years.*** **The second severance therefore does not open inside this volume. It opens in Chapter 894, on a form, when a clerk in a provincial office puts two documents side by side, and the reader has been holding both halves for thirty chapters and has not been told once.**

**Ch 96 · the twenty-second of Greenmonth · Halven, and the office's paper.** **His disclosure is read into a record on his own motion, in his own hand, with the date, the hour, and the words *on his own motion and not on a fee*,** and the marshal's endorsement is produced beside it, with a number on it, and the two together are the first document in the case in which the Crown's shield and the Crown's payment for a book are in one paragraph. **Nobody is charged. He keeps the post. The endorsement stands and is not withdrawn, because the office that gave it has no procedure for withdrawing an endorsement and will not build one inside a quarter.** And Mara does the thing that is her whole character and the whole of her arc in one line: **she enters the disclosure as a copy, with the hour, and enters his reason for making it, and enters that the office has no power to compel or to protect, and does not write a word about whether he should have taken it**, and the not writing is entered, because she has learned in Fallowmonth that the not writing is the part that is a decision.

**Ch 97 · the twenty-fourth of Greenmonth · the ordinary work, and the bill.** **The Sedge Reach scour, at last, in Thawmonth's dry weeks, with a road shut for a fortnight, and a bench of nine asked a third time and saying yes a third time, and Tovan's yard doing the ordinary autumn work out of a chest with nothing in it.** The book's recovery anchor is a bill, and the bill is the volume's smallest and most damning document. **The trust's account for the absence, itemised in pence and footed:** eleven markets lost or held late and thin, by custom, **29 marks 19 shillings 6 pence**; tolls not taken on the shut wharf road, one hundred and fourteen days, **3 marks 19 shillings**; three Tuesday barges held off, **4 marks 2 shillings**; cartage, stone, lime, oak and nails, for the scour repair at the Sedge Reach, **2 marks 9 shillings**; **a line with no figure against it at all — the scour at the third pier, nose, uninspected from the ninth of Embermonth, and not known by anybody in this office to be a cause eleven weeks out.** **Total: fifty marks, one shilling and sixpence** — two thousand four hundred and eighteen pence, added in pence and footed, because the line is small and the year is not. And beside it, in a different book and never in the same sentence, **eleven marks and four pence** owed by a trust in abeyance, **two marks and three quarters** at eight pence a day on the cutting contract, and **twelve marks and ten pence** — sixteen weeks and two days of a copying post at three shillings a week, unclaimed, because he was not in the county to initial the returns, **and the post does not wait, and the county's surveyor took a man on in Thawmonth, and the post is a cage, and a cage is a place a man is sent for and also a place a man is sent away from, and he wrote that difference out in four lines in Fallowmonth and did not see the second half of it coming.**

**Ch 98–99 · the twenty-sixth and twenty-seventh of Greenmonth · the settlement, and the thing that is not settled.** **The list is not burned. It is superseded.** The magistrate's instrument is withdrawn by the county's own bench, in a room, with the physician's four-year-old finding read out and the three hands named, and the superseding is entered as a fact and not as a defeat, **because the magistrate says in the room that the list was the only instrument he had and that he is glad to be shown a second one and sorry it took four years and a stranger, and that is the whole of his testimony and it is in the county's own book.** Ash's dead stay Ash's; Ash's two hundred and fifty-four fired tiles are being re-ground at a cost, on a schedule, with a bench; the softening has slowed and not stopped. **And the thing that is not settled is the eighth ruled line, which is still empty, and which Mara Vey looks at for the second time in a year, and which she does not fill, and which is now the oldest document in the case and the one nobody has asked her about.** And the roll of the dead built out of Ferris Oat's brine weights is **still not built**, and the two day-book lines about the weights are now a year apart and are still read together, and the reason is written out again in full because the reason has changed: **the roll is a ledger and a ledger is a claim, and this office has learned this year from a county of four hundred miles that a claim about people is not a record of them.**

**Ch 100 · the ninth of Greenmonth, YR 313, a Wednesday, a market day · the last image.** The ninth of Greenmonth, at the fourth hour, at the lowest water of the year. **A scheduled message comes down the line from the County of Ash, on a named day, in a fair hand, and it is a list, and a list can only be checked.** It is the county's answer to term four: a struck person answered, and the person is restored, and the roll is in three hands, and **there are two thousand nine hundred and six tiles in a kiln in Kilnholt that nobody has opened, and four hundred and fifty-six of them are going to be ground back into clay in Hearthmonth, and a woman in the county has written, in a hand that is not a clerk's, that she does not know whether that is a good idea and that she is going to do it anyway and would like somebody to write down that she said so.** On the near abutment there is a boy who is sixteen and has been the steward's apprentice for two years and is still not good at it, holding one end of a line, and beside him a carpenter who is a hand and not a keeper, and **at the far end of the line a tile-cutter of forty-one and a person nobody in Merefen has ever seen**, and they ring the bell because that is the arrangement, and it is not Aren's. **A road warden of the Merefen bridge guild, elected on the ninth of Frostmonth by eight hands and a reeve's, is on the Sedge Reach deck with a tool bag, counting load points out of habit, and his left palm is warm, and he does not need it, and nothing in the sound is his.** And four hundred miles off, in a room above a kiln, **a man of fifty-eight who fired the tiles in that kiln last spring does not know which kiln he fires**, and his name is in three books, and the books are right about him, and no instrument in two countries will do anything about it, and the woman who reads for a living has written the whole of it down with the hour on it, against nobody, in a day-book over a weigh-house, and the last line in it is the reason she wrote it.

## 9. Volume climax

**Chapters 88–90: the second severance, which is not an arch.**

The volume's last structural act is a form being filled in by the wrong person for the right reason. In the sixth month of his own time in the County of Ash, Aren proposes — in a room, to a magistrate, a clerk, a rollkeeper and about sixty people, four words into a fifth sentence — that the re-reading of four hundred struck tiles be performed as a rite: each person re-presses their own soft tile in front of two witnesses, the witnesses' marks go onto two other tiles, **and the office does not read the roll, it watches people answer.** It is the best idea anybody has had in the volume. It is consent made physical. It is also, in the trade's terms, a working at the scale of four hundred people with one anchor and one bound, and **the bound is a promise, and a promise about four hundred people to one office is a shape the Gatehouse has refused before, and the shape it refuses is the shape a severance writ has.** He is a bridgewright and he cannot see it, because he is not looking at a structure; he is looking at a document, and the load path of a document is made of other documents, and the load goes into the weakest one, and the weakest one is a copy of a copy in a counting house in a city that does not admit to the Crown that it exists. He had written that down in his own notebook, in the correct vocabulary, **four months earlier**, in Chapter 69, and had read it, and had gone on.

**The rite works for eleven weeks and then kills a man who is still alive.** Vaile is restored on the ninth week and softens in the ninth week, because the softening travels along the marks that are read most and he has been read four hundred times, in a hall, by a stranger from the north, in a list. And the second route goes, not because of the rite but because **the witness at the Ash end of the line was a person the county chose, and that person was struck, and the county did not replace him in time** — and the road does not open, and it does not fail, and nobody is to say afterwards that it failed, and the volume ends with a route that will not answer when it is called and a man four hundred miles away with no door.

**The last act is the three hands, and it is the county's plan and not his.** Ash breaks the re-reading up into eight villages and a market town, over nine days, by its own people, with no office reading anything and no stranger in the room. It is slower than the rite. It is slower because it was designed by a county that had been cut off from the world and not by a man with a bar. **The rollkeeper signs after two days and her reason is the volume's most professional sentence: a roll that can be wrong is not a weaker roll; it is a roll that has to be read twice, and a roll that has to be read twice cannot be read by one man in a morning.** The magistrate signs, and says in a room that the list was the only instrument he had and that he is glad to be shown a second one and sorry it took four years and a stranger. And Ash begins to **re-ground its own fired tiles**, which is the permanent, slow, unglamorous, expensive act, and which is the same motion the man from Merefen carried out of a third slip of a ferry yard ten months ago and put down on a clamp in a filing room, and neither of them says so to the other.

## 10. Concrete resolution

**Chapters 98–100, and the state at the close of the hundredth chapter.**

- **The fever is contained without erasing names.** The list is superseded by the county's own bench, in a room, with the physician's four-year-old finding read out and three hands named. Ash's dead stay Ash's. Two hundred and fifty-four fired tiles are being ground back into clay in Hearthmonth, at a cost, on a schedule, with a bench, and about thirteen men are still out of work and the number is written down.
- **The Roll of Persons is in three hands** — the rollkeeper's office at the assizes, the hand of the Ninebridge, and the press of a creditor's office in a city that does not admit to the Crown that it exists, entered as a transaction at a farthing of salt — and **no single office can add or remove a name, and a person restored is restored when two of the three agree, and a person removed is removed when two of the three agree otherwise, and the third can be wrong.**
- **Aren reaches Stage 2, the Second Door, by qualification and not by award**, in a filing room, with a witness, on the terms printed in the Office of Provincial Registration's hand: two routes, two carryovers of which he cannot tell which path gave him which, **one message and it must be a list, one object under a pound, and the second route does not answer when it is called.** The first-crossing loss of one verse's remembered pitch remains the only fixed melody cost in the book.
- **What he loses, in the body, and what nobody sees:** the exact sequence of an ordinary morning with Tovan Kest — six or seven things in an order, a particular Tuesday, eleven years of it — and he can do every one of them and cannot do them in that order and does not know the order, and **Tovan will not re-teach him his own brother, and says so in a yard, in his own words, and it is the volume's Tovan beat and the reason the estrangement is not healed and is not going to be healed in this volume.**
- **What he hides and what it costs:** he conceals the cost of the Second Door crossing from Mara Vey, and she finds it with a date — two path dates given in the main-world frame, in one morning, in a working room — and **she does not ask, because she promised in Fallowmonth that she would not, and the not asking is the breach, and the reason she writes down is that the not asking has changed from a protection into a wound.** Working trust broken. Not repaired, and not to be repaired in Volume 03.
- **The second severance, four years later, in a county no one in Merefen could name:** the index line carries **press four, die eleven, and a later impression of the season**, and the reader can check it and Aren cannot, because the index has no name and the register does not leave the city. **Alis Merrow enters the signing office's name into a record of his own, in his own counting house, under his own law, at his own cost, and says in a hall that a creditor's press is the worst place in the world to keep a roll of persons and the only place where nobody can quietly take it out of the book, and asks that the argument be minuted and the minute put where a Crown's man will find it in about nine years.** It is the first document in the series held by a person in a path. **The name does not reach a court in this volume.** The two halves are put side by side in **Chapter 894.**
- **Halven's shield stands.** The endorsement is not withdrawn, because the office that gave it has no procedure for withdrawing an endorsement. The disclosure is in the Archive as a copy, with his reason and the hour, and no opinion. **Nobody is charged and he keeps the post.** He is later called back to testify, in Volume 11, and accepts restitution and archive labour, and is barred from office for ever, and the reason he can be is that there is one document in the world in which it says he did it.
- **The Crown's instruments in this county, at the close of the hundredth chapter:** the two found lawful on the ninth of Fallowmonth and never mentioned again in that court; the instruction of the ninth of Longlight still in force and superseded by nothing; the third writ, on the reeve's records, discharged; the provincial ledger with **absent** against the ninth of Greenmonth; and **two dated forms from the Office of Provincial Registration with a figure in each and no name in either.** The count went from four descriptions to eleven, and from eleven to a figure for the county, and **no name has been written down in any Crown's hand since the twenty-seventh of Fallowmonth, and that is the volume's political victory, and it is also how the Crown got further in six months than it got in eleven years.**
- **The bell is on the Old Toll bridge with a band of forty-one rivets and a dull red seam in it,** rung on a named day by a boy and a ferrywright and, from the ninth of Greenmonth, sometimes by a tile-cutter four hundred miles off. **It is not Aren's and he does not ring it.**
- **The eighth ruled line is still empty**, and the brine weights are still cited and not filed, and the two day-book lines about them are a year apart and are read together, and the roll built out of them is Chapter 892's.

## 11. Next-volume question

**Why are the Culling Order's records concentrated around agricultural oaths?**

In concrete form, and stated in the volume's last chapter rather than in a document: the disposition schedule that came out of Halven's ledger runs, in its county column, to eleven other counties, and eight of them are **counties with a boundary oath on a stone** — a long field, a boundary, a season, a stone renewed in public. The schedule's own county column is the only place in the series where the pattern can be seen, and it can be seen by exactly two people: a registrar who sold the book and a keeper who has a copy of a page of it, and neither of them can say what an oath has to do with a severance. **The next volume is a border oath in the Hearth Marches killing the soil and trapping thousands of farmers in a path whose water route is failing, and a Marches council that refuses outside command, and Tovan Kest's bridge crew caught inside it.**

Three hooks leave the volume, in this order.

1. **The disposition schedule's county column, and eight counties with a stone in a field.** Held to `outline/series.md`'s Volume 3 question, and the reader is given it as an arithmetic pattern and not as a revelation.
2. **A form of the Office of Provincial Registration with a marginal instruction that manufactures a keeper and that nobody in this volume understood** — *where no office of keeper is found in the county schedule, the person having knowledge shall be entered under the column headed Keeper, and the entry shall not be construed as the creation of an office* — and Sabin Dain, who wrote the letters and will write more of them, and who in Volume 3 offers a road warden's rank and a sealed personal record in exchange for closing the eastern paths.
3. **A tile on a coping at Ninebridge, a hand's width, with a rope on it and no yoke, carrying the device of a hand laid flat on a book,** which nobody in the County of Ash knows what it is, and whose maker is dead, and which has been on that coping since before the roll was begun. **It is not explained in this volume and no character may explain it.** It is the oldest object in the book after the bell and the reader has been carrying it since Chapter 10.

## 12. Final chapter image

The ninth of Greenmonth, YR 313, a Wednesday, a market day, the fourth hour, the lowest water of the year. A road warden on the Sedge Reach deck with a tool bag, counting load points. ## 16. Continuity traps for this volume

1. **The numbers.** Four thousand is the Crown's figure for the city and stays the Crown's. The villages are seven hundred and twenty, by name, in the county's spelling, with `sum not stated` in the total line and an eighth ruled line that is the city's and **is still empty.** No chapter, no speech, no file and no sum may put them together. **The trust's fifty marks one shilling and sixpence and Aren's eleven marks and fourpence are never set beside either.**
2. **Hushwater.** Reach, in the path, is **near sixty** and counts ferry hands and hearths and not souls. Hushwater, erased in this county, is **eighty.** The two are never printed together, no character notices the gap, and nobody explains why the name is familiar. Held to Volume 09.
3. **The three Hushwater women are three women.** The woman who keeps the crossing book, Sera Quen the ferrywright, and the sett-cutter who cut setts from the age of nine are three people and must not be merged. **Naming the crossing-book keeper is a deliberate reversal of continuity item 88's silence, and the only reason is that Volume 02's far end needs a person who has been on the page since Chapter 23 refusing to let a record be copied, and the reader has earned her. She is named once and her name is used thereafter.**
4. **The words.** Path-side locals say steward, and a steward maintains; the corrupted Crown term is *private keeper*; the Ash word is **the hand**, and *custodian* stays unused in this volume. **No character in Volume 02 may be a custodian, because the word is the Chapter 03 plant and it is not ready.**
5. **The four carried corrections.** No glass in the Old Toll sill and no third seat; the road is a line and two hands; **Bellweather-Living's two stewards are Sera Quen and the man with the mallet and Aren is neither**; **the name of Edric Hunn is on a separate sheet under a separate number and is never on the same sheet in the same county as the claim.** All four are stated in `outline/volumes/volume-01-close.md` §4 and all four are load-bearing here, and the fourth is the one a later writer is most likely to undo by accident.
6. **No path is a reset.** Nobody in the County of Ash is a version of a main-world victim. The living and the erased are never added together. **The four hundred and fifty-six, the two thousand nine hundred and six, and about two thousand and a half people are three different quantities and are never summed, and the reason is the same reason Mara's second wall rule exists.**
7. **The Culling Order phrase.** Spent once in Volume 01, in a claimant's mouth in Chapter 12. **One further appearance in Volume 02, in Chapter 54, in Nell Tace's mouth, with no member attached to it, and nowhere else.** Sabin Dain is not a member of anything in this volume and uses no such phrase.
8. **No new final enemy.** Edric Hunn is the antagonist of the whole book. The Office of Provincial Registration is **an office**, and it is dangerous because it is doing its job.
9. **The mechanism is not explained.** Nobody learns why he can cross. The palm, the stance, the drift, and the fact that a paper sent to a place can be answered by a place are all facts on a page and none of them is a mechanism, and the volume's biggest temptation — the tile on the coping — is **explicitly left unexplained and must stay so.**
10. **Rell and the Fenn sisters.** Rell and Aren meet again **about a form**, and neither enjoys it. Lysa and Aldis Fenn appear in the same room twice and concede nothing to each other. Bram Ottery is **not** related to any other character in the case and is never staged beside Jos Rusk, and Jos Rusk and Pei Tarrow are not staged together.
11. **Chapter 10's column does not foot.** The volume-close audit found that the six lines of the trust's account in Chapter 10 add to **thirteen marks and four pence** and that the chapter states **eleven marks and four pence**; the column closes at eleven marks and four pence only if the cartage line reads **three shillings and sixpence** and not **two marks three shillings and sixpence**. **The prose is wrong and the close records the correction. The stated total, eleven marks and four pence, is canon and is used everywhere. No Volume 02 figure may be derived from that column by scaling it, and every Volume 02 money column is added in pence and footed before it goes on the page.**

**Do not drift further.** The Volume 01 ceiling was three weeks of error at twenty-one days of absence. Volume 02's error is sixty-seven days at eighty-eight days of absence, which is a ratio of about three-quarters of the absence and not a constant, and the ledger allows the drift to widen with each volume. **A later volume may widen it further; it may not silently make a Gatehouse warning wrong by months.** The warning itself has not changed and is not going to: it is three weeks, it is printed in a Crown office's hand, and **the Crown cannot say whose figure it is.**
