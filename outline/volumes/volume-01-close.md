## 1. What the volume was about

A man who could see a load path bought a cracked bell for two pence, rang it on the night the town expected him not to, and came back nine days later to a sealed storehouse, a lost market, a fever, and a road he could not shut. Over fifty chapters he tried to make that road mean something, and the book spent its whole length refusing him: the living city on the other side did not want to be restored, the office that had culled it had never been named by anybody, the one document that named it could not leave the city it was in, the bell could not be taken off its own bridge, and the man who had done the worst thing in the worst minute of his life turned out to be the only person in two countries who could hold the road open. What he learned is not that he is right. It is that a road is a line and two hands and a date, and a person, and the man in the middle of it is a tradesman, and the sentence that carries the volume is one a watch captain said to him on a step in the rain — *a roadmark is not a road* — which he repeated on a bridge in daylight and finished in a notebook: **there is nobody in this kingdom he could say it to, and that is not a mystery any more, that is a trade.** Bellweather-Living is left standing, sovereign, in debt, keeping its own dead, and it has thrown away its four-generation oath about a stone and written a narrower one about a book and a door. That is the volume. Everything else is the cost of it.

## 2. The volume's argument, as it landed

The thesis the book has been pressing since Chapter 4 is that a man who can cross believes that makes him the person who should decide. The volume's answer is that he is one of the things other people can hold.

**Where it is proved.** In Chapter 47, on a market day, under a loaded arch, with four men in a boat beneath him and about two thousand feet on the deck: he saw the load path, he put his own working into the span, and the force went into the second pier exactly as it should, because that is what a Bind is for, and nine feet of stone cracked in one line and a hull-hand of the cooperative was struck at the fourth rib and a stranger nobody in the city could account for was hurt across the back. He took it out in the second minute. The only sentence in the notebook about it is that he took it out not because it was going to fail but because it was his. The work was then done by nineteen men he had not met, by the older members of his own bench, by a creditor's counting house that sent its clerks and its oil and entered the cost in its own book, by a ferry cooperative whose book-keeper would not let anybody touch the crossing book, by a barge escort who came down the river with a crew he did not have to have, and by the city's own two stewards, who put their names at the foot of a sheet in an order and neither signed the other's line. The road opened with four sets of hands on the anchor and Aren in the middle of it translating, which is the only thing he was any use for on the night.

**Where it is proved a second time, differently.** In Chapter 49, four days later, in a filing room over a salt merchant, he put his left hand flat on a table, palm up, which he had not done in front of anybody in eleven years, and told one person the thing he could not explain, and she wrote down what he said and not one word more, entered it as his statement and not her observation, asked him one question and not two, and entered that the office was not going to ask again, and entered *why*.

**Where it is not proved.** Three places, and the volume is honest about all three.

1. **He still decides for people in Chapter 50.** He takes the marshal's money, tells the marshal what to do with the shield, argues the city's refusal in a room where the city is not present, and speaks for four hundred miles about a bench he has never sat on. He is refused in ninety seconds, and Lysa Fenn's correction — the second time she has said the same sentence in the same procedure and it was not procedural this time — is that a constitution is not a charter but a set of people who can say no without a court. He does not have them. He says so, out loud, in a room with a clerk in it: four people in a fen town, and it is going to take him the rest of his life. The volume ends with that on the record and does not pretend otherwise.
2. **He hides from the one person who is holding the documents.** The marshal's letter of the eighth of Longlight — eleven hundred words, accurate, no offer, one imperative sentence, no name in it but the sender's — stays in a locked room over a cooper with no chain of custody and never will have one, and Mara Vey has known he has it for four months and has never once asked. That is the volume's standing cost and it is unpaid.
3. **The second failure is not his.** A bench of nine renews an oath about a stone it cannot pay for and the stone comes down, and the thing that saves the city is a narrower oath written by a woman with a rule, on a drying floor, in six hours. He contributes one sentence to it and is given credit for refusing to promise, and the credit is a courtesy. The best sentence in the volume is not his.

**What it cost.** Nine days of main-world calendar for six path days on the first crossing, against a road-crews' saying that allows three. Nineteen path days for six of the main world on the second. Six months and twenty days in the other world against six days here, on the third, with a Gatehouse warning of three weeks that was out by three weeks. The remembered pitch of one verse of his mother's river song, from Mudmonth, and after the third crossing a second and larger hole: two notes become one, and the one that is left is the higher one, and the higher one is the one Ilyra used to end on, and he had it in his head for nine months and did not know it was there until about the fourth hour of the second of Fallowmonth in a room over a cooper with a fever on him. Nine feet of crack in the second pier of the great arch, a permanent scar in two worlds, a splintered pier, a hull-hand's rib, a stranger's back, two hundred pounds of setts standing in a bar in a city four hundred miles off. A bench seat and a wage refused in a hall in eleven seconds, on the grounds that a bench is a thing that is pointed at and not a thing that is pointed with. And the thing nobody in the county can name: eleven years of the official fever account repeated in four debriefs and twice to a man who was kind about it, and eleven days of field notes in the breast pocket of a coat, and a sworn account that puts both of those in his own hand under a document rather than over it, because if a man's account goes over a seal and a date a reader stops, and if it goes under, a reader has to find out why.

**The book-sized form of the same answer**, and the sentence Volume 02 has to take seriously enough to count people: *A road that cannot be refused is a road that will be used. Somebody has to be able to shut it, and I have been the somebody for eleven years, and I would like somebody in this room to tell me, now, today, with a clerk writing it down, that there is another somebody, and that he is not you.* And there was, and it was a carpenter, a watch captain, a woman with a press, a reeve, a bench of nine over a wharf, four people in a city four hundred miles off, a woman with a book on a north bank, and a woman with a rule and a bench order and a mark in a coping.

## 3. State of the world at the end of Chapter 50

Written in the spirit of the state block at the end of Batch 0001 in `state/batch-summaries.md`: what is on the page, with dates, and what is owed. Main world. The last week of Fallowmonth, YR 312. River at the low water of the year. Bridge season.

**The Old Toll crossing and its trust.** A narrower, older crossing, out of cart use for two generations, four hundred yards up the road from the Sedge Reach. **The trust is in abeyance**, so the crossing has no owner, and a crossing with no owner has no crew, and a crew is not an owner, and the owner is the thing that answers. The bench of the Merefen bridge guild resolved on the fifteenth of Longlight, **on a Saturday, on the reeve's calling, the Saturday minuted**, that the crossing is kept in repair out of the bench's own materials and the bench's own men's time, and not out of the trust, and not at the charge of any person holding tolls, and not on account of any person named in any paper of the Crown — and recorded that it is not the owner and does not claim to be. Eight hands and the reeve's carried it; Tovan Kest said the true thing and did not vote, being a party, for the second time in five months, and the clerk entered that. It was asked a second time on the twenty-first of Fallowmonth, the vote carried again, and the shortest minute in the roll says the bench has asked twice, has been refused once by a chest, and is going to ask again in the spring, and that the asking in the spring is not a new matter. **Maintenance: three marks and about six the campaign, twice a year, seven marks no farthing; the figure that goes in the book is four marks and six a year, because four goes in a book.** The chest had two marks in it. The tolls of the crossing are suspended by the Crown from the first of Longlight, and the toll-holder is Dace Millward, 61, holder by purchase from an abbey two years ago, who obeyed, said so, wanted the obedience on the chalk, and is owed a hearing like anybody else. **The fourth course of the near abutment is open the width of a hand, in register, and scarred.** There is no glass in this county and there will not be a third seat.

**Sedge Reach Bridge.** Four timber bays, the town's crossing, the structure the town knows him by, and the one thing in Merefen that is holding. The scour hole under the nose of the third pier was a bread loaf on the sixth of Mudmonth and a washtub on the twenty-seventh and the honest repair is eleven tons of stone and a fortnight with the road shut. The Sedge Reach bench mark was re-cut by hand in Mudmonth of YR 312 and is **a quarter of an inch low**; the mark has been re-cut by hand three times in thirty-five years, always downward, and every level in the county taken since is out in the direction that makes a deck look sounder. The figure is on the county's own sheet of the second of Longlight in a remarks column with a date, an hour and a witness, and the guild's man with the levels **still refuses the guild's roll** and his refusal is right: a thing that is ours is a thing we are answerable for and a thing we cannot be corrected in, so keep the figures you can be got at.

**The bridge guild, the reeve, and the watch.** The **Merefen bridge guild** is a town's craft body of bridgewrights and road-wardens holding the Old Toll crossing in municipal trust, keeping the scrap roll the bell's six-mark valuation came from, and wanting the cracked bell off its books before the anniversary. It is not the Bellweather Bridge Guild, has no contact with it, and no member of either appears in the other's world. **Reeve Aldis Fenn**, 30-ish, Lysa's elder sister, has been in the room nine years and has never been asked to look at a stone and not at a reason, and has wanted to vote on the guild's own matter for nine years and does not, and is now the independent witness at the Merefen end of a channel. **Captain Rell**, 50, holds the bell lawfully, laid it down lawfully and on no one's authority on the twenty-fourth of Fallowmonth, in daylight, in front of about three hundred people, with the chain of custody read out loud as he did it, and put four lines in a county file offering to be the man who takes the iron off the bridge if any officer above the county thinks the whole thing wrong. A commendation with a grey seal and the hand-laid-on-a-book device is nailed to his own watch-house wall with four nails, and he says he is not being honoured, he is being **picked**, and he has read it every morning since. The instruction of the ninth of Longlight, second hour, received on the tenth and carried out on the tenth, **names no law, requires no court, is in force, and has not been superseded**: the watch shall prevent contact with the object, and shall prevent the assembly of any body of this county in respect of the object, and shall report. He stood outside a guild-house door for eleven minutes on the second of Fallowmonth and a bench met inside, and **a bench and a watch entered the same hour in two books**, which is the first time the hole in the magistrate's finding has cost a town anything procedural. His sergeant was relieved of his post and of nine months and three weeks' accrual on the fourth of Greenmonth for a statement he wrote unasked; he is **Bram Ottery**, he is in Merefen, he is unemployed, and there is no promotion in it and there will not be one.

**The Bellweather Claims Archive, its four places, and its writ.** Two flood-safe rooms over a Merefen salt merchant, who is 68, has weighed salt for forty years, has a weighing book of his own in the room, asked for his second floor back in Longlight, told Mara three things in the worst order on purpose, and did not ask again. The office's rule is on the wall in a fair copy: an anchor is recorded on the word of a sworn tradesman, in his own hand, and not on a clerk's word, and not on a witness's word, and not on the word of a man who has an interest. The second rule, added on the second of Greenmonth: **no combined figure is entered in this office.** **Keeper: Mara Vey**, 29, four years, two claims entered against nobody in the year (the sixteenth of Greenmonth and the seventh of Fallowmonth) and eight claim numbers in four years. **Second keeper: Sarn Oyelaran**, 26, with a key and a warrant in the day-book for a third post, and she has written the one attestation nobody asked for, in her own hand, as a copyist. The writ of the ninth of Longlight, second hour, **a writ of enquiry into the custody of a record office, returnable the ninth of Rainmonth, does not want to succeed** — it wants the thirtieth day to arrive, and while it is stamped the office may not move, copy out, or part with any record it holds. The return was late; the writ was stamped at the second hour of the ninth because it was not in. The office answered on the twenty-eighth of Fallowmonth at the second hour, in a form, with no blank in it, listing nine hundred holdings and **one lending dated the twenty-second of Longlight that names a tradesman of Merefen**, and there is a copy in a county file two hundred miles off. **A second writ of the same kind is on the reeve's office; a third went down the county post on the ninth of Fallowmonth and nobody in Merefen knows what is on it.** A county clerk wrote a note about the reeve's records on a sheet of county paper, put the sheet in a drawer, and then told the reeve about it out loud in a corridor in front of two men, because he is a clerk who does not leave things in drawers. **Four places hold the chain, and they are four different kinds of holding:** the press (a custody), the toll-holder's chest (a lending, eleven sheets, the day and the hour in his own hand), the yard's week-book (**a fact and not a paper** — nothing that belongs to a man goes in this yard's book and only a thing set), and a salt merchant's brother's lock-up on a canal wharf in Harvestmonth, **entered as a transaction and not a favour, at a farthing of salt**, because a document left with a person is a thing a person can be arrested for and an office can only be closed. Not received. Not answered.* A warrant in that office's file names a woman without a name; the asking is dated the fourth of Greenmonth and unanswered. The provincial ledger has **absent** against the ninth of Greenmonth, certified by Petch on his own motion and not on a fee. Four **no reason given** in a guild roll in four months, and the clerk has begun writing the dates in the margin beside them and hopes somebody will find them all and read them together.

**The Old Toll bell.** Nine stone, split lip to shoulder, bronze gone green-black, bought at the Crown clearance for two pence on the first of Mudmonth, YR 312, as scrap bronze, out of a roll on which it was entered in **YR 302 as unclassified salvage with no city and no river written on it, in a bound order between two entries of YR 304, in a hand the same age as its neighbours** — the largest documentary hole in the case, and a copyist's attestation of it in a wrapper in Mara's press with the words *bound out of order, in a hand not of this roll* and no opinion. It is the **third bell** of Bellweather, the crossing-closed bell, cracked on the third stroke of the refusal on the night of the nineteenth of Mudmonth YR 301; it stands on two oak trundles under a tarred cloth in the founder's yard in the other world and on an iron cradle in Merefen, and Chapter 7 adjudicates that on the page in one plain sentence, and **nobody in this volume explains it and nobody may**. It was taken off the abutment at first light on the nineteenth of Mudmonth by four men and a cart off an order dated the sixteenth, and it lay in a storehouse by the fish weir, then in a locked room in the watch house, and **on the twenty-fourth of Fallowmonth a watch captain carried it back in daylight and laid it down on the Old Toll abutment, on the ground of a repair to a parapet ordered by a bench and a scrap-roll entry of the eleventh of Mudmonth, and the town reclaimed its own iron on its own authority.** The band is the town's work — iron off a gate-post at the end of Weir Street, six inches wide, forty-one rivets, set by the guild's man with the levels, who says *it'll hold as long as the crack does, and the crack is not a crack, it's a split*. The old split is re-sealed from **both sides at the same hour** on the twenty-fourth with a dull red oathlight by a Bellweather-Living steward's hands four hundred miles off, because that is the one working in that city that cannot be done by a person on their own, and the north man was not on the deck and was not going to be. **Nothing in the bell breaks again in this volume.** It is rung on a named day by a boy of fifteen on the near abutment, the steward's apprentice at the Merefen end, and by a woman with iron dust in the creases of her hands on the far end, **because that is the arrangement, and it is not Aren's and he does not ring it.** About nine people in a lane off the Sedge Reach approach heard the two of them together on the twenty-ninth and stayed for the sound.

**The road itself.** **A line and two hands, a date, and a witness at each end who is not the person keeping the road. If the witness is not there the road does not open, and it does not fail, and nobody is to say afterwards that it failed.** That is Sera Quen's sentence and it is the volume's whole argument in one line. The channel runs on a named day: a Merefen steward (Tovan Kest, carpenter, who is not the man who cut the seat and does not want to be) and a Bellweather-Living steward (Sera Quen, ferrywright, whose consent is hers and not a bench's and cannot be argued with in a hall), with an independent witness at each end — **Reeve Aldis Fenn at the Merefen end, and the toll-clerk on the north steps at the far end, who refused twice and gave the reason the second time: a man you know is not a witness; a man you have never met is.** The day is written at the head of a page in a book on the north bank, in the roll of the Merefen bridge guild, in the day-book of a filing room over a salt merchant, and in two slates. The first thing across it was a dispute about nine tons of paving stone, argued in two languages for eleven minutes and settled on line ninety-one of a crossing book: *a cart on wheels is not a man, and a man who comes up the steps is not a cart.* It is a dispute and not a treaty. About nine people in Merefen were told afterwards that it was about a load of paving stone and did not believe it and were right not to.

**Aren Kest.** Thirty-two. **Stage 1, the First Stitch, by qualification and not by award.** One open route. **One carryover, and it is a hand's habit of reading a handrail in the dark, and it is not a craft motion and it is not a working.** A **permanent road-marked left palm**, warm four times in five months, and never once in Mudmonth, and on a day that is not the twenty-sixth of Longlight and not the second of Fallowmonth. **A return that must be prepared before every crossing by two hands, a date, and a witness at each end who is not the person keeping the road, or it does not open.** No ability to make the far side answer. A refused bench seat and a refused wage in Bellweather-Living. **No office in this county.** A copying post at three shillings a week he took on the sixteenth of Fallowmonth and refused a schedule of forty-one inspections whose every line carries a ruled column headed *Keeper*, and the reason is written out in four dated lines in the yard's book, in the guild's roll, and in a copy to the county surveyor. **He is on the guild roll, and a road warden is elected in this room in Frostmonth.** Eleven marks and four pence of guild money owed by a trust in abeyance, two marks and three quarters at eight pence a day on a cutting contract, a bond for six against a tool-chest worth three and a half, carried. She has a second form gone to the county council with a printed space under *Function of the office* that she filled with the founding's own words and left the summary empty.

**Tovan Kest.** Twenty-five. Owner of the Merefen crews and the yard. **He is the Merefen steward of the channel, and he is not the man who cut the seat and does not want to be**, and the woman on the north bank entered that in her book in case anybody comes in a year and says the north sent somebody. The yard's rule, invented in a gateway in Greenmonth in front of four men and tested in the long light of the second of Fallowmonth: **nothing that belongs to a man goes in this yard's book and only a thing set.** He entered his brother's unasked handrail in the yard's book, in his own hand, with a date, an hour and a rule over it, and has not told him and is not going to. He said the true thing in a public room three times in five months and did not vote once, and the third time the true thing was about himself: *I have said no to a road three times in that room and I have never once said no to my brother, and he knows the difference, and it is the worst thing about me and it is not in any book.* He is not a member of the bench and has not been since he was twenty-one. **Lysa Fenn and Reeve Aldis Fenn.** Two minutes in the same county court, four days apart, and neither of them has conceded a point. **Lysa**, 30, magistrate of Merefen, has made herself a party on the record four times this year and has been right about it every time. **Captain Rell.** See above. He is right on every count and the reader is on his side and he is still the man who took the bell, and **they will meet again and it will be about a form, and neither of them is going to enjoy it.**

**Dace Millward.** 61, creditor in this town thirty-one years, holder of the Old Toll tolls for two years by purchase from an abbey. ## 4. The four corrections Volume 02 must not undo

These are the ones a reader would notice, and they are on the page, and they are not the writer's to revisit.

1. **The two-disc seat is gone and there is no third.** The seat in the fourth course took its third use at the fourth hour of the afternoon of the twenty-sixth of Longlight, the pin went, the bed of lime was crushed to gravel, and a wagoner prised the two used discs out on the twenty-seventh and carted them away and said they were broken, and Tovan Kest believed him **because they were broken.** The maker wrote a page in his own hand, gave it away, would not charge for it, and said two is easy and three is not a number he would sell. **The Old Toll anchor from the twenty-sixth of Longlight onward is a dressed stone and forty yards of tarred hemp through the abutment on a leather splice cut out of a cooper's bin**, set on a bed of lime and oak dust, put in by four men of a yard on a work of the bench and not out of any trust, witnessed by a reeve who was asked to witness the setting of a thing and not the purpose of it. 2. **The road is a line and two hands, not a piece of glass.** He said it in a notebook in Longlight and it is the sentence the whole of Block 5 turns on: *the seat is not the road. I have been treating a piece of glass in a stone as though it were a road for four months and it is not one. A road is two ends and a date and a person at each end who has agreed to be there, and everything in the middle is a way of holding the two ends to their promise.* The opening was a **line of tarred hemp** with a hand at each end and a witness at each end who was not the steward, and it was opened at the fourth hour of the first of Fallowmonth and came out of a stone in a parapet in a fen and not out of him, and about two thousand people were on the other side and none of them knew he was under it and none of them needed to.
3. **Bellweather-Living's two stewards are Sera Quen and the man with the mallet, and Aren is neither.** The bench-clerk's question — *the oath says the stewards of the crossing shall answer for it and shall answer first, and who is the steward of this crossing?* — was asked at the ninth hour of the thirtieth of Longlight in a room with five men in it and answered at the ninth hour of the first of Fallowmonth by two people putting their names at the foot of a sheet, **in an order, neither signing the other's line**, and the clerk entered both hours and read them back. The toll-taker at the north steps is the witness and is not the steward. 4. **The name is on a separate sheet under a separate number.** Edric Hunn entered the day-book of the Bellweather Claims Archive on the seventh of Fallowmonth at the eleventh hour, on the office's own account, against no person and against no office, on a sheet bearing a separate number from the claim's number and no other mark of identification, with no opinion attached and none to be attached by any court that may read it, and with the reason written once and nowhere else. The name is separately in a county file in a sentence, in a clerk's hand, in a room with a clerk in it, on his own request, and **the two have never been on the same sheet in the same county.** A court that wants to read them together may; a clerk may not; and the keeper's signature is on both.

### 5.4 The audit's findings, stated plainly

1. **The volume made almost every promise and spent one it should not have spent early, which is a credit.** The brine weights are the only ending-plant that a volume-01 writer was tempted by four separate times and declined four times, and the two day-book lines four lines apart are the thread that survives.
2. **The Marshalled letter is the best document in the volume and it is inert.** It has no chain of custody, cannot have one, and its entire function at the end of the volume is to be a thing nobody can lodge. It is also the document the final trial will need. **This is a structural debt, not a defect, and Volume 02 is the first volume in which it can start to accrue interest.**
3. **The volume's pattern is legible by Chapter 23 and that is a real cost to the reader.** Being the only man in the room who can read the thing is a liability five times in fifty chapters, and the reader is ahead of the plot for the last ten of them.
4. **Eleven of the most consequential people in the volume are addressed by trade and none of them by name.** See §7.
5. **The road never takes a life.** Two men are hurt on the arch on the first of Fallowmonth and both live and are carried to the abbey. The volume's own thesis — that a promise about a stone kills people — is asserted four times and cashed in a crack.
6. **The Crown escalates in paper.** A seizure, then a writ, then a returnable order, then an instruction, then an offer. Five instruments, five different documents, and the office never has to change tactic inside the volume. It changes tactic between volumes and the reader has to be shown the tactic changing.
7. **The hundred and twenty-second word problem: the Crown's escalation is correct and the volume's counter is always procedural.** Every counter-move the book made this year was answered by a finding, a minute, an endorsement, a form or a refusal. Four of those five are the Crown's own instruments turned around, which is the volume's best idea, and one of them — the bell coming back onto the bridge on the town's own authority — is a move the Crown did not anticipate. **Volume 02 must be won by a mechanism that is not a document.**
8. **The volume's smallest number does not add up, and it is the one the whole book is built on.** The trust's account in Chapter 10 is six lines. Added in pence they come to **six hundred and twenty-eight pence — thirteen marks and four pence.** The chapter states the bill as **eleven marks and four pence**, and Lysa Fenn reads that figure aloud as the medicine she has, and Mara Vey calls it the most honest page in the office, and eleven marks and four pence has been quoted in four state files, in the continuity record and in a guild roll ever since. The column closes at eleven marks and four pence **only if the cartage line reads three shillings and sixpence and not two marks three shillings and sixpence** — a mark has been duplicated onto it. **The prose is wrong. The stated total is canon and is what the book says. This close records the finding and does not change a chapter:** the correction is a one-mark one on a single line, it is invisible to a reader, and the value of leaving it is that the figure the book quotes out loud in four places stays the figure the book quotes out loud in four places. **What Volume 02 must do is not scale that column.** A volume that costs Aren's absence in main-world days cannot arrive at its number by multiplying Volume 01's. Every Volume 02 money column is added in pence and footed before it goes on the page, and the foot is checked against the total that is said aloud.

## 6. The three hooks, as documents

In the order `outline/volume-01.md` gives them.

**1. A claimant's form at the Archive about a fever in the eastern counties, with a list of names to be removed from the public roll.**
*Document:* a claimant's form, one sheet, in the outer room of the Bellweather Claims Archive, received on a day in Fallowmonth YR 312 and entered in the day-book **against no person, with the hour**. *Owner:* **Mara Vey**, keeper, by the office's own rule that a document received is entered whoever brought it. *What is on it:* a fever in the eastern counties that has already produced a list of names to be proposed for removal from the public roll, and the claimant's own name on that list, and the claimant's contention that the list is the fever's instrument and not its remedy. *Why it matters:* **it is the first thing to reach that office in eleven years on somebody else's initiative, and it is not this office's war.** It arrived because a person went up a stair with a paper, not because anybody sent for it. Nobody in Merefen has asked for it and nobody will.

**2. A registrar quoting a fee for a missing-anchor ledger that should not exist.**
*Document:* the press register of the Crown registry's fourth press, a shelf register, in which the volume *Anchors, schedules and dispositions, Alder Vale* is entered among two hundred and nine other volumes and rolls and is **not on its shelf**, and against it, in the press register's own hand, **the nineteenth of Mudmonth, YR 301. Removed.** — no name, no office, no authority, no destination, no fee, no initial. *Owner:* the **Office of Provincial Registration** holds the register; **Registrar Halven** keeps the register and is the man who quoted Mara four shillings in Hearthmonth for a dated request and told her the gap unasked. *Status:* the copy of the shelf register, attested by **Sarn Oyelaran** in her own hand as a copyist with no opinion, is in a wrapper in the press. An application for the production of records, resting on two facts and no speech, is entered against nobody. **The ledger is still missing. The removal is still undated by any name. And Halven is the only man alive who knows both.**

**3. The writ register's own index, showing a second severance authorization in the same hand, four years later, in a county no one in Merefen can name.**
*Document:* the index of a four-hundred-leaf, five-column outgoing writ register kept by the toll houses of a city in a jurisdiction that does not admit to the Crown that it exists. Nine entries. Eight of Alder Vale. **One dated YR 305, in the same hand, in a county that no map in Merefen carries.** *Owner:* **Alis Merrow**, toll-house steward, who is alive, has a bundle of string, has never been asked, and has spent six months and twenty days watching a bench refuse to read a page of his own book. *Visibility, and this is the new fact:* the line came south inside the fair copy of a fair copy that crossed on the first of Fallowmonth, because a clerk copies an index with a line — a word without a sentence is not a line. It is in a claim in a filing room over a salt merchant; the copy of the copy is in the counting house's own press in a city four hundred miles off; the arrest entered against a stranger for saying a motive is in the same office's book; and **the office that has never produced its own authority has asked, in writing, for the list of those to whom this road warden has spoken.** The visibility of that index has gone from nobody to **four people in two countries.**

And the thing underneath all three, which is the volume's real hand-off: **Edric Hunn's office has stopped asking Aren whether he will stop. It has started asking who else he has told, and the answer to that is a form with a column headed *Keeper* in an office that now has a woman's name in a provincial file, and the refusal is a minute in a county court two hundred miles off.**

## 7. What Volume 01 did badly, and what Volume 02 inherits on purpose

Nine things. The first two are defects of the prose, the next four are defects of the structure, the last three are things to keep.

**1. The apparatus chapters are the longest chapters and they are the least alive.** Chapters 19 and 20 run to 8,610 and 8,070 words; Chapter 40 is 10,585 and Chapter 35 carries the batch's longest single speech. Four of the ten longest chapters in the volume are a filing room, a yard, a court, or a bench minute, and in all four of them the thing that makes the book the book is a *person doing a trade* and the thing that makes it long is a *taxonomy*. The three-instrument taxonomy, the currency, the calendar, the four-part working, the distinction between a custody, a lending and a fact — every one of these is stated in full at least twice. **Correct in Volume 02: the same machinery, stated once, and then carried by hand.** A kiln, a tile, a firing, a third shift, a wage on a Friday. If a rule has already been given to the reader, a later chapter shows it being used and being got wrong.

**2. The prose has one rhythm, and it is the rhythm of a man being told he is wrong.** Approximately thirty chapters end on a line in which somebody corrects him, refuses him, or prices him. It is the best rhythm in the book and it is the same rhythm thirty times. A reader learns it by Chapter 15 and is reading ahead of the prose for the remaining thirty-five chapters. **Correct in Volume 02: the corrections have to come from more directions, and at least one of them has to be corrected in public by the person who made it.** Sarn Oyelaran has been the person who would do it and has been given the refusal twice; give her a correction and let it stand.

**3. Eleven consequential people are addressed by trade and none of them by name**, and the volume is built on a refusal to inflate the cast that is right in principle and overdone in execution. Named in prose in Volume 01: Tovan Kest, Mara Vey, Wenna Koll, Lysa Fenn, Captain Rell, Sera Quen, Jos Rusk, Bram Ottery (named in the state record and never on the page), Halven, Pei Tarrow, Sarn Oyelaran, Ilvane Quarren, Hollis Dree, Alis Merrow, Captain Ruen, Reeve Aldis Fenn, Dace Millward, Iven Sarrat, Rowan Petch, Harl Mounce. Everything else with weight is a trade: the man with the mallet, the salt merchant, the sett-cutter, the toll-clerk on the north steps, the eel-man of the wharf road, the woman who keeps the Hushwater crossing book, the woman on Ferry Lane, the woman on the gallery, the guild's man with the levels, the guild clerk, the bench-clerk, the toll-clerk, the county clerk, the crown clerk, the carrier, the factor, the toll-man on the north steps, the man at the tanner's, the light's keeper at the tower. **Nineteen faces the reader is asked to hold and cannot name.** The volume's intimacy comes from it and so does its fog. **Correct in Volume 02: name a few of the ones the book has already earned** — the Hushwater crossing-book keeper first, because she has been on the page since Chapter 23 refusing to let a record be copied, and she is the most important unnamed person in the manuscript.

**4. Being right is always the beginning of being wrong, five times, and the reader can feel the shape by Chapter 23.** Chapter 6 (the hull), Chapter 23 (the marking book), Chapter 30 (the account), Chapter 42 (the scour), Chapter 45 (the bench seat), Chapter 47 (the Bind). The thesis is correct and the execution has made it a metronome. **Correct in Volume 02: he must be right about something, and being right must be *sufficient*, and the cost must fall on somebody who is not the man who was right.** Chapter 47 gave him the failure; nothing in the volume gave him a success that cost somebody else something. Volume 02 should.

**5. The drift is one of the volume's best ideas and it is introduced in Chapter 47, in a notebook, five chapters before the end.** A reader who has not read the notebook has not been told. **Correct in Volume 02: the terms of the Second Door are on the page in Block 1, in a form, in an office's hand, with a figure in the form that is wrong, and nobody in the room can find out whose figure it is.**

**6. The road never takes a life, so the book's thesis is asserted and never cashed.** Two men hurt on the first of Fallowmonth; both live. A hull-hand keeps a bad joint. The stranger is carried to the abbey and his name goes into a book. The narrower oath releases a *class* of promise. That restraint is the right instinct and the wrong size for a fifty-chapter volume that ends with a bridge going down. **Correct in Volume 02: somebody is lost, and not by an arch and not by dying.** The correct catastrophe for this book is a person who is still alive and can no longer be got back into themselves, and a witness with a book who has to write down that it happened, and no instrument anywhere in the county that will do anything about it.

**7. Nobody in the volume is out of work except Bram Ottery, and he is punished for being right.** Every working man in the book is competent, loyal and slightly stoic, and the bench's money problem is solved four separate times by the same answer, which is that a bench votes. **Correct in Volume 02: the failure is a labour failure, and there is no bench in the next volume to vote.** A county that fires its people into tiles has an employer problem, and employers close.

**8. Two threads were opened and then abandoned to their own schedules, and both of them are now very long.** **Hushwater** is raised four times, answered zero times, and is held until Volume 09 — a nine-volume wait on a hint the reader can already solve. The discipline is right and the *frequency* is wrong: a book that trains its reader to find gaps and then withholds one of them for nine volumes is spending the reader's attention. **Hushwater must not be opened in Volume 02. But one other gap must be opened halfway and left,** so that the reader is not being told, once a volume, that the book is keeping score.

**9. The Crown's instruments are all documents, and the volume's counter-moves are all documents, and the ending is won by a document and a bell.** That is right for this volume and wrong as a permanent condition. **Inherit on purpose:** the refusal, the "not here," the distinction between a road that does not open and a road that fails, the refusal to total, the refusal to file the brine weights, the refusal to write the one sentence the whole case wants, the four different kinds of holding, the eight-inch ring measured twice a year and never compared, and the ordinary labour that everything is built on. **Correct on purpose:** the register. The volume's most portable sentence is *a record is only a record if a person can be got at for it*, and the volume made that argument entirely through people refusing to give things away. Volume 02 has to make the same argument through a record that is **given away lawfully, for money, on a form, with a receipt** — because that is what is actually happening two hundred miles off, and the volume was too close to its own people to see it.

## 8. What Volume 02 must not do

A short list, in one place, because the next three volumes will be written against this document.

1. **Do not total the four thousand with the seven hundred and twenty.** Four thousand is the Crown's figure for the city and stays the Crown's. The villages are seven hundred and twenty, named, in the county's spelling, with `sum not stated` in the total line and an eighth ruled line that is the city's and is still empty. **In Volume 02 that line is a decision made twice and written down twice, and it stays empty.** No file, no chapter, no speech, no sum.
2. **Do not print Hushwater Reach's near sixty and Hushwater's eighty together,** and do not let any character notice that the names match. Held for Volume 09.
3. **Do not explain the mechanism.** Nobody in Volume 01 learned why he can cross and nobody learns it in Volume 02. The private-keeper clause may be **named in an office's hand on a printed form** and nobody may understand it. The roadmark may warm, and a hand gesture may come from one path or the other, and the palm may be a trade and not a mystery. Ilyra is not mentioned, not named, and not guessed at; her fate is untouched.
4. **Do not add a melody cost.** The first-crossing loss of one verse's remembered pitch remains the only fixed melody cost so far. Volume 02's costs are a morning, a hand gesture, and a date in the wrong calendar.
5. **Do not name a Gatehouse Order member as one.** Sabin Dain enters in Volume 02 as a named person with a want that has nothing to do with Aren and **is not a member of anything.** The phrase "Culling Order" has been spent once in Volume 01, in a claimant's mouth in Chapter 12; it may appear **once more in Volume 02, in a document or a claimant's mouth, with no member attached to it**, and nowhere else.
6. **Do not introduce a new final enemy.** Edric Hunn is the antagonist of the whole book. He is always several steps ahead in knowledge and one step behind in values, and he is never surprised.
7. **Do not let Aren be a steward, a bench, a custodian, or a keeper of anything,** and do not use the word *custodian* in Volume 02. See §4.
8. **Do not put glass back in the Old Toll sill.** The two-disc seat is gone. The road is a line and two hands.
9. **Do not record Edric Hunn as a Volume 02 name.** He is first named in `chapter-0026.md` and he is a signature on the Crown's letter from Chapter 38. What Chapter 50 gave him is **his first page and his first room**, in which he was refused in ninety seconds and told that he had been right and cheap for the first time in a year. Five files asserted "one new name in Batch 0005" and all five were wrong; that correction is in continuity item 124 and it stays corrected.
10. **Do not touch `state/phase-ledger.json`, `scripts/`, `.github/`, `.opencode/`, `AGENTS.md`, `PHASE_SYSTEM.md`, `REPO_PLAN.md`, `OUTLINE_GUIDE.md`, or `opencode.json`,** and do not create a second prompt for this phase. The Batch 0001–0005 prompt directories keep their prompts; the controller puts the `.done` marker on them.
