# Chapter 0272

**The twenty-eighth day of Greenmonth, YR 316, a Saturday, and a market day, and the third hour to the ninth hour, and about forty men on a reach four hundred yards down a lane from a market in a town of about four thousand people on a river with the road open over it and the salt coming over the first bay, and the fourth bay finished and pointed on the Thursday before, and a long room over a wharf where a bench of nine sits at the seventh hour and on nothing else in the week, and a yard on a street four hundred yards from that wharf with about nine men in it, and about four hundred people in that town who do not know that the fourth bay is finished and that a bench of nine has been asked nothing and that a man of thirty-two with a limp was in three rooms on that Saturday and was asked nothing in any of them.**

The fourth bay is finished. The pointing went in on the Thursday and the last of it was pointed on the Friday morning and the man of fifty and a half who does the arches and the pointing and does not do the laying put his own hand flat on the last of it at about the seventh hour and went down the ladder and did not say anything about it, which is the ordinary end of a piece of work and which about nine men in that yard noticed.

There is a joint on the parapet of that bay that is a foot and a half short, and it has been short since the man who began it went home at the fourth hour one day about three weeks ago and has not come back, and nobody in that yard measured it and nobody in this county has measured it and a clerk of a bench of nine was asked about it once and said that a clerk cannot measure a thing.

**“He was right, too,” the man of fifty and a half said, when it was raised. “I have thought about it since and he was right. There is nothing in that word clerk that lets a man hold a rule. If I had a rule in my hand at the seventh hour on a Saturday nobody would be able to do anything at all in this county for a week.”**

The boy of fifteen was at the bench-board with a lime tub and a rule and a bill, and he copies the empty space under a number as well as the number, and there has been no bill on that board for about two months and he has the bill under his arm and is not going to be given one and is not the boy who is waiting for a bill.

**“You are going to want a number for that joint,” the boy said.**

**“There is no number for that joint,” the man of fifty and a half said.**

**“There is a number for everything in this yard,” the boy said, “and the space under it is the widest of the four, and I have copied it eleven times and I have never once been told what goes in it.”**

**“Nothing goes in it,” the man of fifty and a half said. “That is what the space is for.”**

The boy wrote nothing under it, and about four men in that yard had watched him not write it and about nine had not been watching, and a yard of forty men is a place where a boy can be right about a form and be the only one in it who is.

---

A carter of thirty with a barrow and a waggon of his own came up the lane at about the fourth hour with nine sacks of salt in the barrow and did not stop at the crossing and did not look at either end of it, and he has said two things out loud in two days to nobody in the last season and would not mind a toll and is not going to say so to anybody either.

A man of about nineteen came down the lane carrying a hod and carrying it badly and was told to hold another man's hod and held it for about an hour and did not know why and did not ask. He is going to be a foreman before he is thirty and everybody in that yard knows it and nobody is going to say it to him.

He has an elder sister of twenty-two in a house two streets off and has not said one word about her to anybody in that lane in about a year, and there is no room in this county where a man can stand beside a relation for the length of a working day and neither of them has to say one word about it, and nobody in that yard has ever worked out why that is so and nobody there is going to ask.

“**You have got the left end wrong,**” he said to the boy of fifteen, about an hour into the hour he was holding the other hod.

“**The left end is the left end,**” the boy said.

“**It is the left end and it is the wrong way up,**” the man of about nineteen said, and put it down the other way round, and went back to his own hod, and the boy of fifteen copied the number again because a number that is the right way up is the same number and a space is a space whichever way the stone goes.

---

The bell goes at the fourth hour and a half and has gone there every day for longer than anybody in this county can remember, and about four hundred people in this town have heard it about four hundred times and have never once asked who keeps it, and nobody in this county can answer, and that is the finding and not a gap, and a bell rung by two people who are neither paid for it nor members of it is a bell nobody in this county can be asked about, and that is why it is rung.

The man of fifty and a half was asked about it once on a Monday and has not been asked since.

**“The boy rings it,” he said, “and the carpenter rings it, and that is the answer, and I have given that answer as many times as anybody has wanted it and I would give it again.”**

“**That is not an answer,**” the man of about twenty-six setting a course said.

**“It is the answer and it is the same answer every time and I have been giving it since the spring,” the man of fifty and a half said. “If you have a better one, say it now and I will stop saying mine and I will not be sorry.”**

The man of about twenty-six did not have a better one and said so, and the man of fifty and a half went back to his ladder, and about nine men in that yard had heard the two of them do that four times in about two months and none of them had ever said anything about it to either of them.

---

And the yard on Weir Street had nine men in it on that Saturday and a bench-board four feet off the ground and about nine feet long, and a press in a room that belongs to the yard and cannot be served with anything, and four sheets of printed paper in that press that have not been sent to anybody and are not going to be.

The yard's rule is that nothing that belongs to a man goes in that yard's book and only a thing set. The owner of it is twenty-five and he was in the yard that morning and he did not say one word about the press and about four of the nine men noticed that and about four did not.

A man of about sixty was packing lime at the end of the yard in the open, and he has been at that end of that yard for about nine years and is not on the yard's book, and about four of the nine men in that yard have worked out that the lime he packs is not the lime that yard bought and about four have not, and nobody in that yard has ever asked him where it comes from and he has never said.

He came in out of the weather at about the fifth hour with a lid on the heap and stood at the wall with his back to about nine men.

“It is the same lid,” he said, to nobody, “that was on it in Thawmonth and it is the same lid that was on it in Mudmonth and there is a crack in it in the same place and if a county wanted to know how long a thing lasts it could come and look at the lid.”

Nobody answered him. Nine men in a yard have heard a man talk to a lid about four hundred times and not one of them has ever asked him what he is doing at the end of their yard, and that is the whole of what is known about the man who packs the lime.

A door on a lane forty feet from that wagon arch was open. It is a door on a lane and it has been open since the spring and the room behind it holds two presses, one of which can be served with anything and one of which is four hundred yards up that street and cannot. Nobody in that lane has said anything about the door being open and nobody in that yard has said anything about the door being open and a man of thirty-two with a limp went up that lane at about the sixth hour and looked at it and did not go in, and there is no column in that lane for a door that is open.

---

**And the bench of nine sat at the seventh hour in the long room over the wharf, on a Saturday, and was asked nothing, and the count on the page under that stair is eight and it was eight before it and it is eight after it.**

A bench of nine has been asked eight things in nineteen years. Four of them were said yes to and three of them were said no to for a reason and one of them was brought up two flights of stairs and not put. The number is on a page under the stair in that room and nobody in that room has ever entered it on a form, and there is no form in this county that a count of askings goes on.

A clerk of about sixty kept the minute and the minute is nineteen years old in its binding and has about four hundred entries in it and a great many of them are about salt.

The man of forty-seven was on the bench because a man in a market asked who was going and he put his hand up, and he is not on a trade and has not been since the year before last, and he is paid fourpence a week out of a private box and not out of the chest, and he does not hold the reach or the crossing or anything else in this county, and he has not used his vote since the day he was elected and did not use it then.

He said nothing for two hours. Nobody asked him anything for two hours. About four people in that room have said since that a man who has said the same four things five times in a year to a man with a stick and to a yard wall and to a bench-board has now said nothing at all in a room with nine of his own in it, and that the nothing took longer than the four things have ever taken, and that no one in that room has worked out which of the two is the harder.

The reeve sat at the end nearest the door and did not vote on the guild's own matter, which she has not done for nine years, and the magistrate sat at the other end and had a construction ready that she has had for about a year and did not use, and there is nobody in this county she can ask to write down that she did not use it.

**“The first of Longlight is Tuesday,” the clerk said, at about the eighth hour, to the room and not to anybody in it. “I am four miles up the road at the fourth hour and I am taking the other book. I am telling you because there are about four hundred people in this town and about nine of them are in this room, and one of you may want to come, and I am not asking any of you.”**

“**I will be there,**” the man of thirty-two with a limp said.

The clerk wrote nothing down about that, and it was the only thing said in that room between the seventh hour and the ninth hour that anybody would have called a decision, and a man of thirty-two with a limp does not vote on the bench of nine and has not used his vote since the day he was elected, and a Saturday in a market morning is a day a bench says things, and this one said one thing and it was not said by the bench.

The bench rose at about the ninth hour. The lane was wet. The salt was coming over the first bay for the eighth week running and the tide was under the wharf, and about four hundred people in a town of about four thousand people on a river went to bed that night not knowing that a stone in a hedge four miles up their own road was going to be said over on Tuesday, and that a man with a stick had said he would be there, and that nobody had asked him.
