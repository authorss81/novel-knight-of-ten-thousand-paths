# Chapter 0292

**The thirteenth day of Harvestmonth, YR 316, a Thursday, and a market day, and the second hour and the fifth hour and the seventh hour, and about nine sills in one lane in a town of about four thousand people on a river, and a toll house four hundred yards up the county's own road, and two rooms over a weigh-house at the end of a lane with a shelf in them, and about four hundred people in that town who do not know that nine sheets of a printed series went onto nine sills that morning, and that the stone that had been sitting on the ninth of those sills since the twenty-eighth day of a month in Longlight with nothing on it was put back down on top of a new one.**

He is a man of about thirty-one and there are about two thousand sheets of that office's own printed series behind him in six years of carrying them, and in none of the six years has anybody found out what stands in the four places on one, and he has said so three times in the open, once in a yard and once in a room and once in a market, in the voice a man uses for a price, and about four hundred people in this town have heard none of the three.

There is an order to the nine and it is not the order a quick man would use, and the order has been the same one for six years and it was not this one the year before last, and it has never been questioned by anybody and he has never offered it to anybody.

Nine doors in a wet week get nine sheets and a carrier who could get round them in a quarter of an hour takes the whole morning over them, and nobody in that lane has ever been asked why, and about four people in it could each say the reason out loud and none of them ever has.

---

A cresset by the first door, a step under the second shutter, the inside of the third door where it stood open, and a sill under the fourth. Four doors in about nine minutes and not one knuckle raised at any of them.

At the fifth there was a woman standing in the lane with a pail and she had been there before he came.

“**You are the second one this week,**” she said.

“**I am,**” he said.

“**I have told nine doors since Friday and I have not told anybody else and there is a room at the end of this lane and I have not told the room.**”

“**Then do not tell the room,**” the carrier said, and went on down the lane, and a woman who tells nine doors and not the room told the man carrying the satchel, and a carrier of about fifty has carried about two thousand sheets in six years and has been told one thing by about four hundred people in that county in six years and it was not that.

She took the sheet off the step at about the third hour and carried it down the lane to the end of it and put it on a table in a room over a weigh-house, and she did not read it, and she said one sentence at the door of that room on her way past, and about four hundred people in this town do not know that a sheet of a printed series came into the only office in that county that anything can be served with on the back of a woman's arm.

---

The ninth of the nine went into the sill of the toll house, and the sill is a foot and a half wide and it has no glass in it and no third seat, and there was a stone in it, and the stone was a stone off the bank and it had been on that sill since the twenty-eighth day of a month in Longlight with nothing at all on it.

He saw the stone and put the sheet down under it and did not move the stone, because the stone weighs a sheet and the stone is there for that, and a man who puts a sheet under a stone he did not bring is a man who has read what was in the sill before and has not said so.

There was no one in that building. The door at the low end has been open since the spring and there is a bench inside it with its back to the door and there is nothing in the building to light, and a man going up a lane with a satchel is looking at sills and not at doorways.

A man of fifty-five was in a hut at a barrier of three bars three hundred yards down that road with a fire going in it in a month when nobody needs one, and about nine people came through the bars that morning and got four words out of him each and nine identical sentences out of the county, and he has been in that hut nineteen years and nobody in this county has asked him where the four words come from, and he has never said and is not going to.

---

She is twenty-nine and has kept that office four years and a half out of two rooms over a weigh-house with a door on a lane, on a deed of thirteen words, at fourpence a year, and the word keeper in that office is employment and is not jurisdiction. The scales under her have not been used since the fourth of a month. There is a press in that room with two unserved books in it and a day-book and a half a tile that weighs a shade over ten ounces, and on that wall there is a fair copy nineteen years old in a keeper's own hand saying that no combined figure is entered in that office, and nobody has read it out loud in that room including her.

She had a minute of a court on a shelf since Saturday and it had come up that lane on a Saturday at about the second hour in the hand of a man with a bag who said it had been for the keeper for seven days and gave no name and was not asked for one.

The minute says four things and only four things, and one of them has a day on it.

She got a pencil out of the drawer at about the fifth hour and wrote the day on the back of that sheet, small, in the corner, and it took her about four seconds, and she did not enter it in the day-book and she did not enter it in the register and she did not put it in the press.

A day on the front of a sheet is a thing about the sheet. A day in pencil on the back of a sheet in a corner of a room with a door on a lane is a thing about a county, and there is no line in that office for it and there is no office two hundred miles off that would take it, because a day is not a place and not a person and not an hour and not a column.

---

**And the thing that day is on is a council.**

It holds to that day and after that day it holds if a local council renews it in public. A clerk of a Crown read the term out loud in a room about two hundred miles off and a woman of thirty-eight asked whether anybody in that county could renew it and a clerk of that Crown said that there is nobody in that county at all and that it is a room about two hundred miles from any county that is on any paper he had, and she said *then write that down* and it was in the ninth line of a minute and nobody in that room heard the ninth line.

There is no council in this county. There has never been one in the four years and a half of that office's keeping and there is no writ and no seal and no office in that county at all, and a county of about four thousand people has a bench of nine that has been asked eight things in nineteen years and has not been asked anything since the sixth of a month, and a bench of nine is not a council and has never claimed to be one, and a magistrate is a magistrate and does not renew anything in public, and the reeve does not vote on the guild's own matter and has not for nine years.

And the thing a bench of nine is for is nine people saying yes or no about a thing, and a thing with a day on it that a body has to renew in public is a thing that has to be put to a body in a room, and about four hundred yards from that room over a wharf there is a clerk of about sixty with a minute he has kept for nineteen years and a page under a stair with a count of askings on it that is eight, and nobody in that county has told him there is a day in a term, and nobody in that county is going to.

She put the pencil back in the drawer and shut it and did not go down to the wharf and did not go up the lane to ask anybody, and about four hundred people in that town do not know that a term with a day on it is lying in a room at the end of a lane in a county that cannot do anything about it and has not been asked to.

---

---

The keeper of records asked him one thing and he gave her one answer and it was the whole of that exchange.

“**Do you knock.**”

“**I do not knock,**” the carrier said.

“**Then how does a person know it is there.**”

“**They do not,**” the carrier said, and went on down the lane, and a man has carried about two thousand sheets in six years and has never once knocked on a door and has never once been asked why by anybody who was going to do anything about the answer, and he was asked on a Thursday morning by a woman of twenty-nine at the end of a lane and gave her the same answer he gives the weather.

And the pail woman, who had come up the lane behind him with nothing in her pail at about the fourth hour, said one thing at the door of that room and it was about the round, and the keeper of records said one thing back and neither of them said anything else.

“**You will be back.**”

“**I will be back,**” the woman said, “**and you will not read it, and I will not tell you what is on it, and the two of us will go on like that until one of us is dead.**”

“**That is a long arrangement,**” the keeper of records said.

“**It is the only one there has been,**” the woman said, and took the pail up and went on down the lane, and about four hundred people in that town do not know that about nine doors in that lane and one room at the end of it have an arrangement and that the arrangement has no paper in it anywhere and could not be produced in a room two hundred miles off if anybody asked for it.

---

That street filled at about the second hour and was empty again by about the seventh, and a salt waggon stood at the north end of it for the better part of an hour. A woman of about thirty went up the lane at about the fifth hour with a pail and said one thing at each of nine doors and was gone from the step before the knuckle had finished ringing, and at the door forty feet from a wagon arch she said that a man with a satchel had gone up that lane that morning and had not knocked at one of the nine, and she said it to the door. There are eight doors behind her in that lane and nobody in that lane has ever asked her why she says things at doors.

**And at about the sixth hour a man of thirty-two with a limp came down the outside stair of a room over a smithy in that town and got as far as the end of his lane and sat down on the step of a door about four feet off the ground, and he did not go up the lane, and about four hundred people in that market were standing in a street four hundred yards off and did not see him.**

He had been in a room over a smithy since the sixth day of a month in a year in which the year turns and the fever had been out of him sixteen days, and he could get down one flight and could not get up a bank, and a man who can get down a flight and cannot get up a bank sits on a step and does not explain the difference to anybody.

A woman of about thirty with a pail went up that lane at about the sixth hour and saw him on that step and went round the step.

“**There is a room at the end of this lane,**” she said, “**and I have not told the room.**”

“**You have told me,**” he said.

“**You are not the room,**” she said, and went on down the lane, and a woman who tells nine doors and not the room told a man sitting on a step outside his own door four feet off the end of it, and a man who has been in a room for about a month was not asked what the room is and did not ask, and the two of those are the ordinary way round.

Four hundred yards down the lane the reach had about forty men on it and the road was open over it and the salt had been over the first bay since the spring, and a man of fifty and a half who does the arches and does the pointing and does not do the laying was on the second bay with a bar he was not using, and a boy of fifteen was at a bench-board with a bill under his arm and no bill on the board, and the same man with the satchel had come up the market end of the lane at about the second hour with about nine sheets in it and had gone back down the market end at about the fourth and had not gone into the yard on Weir Street, which is four hundred yards the other way and which he has not been into in six years.

Nobody walked any part of that Thursday with anybody. The carrier went up that lane alone and came down it alone, and a woman carried a sheet down it on her arm and left it on a table, and the keeper of records wrote a day on the back of another sheet and did not go four hundred yards to tell anybody, and the tide was under the wharf at about the tenth hour, and the salt was over the first bay, and a sheet of that printed series is lying under a stone in a sill four hundred yards up that road with nobody in the building, and it will be there on Saturday.
