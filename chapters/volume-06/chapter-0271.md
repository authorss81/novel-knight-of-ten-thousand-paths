# Chapter 0271

**The twenty-fourth day of Greenmonth, YR 316, a Tuesday, and not a market day, and the fourth hour, and a field at a parish boundary four miles up this county's own road and about nine hundred yards off it, and about nine people in that field, and a stone that has come out of the ground that morning, and a clerk of about sixty with a book under his arm that is not the book he has been carrying about with him for nineteen years, and about four hundred people in a town of about four thousand people on a river who do not know that a boundary in this county is going to be said out loud in the open on a Tuesday, and that a bench of nine was not asked and was not going to be, and that the reason for that is the oldest thing anybody in that field knows and that nobody in that field has said it out loud.**

The stone is a foot and a half out of the ground and about nine inches square at the top, and it has a face on it that four people in that field have looked at and about four hundred in this county have never once looked at, because it stands in a hedge at the corner of two fields and a hedge at the corner of two fields is not a thing anybody stops at.

It has been out of the ground and put back four times in nineteen years, and this is the first time anybody has set it in the same week it was taken up, and the difference between the two is the whole of the morning.

Two men had it out of the hole and on a barrow by about the second hour and a half. It weighs what a stone that size weighs and it came up easier than a mason of fifty and a half said it would.

**“It will want the barrow again,” the mason said. “It always wants the barrow again. That is not the stone, that is the ground it has been standing in.”**

Nobody wrote that down. Nobody writes down what a mason says about ground.

The hole is about two feet deep and the sides of it are nearly straight, which means the stone has been lifted out and put back on its own four times by people who knew which way up it went, and the clay in the sides of the hole is smooth in a way that four inches of rain does not do.

A bucket of lime was brought up the hedge in a hand barrow by a man who is not on any roll in this county and who was not asked to come and was not asked anything when he had.

The two men who had the stone out were about twenty-six and about twenty-nine and they work on a reach four hundred yards down that lane, and one of them is from this town and the other is from nine miles up the channel, and the man from nine miles up the channel is the one who was not asked to come and did come, and he dug on his own time on the Thursday and charged nothing for the Thursday and has been paid for the other three days out of a private box that a bench does not hold.

“**You are not on anything,**” the reeve said to him.

**“I am not on anything,” he said, “and I have dug four of these holes in nineteen years and I have been paid for three of them and I was paid for the fourth one by a man who has since gone to a place that is not on any map, and that is the whole of my connection with a boundary.”**

Nobody in that field had heard about the man who had gone to a place that is not on any map, and about four people in that field had heard something else about that man, and none of the four said anything, and a field at a parish boundary is not a room and there is no rule in a field.

And the two fields either side of the hedge are not the same field, and the two parishes either side of the hedge are not the same parish, and the hedge is about two feet thick and has been there so long that there is a hollow in it about four inches from the ground on the eastern side of it, and nobody in that field knows which side of the hedge the hollow is on and nobody in that field has ever tried to find out.

---

The clerk of about sixty got there at about the fourth hour with a book under his arm and a straight edge and a rule, and the rule was not for the stone.

He had a book under his arm on a Tuesday in a field four miles up a road, and it was not the book he has kept for nineteen years in a long room over a wharf, and about four people in that field worked that out separately in about a minute and none of them said it to the others.

**“Is that the other one,” the reeve said.**

**“It is a book,” the clerk said. “That is the whole of what I can say about it in a field and it is more than I could say about it in a room, and you will both want me to say the rest of it in about a quarter of an hour, and I am not going to, and it is not because of you.”**

The reeve is the elder sister of the magistrate and has been the reeve of this county for nine years and has not voted on the guild's own matter for any of them. She got to a true thing in that field by saying a false one first, which is the only thing she is good at in the office and which she has said out loud exactly once in nine years, to nobody.

**“I am not going to ask you what kind of book it is,” she said. “I have two minutes of standing in a field and they are the two minutes I get, and I would rather spend them on the lime.”**

The magistrate is thirty and has been on a bench of nine for nine years and has beaten two counts of persons with the form's own construction and has said nothing about either of them in that room, and on a Tuesday in a field she did not sit down either, because there is nothing in that field to sit on and nobody had brought anything to sit on.

---

**And the four people who had worked out that a bench of nine has no standing at a parish boundary had worked it out in the ordinary way, over about half an hour, without saying anything to anybody about it.**

A bench of nine sits on a county matter. A boundary between two parishes is not a county matter and has never been one, and the bench has never claimed that it was, and there is no place in the long room over the wharf where a boundary is entered, and a count on a page under that stair in that room is a count of the things the bench has been asked, and the number of things it has been asked in nineteen years is eight and it is on that page and it is not going to be nine this month.

The magistrate knew it. The reeve knew it and had said so in her own way. The clerk of about sixty had known it since he was asked in Harvestmonth and had said nothing to anybody, which is his ordinary practice and has been for nineteen years.

A fourth person was the mason of fifty and a half, who knew it because he has cut stone for thirty-one years and has set boundary stones in four counties and has never once been asked to produce a minute about it.

**Nobody in that field said it out loud and the minute was kept anyway.**

A thing that four people know and no one says is not a secret. It is an arrangement, and the arrangement in that field on that morning was that the bench of nine was not asked, and nobody was asked to explain why, and nobody was going to be.

---

**And a boundary oath-stone is a promise, and a promise that can only be kept by breaking something else is a claim and not a record, and there is a difference between the two that about four people in that field understood in the ordinary way and one of them said.**

The reeve said it, standing up, with her hand on the barrow.

**“We are not writing down where the hedge is,” she said. “The hedge is where the hedge is and it was there before any of us and it will be there after. We are writing down that nobody moves the stone. And if the day ever comes that a man on this side wants the hedge four feet over, or a man on that side wants it four feet back, then the stone is the only thing in this field that says no to him, and a thing that says no to a man is not a record of a field, it is a claim on a man, and he does not have to accept it, and nothing in this field can make him.”**

The mason put his thumb on the top of the stone and wiped it and looked at the thumb.

**“That is four times too many words for a Tuesday,” he said.**

**“It is four times too many words for any day,” the reeve said, “and it has not been said at one of these in nineteen years, and I have just said it, and you may all go home.”**

Nobody wrote that down either. The clerk of about sixty had his book shut under his arm and he was standing four feet off with his back to the wind and he had not opened it while she was talking, and a man who has a book under his arm and does not open it while the thing he came to write is being said out loud in front of him has made a decision about the order of two things and nobody has asked him about it.

---

The clerk opened the book at about the fifth hour and asked for the day.

Nobody in that field had a calendar. This is not a figure of speech and it is not a thing about farmers. **There are about four hundred people in a town of about four thousand in this county, and the county has a market twice a fortnight and a court that sits on a day, and a person who does not want to be asked what day it is has about four ways of finding out and all four of them involve another person.**

The clerk had all four of them ruled into a column on the inside cover of the book under a line he had drawn himself, and he read the day off it in about two seconds without counting on his fingers, and about four people in that field looked at the column and none of them asked what the other three lines were for.

**“The twenty-fourth of Greenmonth,” he said. “That is a Tuesday and it is not a market day, and the first of Longlight is a Tuesday and is not a market day either, and there will be about nine of us in this field on that day at the fourth hour and a man with a barrow, and I want the hour in the book and I want it in the book at the time and not afterwards, because a minute with the hour filled in afterwards is a thing somebody could have put in.”**

**“You have been filling them in afterwards for nineteen years,” the magistrate said.**

**“I have been filling them in afterwards for nineteen years,” the clerk said, “and the last nine of them are in the same hand and I have never once had to explain that, and I would rather go on not having to.”**

The lime went in wet. The mason wanted it dry and the clerk wanted it wet and they settled it in about four sentences with neither of them giving way on the principle, only on the weather, and the weather was against the mason, and the mason said so twice.

The stone went back at about the sixth hour and it went back the way it had come out, and the clerk ruled a line on the ground with his heel to stand on while he said the day out loud, and the line is not on the page and has been trodden out and there is nothing whatever left of it.

And about four hundred people in a town of about four thousand people on a river do not know that a stone in a hedge four miles up that county's own road was put back on the twenty-fourth day of Greenmonth, YR 316, and that it is going to be said over on the first of Longlight at the fourth hour, and that the words are a copy of the words said over it nineteen years ago, and that the words said nineteen years ago were a copy of words in a book this county has never had and that has now been in a bag on a road somewhere for eight days.
