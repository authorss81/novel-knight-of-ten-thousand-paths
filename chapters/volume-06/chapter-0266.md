# Chapter 0266

**The twenty-sixth day of Mudmonth, YR 316, a Tuesday, and not a market day in a county with a market, and the seventh hour and the eighth hour, and a room over a cross in a valley four hundred miles from any county that is on a map, and about four hundred people in that valley of whom about thirty are in that room, and a book on a trestle with nine spreads in it, and a bench at the back of the right-hand row with a hundred and forty cut into the end of it, and an aisle four feet wide between the two middle rows that has gone three generations without being used, and one stranger in that room who has come nine days up a made road and is not on any bench and is not going to be.**

He came into the room at about the seventh hour and there was a fire in it and about thirty people in it and about nine of them on benches, and about two hundred of the ninth generation were standing at the back, and not one of the standing ones was twenty-two and fewer than nine of them were.

The benches are bolted to the floor in four rows and there are twenty-nine of them and a number is cut into the end of each one, and the number is not a seat and is not a name and is not a count of anybody. A room in which every bench has a number cut into the end of it is a room in which somebody at some point expected to be able to say *the fourth row, the second bench*. Nine generations have never once used it that way, and that is a thing about a room and not about the people in it.

The bench at the back of the right-hand row has a hundred and forty cut into the end of it, and the hundred and forty is a count of the times anybody has ever stood in the aisle to be asked, and no name has ever been struck off one of those benches and none of the twenty-nine numbers is a name.

**And the aisle is four feet wide, and it has gone three generations without being used, and about four people in that valley have said since that a hundred and forty and three generations are two figures about the same aisle and that nobody in that room has ever put them side by side and that the man of fifty-eight could do it in about a second and has not, and that a woman of sixty-six could do it faster and offered to in writing and gave it up in writing and has not brought it up since.**

---

**And the ninth spread is the one they keep and there are about two hundred lines on it and they begin at the top of a spread and go down in one run of the page, and every one of them has a day against it and a mark against it, and a day against a line of that kind is the day the person was born, and about two hundred of that generation have never gone out of that valley and have never been asked to and never will be, and the man of fifty-eight has said out loud in a room that nobody put two hundred people in a column to be struck and that there is no form on this earth the ninth spread could be used for, and it is a birth list with a day in it that has been kept in the shape of somebody else's answer for a hundred and forty years.**

There is a second column on that ninth spread about the width of two fingers, ruled in the man of fifty-eight's own ink, and eight people have written their own names in it, and the ninth line of it is empty and is not ruled across and is not going to be, and there is a figure of nine in the day column that is wrong by one with a margin note in the same man's hand saying *eight named and one not found*, A margin note is the only place in that book where a man has ever admitted that a figure of nine was not a figure of nine, and the admission is a hundred and forty years old or thereabouts, and nobody in that valley has ever read it out.

---

The book came off a trestle at about the seventh hour and a half. It is about forty leaves tied with a piece of tarred hemp and there are nine spreads in it, one to a generation, and it has been kept nineteen years by the man of fifty-eight and by his father's before him and by his father's father's before that, and the first spread is not the beginning of anything.

Nobody put a hand on it for about a minute after it was on the trestle.

---

---

**And a man of eighty-one came and had not been asked to come, and a man of sixty-one came out of a mill at the low end of the water who cannot leave his wheel and had not wanted to come and came, and a woman of about thirty who has carried a slate for eleven years came and sat on the second bench, and a man of about thirty-one of the ninth generation who farms four steadings had not wanted to come and came, and the woman of sixty-six came and gave it up in writing in a minute nobody can be served to, and the man of fifty-five came and had voted against keeping the winter eight to one in the fourth week of the fourth cold and has not stood in the aisle at any point in thirty-five years.**

Nine people on the benches and about thirty in the room, and about two hundred of the ninth generation at the back not in a seat, and about four people in that valley have said since that a room which seats twenty-nine and holds two hundred is a room that was built for nine and has been used for two hundred every time it has been used, and that nobody in that valley has ever proposed to build another one and that the proposal has never been needed because nobody in that valley has ever wanted to be in a room with more than about nine people in charge of it.

---

**And the man who reads it asked for the reading about four minutes after the book was on the trestle, and he asked in front of about thirty people, and about four people in that valley have said since that a man who asks for a thing in front of thirty people in a room that seats twenty-nine is a man who could not have asked for it in a room of nine and that the thirty is not a crowd and is a wall, and that he has wanted the four lines read out in that room for six years and has said so to about four people in kitchens and has never once said so in that room until the twenty-sixth day of a month that nobody in that valley can name.**

A man of thirty-four with sixteen men and no work seconded it and a man of fifty-five did not and a woman of sixty-six did not and a man of eighty-one said nothing at all, and nobody counted the hands and there is no column in that room for a hand and there has not been one since the second cold of the ninth generation. A room that counts its own hands is a bench, and a room that has nine people on benches in it is not a bench. The difference between the two is a bench, and this room has one.

Nobody went to the fire and the fire went down about a foot over the four minutes before the reading, and about four people in that room have said since that a room of about thirty people does not move for a book and a room of about nine people does, and that on the twenty-sixth day of that month about thirty people stood in a cold room for nine minutes for a man of fifty-eight, and about nine of them were on benches and about four of the nine had come for the fire.

---

**And the four lines at the head of the first spread were read out in that room on that evening for the first time in a hundred and forty years, and they were read out by the man of fifty-eight who has read them about nine hundred times in a room over a byre and has never once worked out what they were a rule about, and the man who read them out had asked for them to be read out and nobody else in that room had asked and nobody in that room stopped him.**

He read them in the voice of a man reading a rule and he read them at the speed of a man reading out a thing he has not read in nineteen years, and the room was of about the size it was made for, and there was a man at the back of the right-hand row who was not on that bench and who did not know until after the reading was done that the reading had been slowed down.

*This book is kept and the room is kept. The keeper of it is a man and not an office.*

*The keeper of it answers for the book and the room and the day and the mark and the hand.*

*The keeper of it answers for no person in this valley and speaks for no person in this valley and has been appointed by no person in this valley.*

*Where a man is named in a book of this valley it is because he did it himself and not because anybody asked him.*

**And about four people in that valley have said since that the four lines are a hundred and forty years old and that the hundred and forty is the same figure as the one cut into the end of the bench at the back of the right-hand row, and that a figure being the same in two places in one room is not a coincidence and is not a mystery either, it is a room that has been measuring the same thing two ways for a hundred and forty years and that nobody in that room has ever been asked to say why, and that the man of fifty-eight has read one of them about nine hundred times and has never once put the two together and that he is fifty-eight.**

He squared the book before he read and squared it again after, and about four people in that room have said since that a man squares a book about four hundred times a year and that a man who squares it twice in nine minutes has done a thing with his hands that his head has not caught up with, and that about two hundred of the ninth generation at the back of that room were watching his hands and not his face and about four of them had worked out what he was doing before the room had.

He put his hand flat on the cover and left it there for about four seconds, and he was in no hurry about it and nobody in that room was in a hurry about it either.

**And a man of thirty-four with sixteen men and no work said what he had about those four lines and it came out in three parts, and all three of them were about a column, and nobody improved on any of them, and a man of thirty-two with a limp who has been in that room for about a quarter of an hour understood all three of them and is not going to say so and did not.**

The first was that the third line is the answer to a column in a form. The second was that the fourth line is a rule about a name and not about a book, and that whoever wrote the four lines wrote the opposite of what the form asked for and put it at the top of his own answer. The third was that a valley with no writ and no office and no clerk has been answering a count of men going out for nine generations by putting down every person in the place where the men would be.

Nobody said anything after the third one for about a minute and a half.

**And the man of fifty-eight said at about the eighth hour that he had wanted the four lines read out in that room for six years, and nobody in that room had asked him about wanting it, and about four people in that valley have said since that a man who has wanted a thing for six years and has never once been asked about it has been in the same position as a person who has asked and been refused, and that the two are not the same and that the difference is the whole of what a room is for, and that about four hundred people in that valley cannot be got to see it and are not going to be.**

He said the six years out loud for the first time and said nothing else about it, and a man of thirty-two with a limp was in that room and said nothing, and there was that county's own form folded once in the inside pocket of a coat that was over a chair nine feet off him, ruled in seven, with a column for a witness at this end and a column for a witness at that end, and about four people in that valley cannot see inside a coat and are not going to.

---

**And the woman of sixty-six was at the front on the second bench and she had been asked what she swore forty years ago in that aisle in the cold in front of the whole valley and it took nine minutes and it was the worst nine minutes of her life, and she has told the ninth generation about it four times and told them it was survivable, and she was wrong, and she knows she was wrong, and she has never told anybody which of the three was the wrong one.**

She said one sentence on that evening and it was to her knees and it was fourteen words, and it is the only thing she said in that room in about four years and she has said it to nobody and will not say it again.

“**I have had my turn at it and I would not have it back.**”

Nobody asked her a second question and the man of thirty-four did not ask her one and the man with a stick did not ask her one, and about four people in that valley have said since that a woman of sixty-six who gave up a thing in writing and has said fourteen words about having given it up has been left alone about it by everybody in that room including two men who had come nine days to ask questions, and that this is the arrangement this valley has and that it is not politeness and that it has cost her about forty years and has never been entered anywhere.
