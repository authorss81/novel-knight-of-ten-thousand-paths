# Chapter 0273

**The first day of Longlight, YR 316, a Tuesday, and not a market day, and the fourth hour, and a field at a parish boundary four miles up a fen county's own road, in the open, with about nine people in it and a man with a barrow, and a clerk of about sixty with a book that is not the book he has been keeping for nineteen years, and a magistrate of thirty who is on a bench of nine and has nine years of a bench and no standing whatever at a parish boundary, and about four hundred people in a town of about four thousand people on a river who do not know that a promise was said out loud in a field on that Tuesday and that it was written down, and that the writing of it is the second kind of minute that man has kept in nineteen years, and that a bench of nine was not asked, and that the not-asking is the oldest arrangement in that field.**

It rained on the Thursday before and it had stopped by the fourth hour on the Tuesday and the ground was in the state a mason of fifty and a half calls worked, which is to say it will take lime and hold it, and he said the word worked twice and would not define it.

The stone was out of the ground again by about the third hour. It is a foot and a half and about nine inches square at the top and it has four letters cut into the face of it and a figure under the letters that is the year it was last done, and the figure is nineteen years old and is the figure of the last time anybody in this county did this.

The man with the barrow and the two men who had it out on the Thursday put it back at about the third hour and a half and the mason set it in lime and the clerk ruled a line on the ground with his heel and the line is not on the page and was trodden out within the hour.

And about nine people were in that field, and about nine people have been in that field every time anybody has done this in nineteen years, and about four of those nine had worked out that a bench of nine has no standing at a parish boundary and has never claimed any, and not one of the nine said it to the others, and the minute was kept anyway.

The clerk asked, before anybody else had said anything, who was going to say the words.

He asked it the way a man asks a question he has already got an answer to and is giving the room a chance to have a different one, and about four people in that field recognised the shape of it and about four had not.

“**You are the magistrate,**” the clerk said, to the magistrate.

**“I am the magistrate of this county and I have nine years of it,” she said, “and I have never in nine years spoken at a parish boundary and I am not going to pretend to anybody that I know what is said at one.”**

**“Then nobody is going to say it,” the clerk said, “and a minute of a thing nobody said is a minute of a field with about nine people in it, and that is a worse minute than a minute of a promise, and I have kept one of those in nineteen years and it is not worth the paper.”**

The reeve said one word at that, which was *Aren*, and then said nothing else for about a minute, and nobody asked her which Aren she meant and there is no column in that field for a first name.

---

She read it from a sheet that was not the book. It is four lines and it is nineteen years old in its wording and it is a copy of a copy of something this county has never had in its hand, and she had it on a sheet of her own and she had it folded in four and she took it out and unfolded it and squared it on the barrow, and she did not read it at once.

And the bench of nine was not asked, and the minute has nothing in it about a bench, and the count on a page under a stair in a long room four hundred yards down that road is eight and it is eight now, and a thing that is not asked is not a refusal and is not a yes, and this county has not had one of either in nineteen years and is not going to have one this month.

Nobody read it a second time. That was arranged before anybody came up the hedge, and the arrangement was made by the reeve and not by the clerk, and the clerk found out about it the way he finds out about most things in that county, which is by being handed it.

The magistrate read it in the voice of a woman reading out a rule she has read about four times in nineteen years and not out loud.

*We are two parishes and this stone is between us and neither of us will move it.*

*We will not set it forward nor back and we will not set it deep nor shallow.*

*If a man comes to this stone and says he will move it, he will be told no by both of us together and not by one of us alone.*

*This stone is the answer and it was put here before anybody alive and it will be here after.*

Four lines. She read them in about eleven seconds and stopped and put the sheet down on the barrow and squared it with the edge of her hand, and the four of them that had worked it out had not said it and the five that had not looked at what she was reading had not either.

Nobody in that field improved on a word of it. A man of about sixty packing lime in a yard four hundred yards away is the closest thing this county has to a person who keeps anything and he is not in that field and was not asked and would not have come.

---

**And the clerk of about sixty kept the second kind of minute he has kept in nineteen years, and the first kind is the minute of a bench of nine in a long room over a wharf that he has kept for nineteen years and has about four hundred entries in it, and a great many of them are about salt.**

A clerk who keeps two kinds of minute and has kept both for nineteen years is a clerk who knows that a thing an office can enter is a thing an office can classify, and he has known it for nineteen years and has never said it out loud, and he said it out loud on that Tuesday at about the fifth hour to a barrow in a field, and it was thirteen words, and nobody improved on it.

**“A thing an office can enter is a thing an office can classify.”**

He did not raise his voice and he did not look at anybody while he said it, and he went back to the book, and a man of thirty-two with a limp was nine feet off with his stick in his hand and heard all thirteen words of it and has not written them down and is not going to.

**And that clerk has entered the absence of a vote once in nineteen years and he entered it by not entering it, and when a man asked him in writing what the bench had decided about a crossing he entered nothing at all, and the nothing at all is still in that book, and there is no line in it and there has never been a line in it and the space where a line would go is the same depth as the spaces either side of it.**

---

The entry describes what is at that boundary. That is the whole of what an entry can do and this one did it in the ordinary way and did it well, and the description is a list of things and of what is not there, and a list like that goes down a column without anybody having to think about it.

**A crossing with no owner and no money. A sill with nothing in it and no glass in it and no third seat. A dressed block in the abutment, squared, and nothing in the stone that anybody has ever read. A bell a hundred yards off that goes at the fourth hour and a half.**

Nobody in that field asked what the bell is for and nobody in that field could have said, and the bell is not on the boundary and is not part of the promise and is in the entry because a description of what is at a place does not leave things out.

The clerk wrote the place in the object column the way that office writes a place, which is as the name of the nearest thing anybody in that office has ever heard of, and he wrote it in about four seconds, and there is a word in front of the place name.

**The word is in the same type as the heads of the five columns. It came out of a case in a press room in a provincial town two hundred miles off about eleven weeks ago and it was struck once by a boy of nineteen who does what he is told, and nobody in that office has ever said what the word is for, and no character in this county has asked.**

The clerk read the line up before he entered it, because a line is read up, and he read the word out loud with the rest of it, and about nine people in a field at a parish boundary heard a man say a word they had not heard before and about four of them have since worked out that he did not know it either, and he did not know it, and he was asked about it once at about the sixth hour.

**“Where is that word from,” the magistrate said.**

**“Off the head of the column,” the clerk said. “It is the word that goes in that column on that series. If I left it off, it is not a line of that series, it is a piece of paper with writing on it, and I have got four hundred things in a book in this county that are pieces of paper with writing on them and I am not going to make a fifth.”**

Nobody asked him a second question. He had one answer and he had given it and he went back to the book, and a man of about fifty who has been a clerk of a press for thirty-one years in a building two hundred miles off entered about eleven thousand forms and never once asked where any word came from, and nobody is going to ask him now.

---

And the whole of what it cost that field to say a promise out loud in the open and write it down was this.

A sheet of the county's own form, a penny. A second sheet of the same form for the parish on the other side of the hedge, a penny. A clerk's fee for entering a minute, fourpence. Lime for the setting of a stone that has been out of the ground and put back four times in nineteen years, twopence. Four letters cut into the face of it, fourpence. A carrier's hand for four miles, twopence. The bench of nine at no rate and never has been, and the nine men in that field at no rate either, and the office two hundred miles off at no rate, because nobody in that field has ever been sent a bill by anybody.

*A penny and a penny is twopence, and twopence and fourpence is sixpence, and sixpence and twopence is eightpence, and eightpence and fourpence is twelve pence, and twelve pence and twopence is fourteen pence.*

**Total: fourteen pence, a shilling and twopence. And a carrier took the sheet down to the market on Weir Street that afternoon and put it in a bag in the ordinary post, and a bag in the ordinary post is the second thing that has ever left that county out of a field in nineteen years, and nobody in that field knew what a description of a thing at a place is for, and nobody in this county has ever known, and the sheet is four hundred yards from a crossing in a book in a press in a building two hundred miles off, and the word in front of the place name on it is in the same type as the heads of the five columns, and it is the first time that word has been said out loud in this county and it is not going to be defined here or anywhere.**

Nobody in that field said anything else. The barrow went back down the hedge with the tools in it, and the clerk shut the book and put it under his arm, and the stone stood in the hedge in the wet with a figure nineteen years old cut into the face of it, and about four hundred people in a town of about four thousand people on a river went on with the rest of that Tuesday and did not know that a promise had been said in a field four miles up their own road and had a column in it.
