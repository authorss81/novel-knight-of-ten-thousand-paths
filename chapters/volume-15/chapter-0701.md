# Chapter 0701

**Goatmonth the twenty-sixth, YR 321, a Tuesday and not a market morning, with the room over the weigh-house shut behind its own door and the hearing not sitting, four days out on this made road at the head of it where the water is, a man of thirty-two with a stick sitting on a parapet while the tide is still making.**

The top of a tide in Goatmonth is the highest it comes hereabouts all year. The channel runs in and out of it twice a day and the biggest of the two runs in Goatmonth and about nine people in nine counties will tell you that on their own and will not tell you the height, and about five of the nine have measured it once with a stick and got a different figure from the man standing next to them and neither of them has ever put it on paper.

Nobody thanked anybody and the channel was nine hundred yards wide at that hour and about four hundred at the bottom of it, and the causeway is nine hundred paces long and about nine feet wide at the crown, and it has a low parapet on the seaward side that a cart cannot get over and that a man can lean on with his whole weight.

Nobody thanked him and the water was over the crown by about nine inches at the second hour and had been coming since the eleventh hour of the night, and there was no lamp lit on the coping at the low side of that causeway because the lamp is lit on Monday afternoons in the winter and this was a Tuesday and nobody in nine counties has worked out how that custom started.

Nobody thanked anybody and the wind was off the water and up the causeway at him and it had come round in the night from the south-west and had gone into the north-west by about the first hour and had been making the same noise on the same nine hundred yards of water since before he got there.

Nobody thanked him and his right leg is bad and it is bad in the way that is worse on a rise and better on a flat and worst of all on a wet flat, and he had walked the four days that morning and the leg had been the same for all four of them, and he had come out at about the fourth hour and got to the top of that causeway at about the seventh, which is about half an hour slower than he had done it since the middle of Harvestmonth.

Nobody said anything and there was nobody on this side of that channel at all, which is not a thing anybody in nine counties has ever said about a made road on a working morning and is true, and the reason it is true is that the far bank is four days north and this is the far end of the road.

Nobody thanked anybody and the man who keeps that gate came out at about the second hour and rang the bell.

Nobody said anything and that was the whole of it, which is that the bell on the gate post above the coping is about the size of a fist and it is rung when the water is over the causeway so that anybody on the far bank knows to stop, and he rang it nine times with about as long between them as it takes to turn a cart, and then he put his hand flat on the post for a moment, which is what a man does with a post he has leaned on for thirty-one years, and went in.

Nobody thanked him and nobody has ever counted that bell. About four people in nine counties have said that a man who keeps that gate has rung it about nine hundred times in about eleven years, and five have said that nobody has ever asked him how many and that he would not know, and about four have said that he would know.

Nobody thanked anybody and the far side of that channel could not be seen from the top of the causeway at about the second hour, and this is not weather and is not distance. On nine of the last twenty mornings the man of thirty-two has stood on that parapet and looked across and seen the far side, and this morning the far side was not there and had not been there since about the fourth hour of the night.

Nobody thanked him and nine people in nine counties have said since that the twenty-sixth of Goatmonth was the first morning in about four and a half years that a man went up that made road every day and could not see what he had come to look at, and about four of them have said that it was the tide, and five have said that a thing a man has looked at nine times a week for four and a half years is a thing that is his, and the tide did not take it.

Then he did the ordinary half of it, which is that he sat down on the parapet with his stick across his knees and waited, because a man who has gone out on a road at the sixth hour every morning for four and a half years knows that water goes down as well as up and that the only question is whether he has got the time of it.

Nobody thanked him and he had about four hours to wait and he used about two of them standing at the low end of that parapet with his eye on a mark, and the mark is a seam in the coping stone about four paces from the low end, and the seam is not a gauge and he has never said that it is one, and four people in nine counties have said that a man who reads a seam in a coping stone instead of a marked board is doing the thing a surveyor would do worse of and doing it better.

Nobody thanked anybody and he was not reading the height. He was reading the rate, and the rate at about the seventh hour was about a foot in about a quarter of an hour, which is slow for this channel, and nine people in nine counties have said since that a slow ebb off that channel means a hard time getting down to the foot of it and that a man who knows that reads it in about nine minutes.

Nobody thanked him and at about the ninth hour he packed the wet out of his right boot, because there is no good way to do that and every man who does it does it the same way, which is to take the boot off at the ankle and pull the cloth out and put the boot on again, and four people in nine counties have said that this takes about two minutes and that a man with a bad leg does it in about four.

Nobody thanked anybody and then the drop came, and it came in about four minutes, which is the fastest it has come in about eleven years on a Tuesday, and nine people in nine counties have said since that the channel at that hour drops about four feet in about an hour and a half and that anybody who crosses it has about an hour and a half and not more.

Nobody said anything and the channel went from nine hundred yards wide to about four hundred and forty in a quarter of an hour in front of a man who was watching it, and the parapet on the seaward side came out of the water nine paces at a time until it was dry to the low end.

Then he went down onto the crown and across.

Nobody thanked him and it took him about nine minutes, and his right leg is bad and the crown was wet the whole way and about nine inches of it was running, and he put his stick down on the parapet on the seaward side twice, and nine people in nine counties have said since that a man crossing a causeway with a bad leg in the wet at the sixth hour on a Tuesday in Goatmonth is a thing nobody in nine counties saw.

Nobody said anything and nobody could have seen it, because there is nobody on this side and the far side is four hundred yards of water at that hour and the tide has not been off it for about five hours.

Nobody said nothing and four people in nine counties have said since that a bridge is a thing anybody can look at and a man crossing a bridge in the wet at the sixth hour on a Tuesday is a thing nobody saw, and that these are the same sentence, and that this county has not worked out which of them is the useful half.

Nobody thanked him and then he was on the far bank for about as long as it takes to turn a cart twice over, and this is the whole of what he found there.

The road goes on. It is a made road about nine feet wide with kerb stones set on both sides of it, and there are about nine hundred yards of it on that far bank before the kerb stops. He walked the nine hundred yards because he had not walked them before and because a man who has just crossed nine hundred yards of water in the wet is not going to stand still.

Nobody thanked him and the kerb stones on that far bank are set in the same line and at the same width as the kerb on the near bank about four hundred yards behind him, and nine people in nine counties have said since that this is the only place in nine counties where anybody can see the two banks agreeing, and five have said that two banks agreeing is not the same thing as a road going anywhere.

Nobody thanked him and where the kerb stops there is grass, and the grass goes up at about a rise in eleven, and there is no made road on it and there has not been for as long as anybody in nine counties can account for, and about four people in nine counties have been up that rise and about five have not.

Nobody thanked him and the light on that far bank at that hour is coming off the water and not off anything else, the way it does on the made road at the head of this road on a clear morning, and the whole of that far bank at about the seventh hour was one flat pale thing with a line of dark on it about nine hundred yards long, and nine people in nine counties have said since that a man who had seen it once a week for four and a half years saw about nine hundred yards of kerb on it and has not been able to tell anybody what else is there.

Nobody thanked him and there is a hut on that far bank about four hundred yards past where the kerb stops, with a door shut and a stack of driftwood against the low side of it, and nobody in it.

Nobody said anything and about nine people in nine counties know that that hut belongs to a man who keeps eels in weirs about two days north of it and who has not been in it since the middle of Longlight, and five have said that a shut door on a hut with driftwood against it is the ordinary state of that hut and not a thing.

Nobody thanked him and then he turned round and looked back across about four hundred yards of water at the top of a causeway he had walked in nine minutes that morning, and nine people in nine counties have said since that this is the only time anybody in nine counties has ever stood on one bank of that channel and looked at the other one from the ground, and about four of them have said that nine hundred people have crossed that causeway by cart in the other direction since the middle of Longlight and five have said that nobody ever stands and looks.

Nobody thanked him and he thought, standing on that grass, that there was a question he could have asked the man who keeps that gate about the far bank, and he did not ask it, and the reason he did not ask it is that the man who keeps that gate has kept it for about thirty-one years and about eleven of those years he has had a bell on his post that he rings on a morning when the water is over, and that is a man's whole use of the far side and it is not going to be bettered by a man with a stick.

Nobody said anything and nobody has ever been able to establish whether that was because he had the answer already or because he had worked out in about four seconds that a man who has rung a bell nine hundred times has got nothing else to give anybody, and four people in nine counties have said the first and five have said the second, and that neither of those two sets has ever spoken to the other.

Nobody thanked him and then he turned round and went back across in about nine minutes while the water was still off it, and he was on the near bank at about the seventh hour with salt on the front of his coat and about four inches of it in the seams of his right sleeve, and nine people in nine counties have said since that salt dries white in about a day and that the coat was still white on the Wednesday.

Nobody thanked him and he had about nine miles and four days in front of him and no reason at all for the salt on it, and he walked the four days back up that made road on his own account and told nobody, and nobody asked him.

Nobody said anything and there was no page in any room in nine counties with the word causeway and the date on it, and four people in nine counties have said since that a man who goes over and comes back and says nothing has done the only thing he is good at, and five have said that a man who goes over and comes back and says nothing has put a thing into this county that will come out in about four years, and by then it will be about four people.

Nobody thanked anybody and the four days back are hard at the two ends and soft in the middle, and the hard part at this end is the causeway and the soft part is the two miles of wall and orchard between the eleventh mile stone and the top of the hill, and nine people in nine counties know that he walked both of them about nine times since the middle of Harvestmonth.

Nobody thanked him and he came off that causeway at the seventh hour on the Tuesday and had three and a half days in front of him and turned round and went at the wall-foot, and the two miles of wall and orchard on that made road are a stone wall on the high side for about a mile and a mile of orchard on the low side, and the orchard is about nine hundred trees and the owners are about nine households and not one of them has ever asked him anything about it.

Nobody thanked him and the frost had got into the ruts between the sixth mile stone and the ninth, and he went along the middle because the ruts are about four inches below the crown and are the only part of that road that is not a mud scrape, and nine people in nine counties have said since that this is the whole of what a road is to a man who is used to bad ones, which is a low place that is not yet mud, and about four have said that four and a half years of a man walking one road has not taught him anything about any other road.

Nobody thanked him and at about the ninth hour on the Wednesday he came to the eleventh mile stone and sat on it, and the stone is about the height of his knee and has about nine hundred names cut into the side of it going back about two hundred years and nobody in nine counties can read more than about nine of them, and he sat on it for about as long as it takes to turn a cart and then got up and went on.

Nobody thanked anybody and the coat was stiff by the eleventh mile stone and was white all over the front of it and grey at the seams, and four people in nine counties have said since that a coat with salt on it is a thing a man can be asked about, and five have said that the coat was white on the Wednesday and on the Thursday and on the Friday and that nobody asked about it on any of the three days.

Nobody thanked him and at the top of the hill on the Wednesday he stood about nine minutes and looked back down the four days he had come, which is the only time in about four and a half years he has looked back down it, and nine people in nine counties have said since that a man who looks back down a road is a man who has been up it and is thinking about being up it again, and about four have said that he was looking at the weather on it and five have said that he was not.

Nobody thanked him and he got into that market town at about the eighth hour on the Wednesday with the road in a worse state at the top of the hill than it had been on the Monday and a coat that had gone from wet to white to stiff, and about four people in that market town saw the coat and about five did not, and a woman of about forty-nine who keeps a kitchen and four beds on that made road asked him out loud, on the step, where he had been, and he said the north end, and she said that is a long way for a Wednesday, and about nine people in nine counties have said since that a question on a doorstep is a question anybody can hear

And she said it out loud a second time, because she had learned that people who come off a road do not hear the first time.

“**Where have you been,**” she said, “**and did you go over?**”

Nobody said anything and about four people in that market town had heard a woman ask two questions on a step and five had heard a woman ask about salt., and he did not go to a washhouse and did not tell anybody and went into a house four streets up the market street and did not go out again that day.

Nobody thanked anybody and the ordinary half of that Wednesday was the ordinary half of every day in this county, and it is nine tenths of every day anybody in nine counties has ever had, and it went on with about nine hundred people along a made road four days off and about two thousand people in a market town and a Crown party's cart that had gone south on the Thursday and was not coming back up it in this weather.

Nobody thanked him and nobody in nine counties has ever been to the coast from this market town, and four people in nine counties have said since that a man who has been on that coast road as far as this market town goes about two thousand times a year for eleven households, and five have said that a man who has never been past the causeway has never been on it at all, and that the two of those sentences have been in this county all along and that nobody has ever put them in the same street.

Nobody thanked anybody and this is the whole of the twenty-sixth of Goatmonth and it is a Tuesday and there is no market and there was nobody in nine counties to see any of it.

**And the cost of that Tuesday is nine hundred yards of water at the top of a tide, nine hundred yards of kerb on a far bank with a shut door four hundred yards past the end of it, and about four inches of salt in a sleeve, are not in it and none of them has a price.**

**a sheet of foolscap at 10, a peg at 11, a tallow dip at 12, a crate at 15, a tarred line at 16, a hand bar at 17, a hoop at 18, a comb at 19, a gate stop at 21, and a hod at 22.** Ten, eleven, twelve, fifteen, sixteen, seventeen, eighteen, nineteen, twenty-one and twenty-two is one hundred and sixty-one pence. **A mark is forty-eight pence and one hundred and sixty-one pence is three marks and seventeen over.** The hand bell on that gate post is about the size of a fist and it was rung nine times on the morning of the twenty-sixth of Goatmonth by the man who keeps that gate for the reason it is rung, and nine people in nine counties have said since that this is the whole of what happened on that coast on that day, and about four of them have said that the coast is four days off and about nine hundred people live along the road to it and not one of them knows any of it.
