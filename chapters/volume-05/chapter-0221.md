# Chapter 0221

**The twentieth day of a count of days that this city keeps from the last renewal and keeps two of, and the second hour, and a road coming down off the eastern side with a man of about forty-six on it, and the light is standing where it was standing yesterday and where it stood on the first day, and the man of forty-six has a satchel and a stick and a list, and none of the three of those things is a trade, and the man of thirty-two with the limp and the ferrier's stick is nine hundred feet off in a lane and has been in this city nineteen days and has not been asked one question by anybody and is not going to be asked one today either, because the twentieth day is not the day anybody in this city asks anybody anything, and that is not a decision and has never been a decision, and a few hundreds of people in this city have not noticed it and about four have.**

He came down the road for about an hour with the light in his face and did not get the hour wrong, and a few of the people who came up that road in the two hours after him have said since that that is the first thing about him, that a man who has come down off a road in a place that keeps a standing hour either gets it wrong twice or gets it right at once, and that this one got it right at once and did not appear to be proud of it.

He had been on the road since before the light. That is the whole of what the road knows about him and it is more than this city knows about anybody who is not of it, because this city has been asked nothing for four hundred years and has answered nothing and has had nine thousand people in it, and a man who has been walking since before the light has been doing the only thing anybody in this city thinks is worth doing, which is working.

**And he is not a trader and he is not a man with a message and he is not anybody anybody has sent, and the first thing he said in this city was said to a man of about thirty with a barrow and a mule who was going up the hill and who did not stop his animal to hear it and who has repeated it since four times, and the four times have not improved on the first.**

“**I leave them,**” he said, when the man with the barrow asked what was in the satchel, and he said it the way a man says the price of something. “**Four hundred doors and I leave one at each and I go on to the next. That is all of it. I have never once been asked to fill one in and I have never once filled one in, and I have been doing it eight years and I have left three thousand and I could not tell you what is on the top of one of them.**”

“And if somebody fills one in.”

“**Then they have decided to and I do not ask them why, and about one door in nine fills one in and the other eight take it off me and put it somewhere and I do not see them again and that is what a form is for. A form is a thing a door can throw away. That is why it is printed and not written. A thing you write you keep.**”

The man with the barrow went up the hill. And a few of the people who came up that road in the two hours after it have said since that a man of forty-six who has left about three thousand forms at about three thousand doors in eight years and could not tell you what is on the top of one of them is the first person in nineteen days in this city who has said out loud the thing this city does and has never had anybody to say it to.

---

**And the paper in the satchel is not this city's paper, and that is the first thing anybody in a lane noticed, and it is the reason a man of thirty-two with a limp looked at it for four seconds on the twenty-first day and did not say anything, and the reason a man of thirty-four with lime on his hands put his own hod down six feet short at the end of a run and picked it up again while it was going on and has never mentioned it since.**

It is printed and not ruled, and it is printed both sides, and it is about the size of a broadside with a little less paper than a broadside, and the stock is a different sizing from anything four hundred people in this city have handled in a year, and the ink on it is not laid on and does not sit up off the sheet the way the thinnest glue in a yard at the bottom of a lane sits up off a strip of paper a foot long.

It is ruled in six columns. Five of them have a head on them and the sixth has no head at all and is the same width as the other five, and a man of thirty-two with a limp looked at that sixth column for four seconds on the twenty-first day and did not say a word about it, and a few of the people in that lane have said since that he looked at it the way a man looks at a bench with a rule on it, and that he has a page in a coat with a line on it that is not finished and that nobody in this city has ever seen the page and that the two of those are the only two things in this city that a stranger has looked at twice.

**And four of the six columns are ruled for a figure, and a figure is the only thing on all of that sheet that is the same shape in all four of them, and the man of forty-six did not know that and would not have said so and a man of thirty-two with a limp worked it out in a lane in about half a minute and has not written it down.**

---

**And the money was footed on the sill of a bench in a room the size of a cart on the first night and the addition was done by the woman who owned the room and not by him, and neither of them pretended it was a complicated sum, and it is the same sum that a man with a limp did in the same room on the first day of his own stay and the two of them did not know that and both of them said the same sentence about the money afterwards without either of them having heard the other.**

The room was two pence. Bread at the second hour was a penny. A second loaf, because he had come down off the rise since before the light and there is a road out of this country that is four days long if a man is not in a hurry, was a penny. Two and one is three, and three and one is four pence.

“**Four pence, and the room is at the second hour and not the ninth, and do not ask me for the ninth money,**” said the woman who owned it, and weighed the coin on a small stone balance and put the weight in a column in a book, and then said the other half of it, and she said it to a few of the people over the two days and did not know she was saying it twice. “**I have had a man in this room nineteen days with a stick and a coat and he has never once asked me what the weight was in the column for, and you have not either, and I would like it noticed that there are nine thousand people in this city and there is not one of them who has ever asked me that.**”

And the man of forty-six said no, and he was not in a position to say it well, and a few of the people in that room have said since that the answer of a man of forty-six who has walked down off a rise since before the light and cannot put down the two pounds of paper in his satchel was *no, and it is a good thing to be, in this city*, and that nobody there improved on it.

---

---

**And a city where recognition is the trade read a man of forty-six in a minute and a half and got four of the four wrong in the way that a market gets a stranger wrong, and none of the four was unkind, and the man of thirty-two with a limp, who was in the same street within a quarter of an hour of two of them, has said since that this is the only thing he has been in nineteen days that he is on the correct side of.**

Four of them said it and the four were about thirty apart in the street and none of them said it to the next one out loud, which is the arrangement in a city where a name in about four hundred heads is not a name and a name in one other head is a thing that can be carried to a gate.

The first was a woman of about fifty at a reed stall who said he was a man off a road. The second was a boy of about ten on a corner who said he was a soldier, and had been for about a year, and could not say whose, and was corrected by nobody and has not changed. The third was a man of about forty at a cart who said he was a man who sells lists, which was wrong, and a man of about thirty who sells names for a penny on that same street was within nine feet of him and heard it and said nothing. The fourth was a woman of about thirty with a pail who said nothing at all, and went on down the lane with the pail, and a few of the people in that lane have said since that the fourth description is the one that was correct and that the woman with the pail has given four of them in nineteen days and that two of the four have been right and that she has never once improved on any of them.

**And a man of thirty-two with a limp heard all four of them in about four minutes, and did not correct one, and a few of the people in that street have said since that the day before this one he was described four times in an hour and three of the four were right and the fourth was wrong about the future and he did not correct that one either, and that a man who has been in a city nineteen days and has been described eight times in two days and has not corrected any of them has worked out that correcting a description in this city is a thing you can only do if you know the true one, and that he does not.**

---

**And he was in the city for four days and nobody asked him one question, and a few of the people in the four places he stood in have said since that the twentieth day, the twenty-first, the twenty-second and the twenty-third were four days in this city in which nobody asked a man of forty-six anything at all, and that this was not a courtesy and was not a policy, and that the man of fifty with a waggon who fed a stranger for five days did not ask him a question and had a reason, and that a man of about thirty-four with lime on his hands put a hod down six feet short eleven times in an afternoon without once saying why, and that the reason in both of those cases was the same reason and it is the ordinary reason in a city where a man who is paid is a man somebody has had to write somewhere.**

He worked. That is what happened on the twentieth day and the twenty-first and the twenty-second and the twenty-third, and a few of the people in four places have said since that a man of forty-six with a satchel worked four days in this city and was fed at the second hour and at the ninth and was given a bed at night, and that he was not paid, and that nobody offered to pay him, and that the man of thirty-four with lime on his hands and the man of fifty with a waggon and the woman of about thirty with a pail did the same four things for four other men on the same four days and never once put any of it in a book, and that a man of thirty-two with a limp has been here nineteen days and has watched all of it and has still not worked out whether that is kindness or arithmetic, and that the two are the same thing and have been for four hundred years.

**He carried a hod eleven times on the twentieth day in a yard with a furnace in it and a man of thirty-four did not count them, and he carried a bundle of reed up from a lighter on the twenty-first, and he turned a drum on the twenty-second, and on the twenty-third he stood in a bindle yard at the bottom of a lane past the works and round by a store and watched a man of sixty-six at a bench under a roof cut a strip of paper to a line, and that is where the fourth day went, and a few of the people in that yard have said since that a man of forty-six stood in it for most of an afternoon and that nobody in that yard asked him one question either.**

---

**And on the twenty-first day a man of about thirty with a barrow and a mule came up the lane and saw a man of forty-six standing outside a yard with a satchel, and did not stop, and the man of forty-six said to the barrow-man, and the barrow-man has repeated it since without being asked and without improving on it, and the man of forty-six did not know he was saying the whole of the block.**

“I need a door.”

“**A man with a satchel needs about nine doors and has nine days for them, and there is one of them at the top of this lane with a step on it and a door that has never once asked anybody their name, and I have never been up it, and if you go up it do not thank him.**”

**And a man of forty-six went up a lane that goes up and not down on the twenty-first day, at the fourth hour, with a satchel, and put a printed sheet on a table in a long room with eleven people in it, and none of the eleven asked him what was in it, and the door did not ask him his name, and he said thank you to a boy of about fourteen on the step who told him where the man who made the paper was, and the boy told him not to thank him, and a few of the eleven in that room have said since that two strangers in three days have been told not to thank that boy and neither of them has understood why and the boy is fourteen.**

---

**And on the twenty-first day, in a lane, the light was standing where it had been standing since the first day, and a man of thirty-two with a limp stood in it with a stick and a coat and a page in the coat that says a person on its seventh line and has not been looked at since the ninth day, and a man of about thirty-four with lime on his hands was in a yard two hundred yards off putting a hod down six feet short at the end of a run and picking it up again, and a man of fifty-two was on a ladder going up the east wall of a glass works with a bucket in one hand and his other hand on the top of it, and nobody on that ladder looked down at anybody, and a few hundreds of people in this city did not know that a piece of paper about this city had been put on a table in a room at the top of a lane, and a few of them knew something about it, and the four of them were nine hundred feet away and two of them were the right nine hundred feet away and none of them was on a ladder.**

**And the man of about thirty with the barrow and the mule went up the hill with nine small glass things in straw and did not turn round, and a man of forty-six was in a lane with a satchel and a list of about four hundred places, and every one of the four hundred had a name on it and a district against the name, and three hundred and ninety-nine of the names were names of places, and the four hundredth was a word he had never heard in eight years of reading names of places and had never asked about and would not, and the district column on that line was filled in like all the others, and the ink was dry.**

**And he had nine days of the round left, and the last of them was a place he had never been to, and the road went there, and the market was four hundred yards long and open, and a man of forty-six came down off the rise before the light and went up a lane that goes up and not down and did not get the hour wrong once.**
