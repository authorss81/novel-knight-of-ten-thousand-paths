# Chapter 0215

**The sixth day, and the fifth hour, and a yard at the bottom of a lane past the works and round by a bindle store, and two hundred bundles of cord in it on racks with a roof on two sides and no roof on the other two, and a man of sixty-six at a bench under the roof who makes forms, and a man of thirty-two with a limp and a stick who had been told the name on a step and has come down four hundred yards to be told what the name is for.**

The bindles are the trade that pays for the yard and the forms are the trade that pays for the man, and a few people in that city have said since that those two facts have been true for about four hundred years and that nobody has ever put one of them near the other in a sentence, and that a yard in which a man of sixty-six makes paper nobody may keep, and beside him two hundred bundles of cord that anybody may buy is the ordinary shape of a place that has decided not to be met.

**And his name is Dermit Crane and the Dermit is a thing that happened to his mother's family and is in nobody's mouth, and he has been Dermit Crane for sixty-six years and has been asked about the whole of his working life by about four hundred people and has been asked about the first name by none of them, and about four of the people who have been in that yard have said since that a man who has never been asked his first name is not a man who is hiding it and is a man who has worked out that nobody in this city is going to ask him anything else either.**

He was working when the man with the stick came into the yard. He had a rule nine inches long and a strip of whalebone and a pot of the thinnest glue that has ever been made, and he was cutting a strip of paper to a line, and he did not stop, and four men on the rope have said since that a man of sixty-six who does not stop cutting a strip of paper for a stranger has either decided the stranger is not going to be answered or has decided he is going to be answered later, and that in this yard it is nearly always the second one.

Then he put the strip down and said, “You have been in the room upstairs this morning.”

“I have.”

“What did they tell you.”

“That they do not answer things and that what comes out of the room is a form and that you make it.”

**“Then you have been told all of it in one breath by eleven people, and I would like it noticed that it took them eleven people to do what I do in one, and that I am not saying that to be clever, because I do not think it is clever, it is only that I have been doing this for twenty years and they have been doing it for nineteen and neither of us is going to stop.**”

---

**And he has received every refusal this city has given for twenty years and entered them in a book, and the book is on the bench under the roof with a board over it, and the board is not off, and four people in that yard have said since that a man who puts a board over a book on a bench in an open yard in a city of nine thousand people is a man who does not think anybody is looking, and that this is the correct view and it is also the reason the board is there.**

“They say you keep a book,” said the man of thirty-two.

“I keep a book.”

“Of what.”

“Of what we say no to.”

**And then a man of sixty-six looked at a man of thirty-two with a limp and a stick and took four seconds over it, and four people in that yard have said since that those four seconds were the only time in the five days he had been here that anybody in that city took a second look at the stranger and that what went on in them was not suspicion and was arithmetic, and that a man of sixty-six was deciding whether the answer to the next question cost more than the question, and that he decided it did, because he answered it.**

“It cannot be shown to anybody,” he said. “**Not because it is against the city and not because it is anybody's business. Because a book that leaves this yard is a book that has been seen, and a book that has been seen is a reason somebody has for asking a second question, and there is nobody on earth who has ever asked a second question and then gone away.**”

“That is the reason it cannot be shown.”

“That is the reason it cannot be shown,” said the man of sixty-six, “**and it is the correct reason, and four people in this city have worked it out on their own in four hundred years, and I would like it noticed that it took them four hundred years and it took me twenty.**”

---

**And the finding, and it is the truest thing anybody has said in that yard, and it is about fifty words long and he said it once, to a stranger, at about the fifth hour of the sixth day, and four people in that yard have said since that he has not said it since and was not asked to and is not complaining about it.**

“**Nobody outside this place has ever been told why we say no to anything, ever. Nobody in here has noticed that either. I have got nobody to tell. I am not complaining about it and I am not going to be, because I have been on this bench twenty years and the bench is right.**”

And he went back to the strip of paper, and about four of the people who have been in that yard have said since that a man who says a thing like that and then goes back to a strip of paper has said the true thing and that the true thing in that yard is not a complaint, it is a finding, and that nobody in that city has ever entered a finding, and that this is the eleventh thing in twenty years that he has said in a yard and not in a book and that nine of the eleven were about paper.

---

**And then he made the form, and it took him four minutes, and a man of thirty-two with a limp watched every minute of it, and about four of the people who have been in that yard have said since that a man who has watched a form being made in four minutes has not been in an office in his life or has been in nine of them.**

The paper is the thinnest that can be had in that city and it is about a foot long and it is ruled in five lines, and the four lines are not for a person and the fifth one is empty when it is finished. He wrote with a pen that had been cut down and not with a quill, and he ruled it with the same rule and the same hand and the ink pot on that bench has never once been shut, and four of the eleven in the room upstairs have said since that the clerk's ink pot in that room is shut and the man who makes the forms in this yard has never shut his, and that those are two men in the same city keeping two different kinds of quiet.

He wrote eleven words and then he turned the paper round and showed it to a man of thirty-two with a limp, and four people in that yard have said since that he turned it round without being asked, which is the one thing in twenty years that every person who has ever come into that yard has said was the worst of him.

“**We do not give work to a person we cannot name,**” it said.

“That is eleven words.”

“That is eleven words,” said the man of sixty-six, “**and I have written about four hundred of them and every one of them is eleven and not one of them has been twelve, and I am not going to tell you why, and you would not be able to check it, and there is nobody in this city who could check it and about four people have asked.**”

“Where is the number.”

“What number.”

“The number of what. The number of us. The number of men asking. There is a column for it on nine forms out of ten that I have ever seen a man fill in.”

**“There is not one here,**” said the man of sixty-six. “**And I will tell you why short and then I am going to go back to my bench, and the reason is that a figure of persons is the only kind of paper that cannot be refused, and this one has to be refusable, or it is a lie with a stamp on it.**”

**And then the man of thirty-two asked the question that is the whole of the sixth day, and it took him four seconds to work out, and four people in that yard have said since that a man who worked out in four seconds that the reason a form cannot carry a name is that he has not given one, and that a man who works that out that fast has been in nine offices and that none of the nine was the one he was in.**

“And there is no name on it.”

“There is no name on it,” said the man of sixty-six, “**because you have not told me one. You came into my yard and you have not said your name and I have not asked you for it and I am not going to, and if you had told me your name it would not be on that paper either, because a piece of paper with a name on it is a piece of paper that can be asked about, and a piece of paper with no name on it is a piece of paper that can be asked about just the same and takes longer.**”

“You could have asked.”

“**I could have asked, and about four hundred people have asked me why I do not, and I have said the same thing to all four hundred, and I am not going to say it to you, and you would not be able to check it, and there is nobody in this city who could check it and about four people have asked.**”

And a man of thirty-two with a limp looked at eleven words on a piece of paper about a foot long with four ruled lines and a fifth one empty, and worked out inside a breath that he had been refused work by a piece of paper that could not name him because he had not given his name, and that the two of those were the same fact, and that nine people in that yard could have told him so and that not one of them did, and that the man of sixty-six had done all of it in four minutes without once looking at him while he worked out what had happened to him.

---

**And he asked what it cost, which was the ordinary question of a man who has been fed four days and given a piece of paper, and the man of sixty-six said that it cost nothing and then said four more things about the nothing, and about four of the people who have been in that yard have said since that the four things are the most careful thing anybody in that city has ever said about money and that not one of them was about money.**

“**Nothing,**” he said. “**A refusal is not a price and you are not a customer and if you were to offer me something for that paper I would have to take it, and then it would not be a refusal, it would be a sale, and this city does not sell anything to anybody, and that is not a thing it has got a rule about. It is a thing it has never needed a rule about.**”

**“And I will tell you the other half of it, because you have asked one question and got a straight answer and there are not many of those in this city. A man of fifty went up a pass nine days and came back down four and put something on a bench in a town two hundred miles off and was told to take it away, and he took it away and came back twice, and he has a figure for it and has not taken the figure and has not refused it. He has never been in this yard. If he had been in this yard he would have been given a form and it would have cost him nothing and he would have taken it away and come back twice and been right to.**”

**And the man of thirty-two with a limp stood in a yard with two hundred bundles of cord in it and found that he had been given a piece of paper about a foot long with eleven words on it and no number on it, and that a man of sixty-six had told him the reason a figure of persons is the only kind of paper that cannot be refused, and that the man had said the thing about the jar and the pass and the two visits because he wanted it said in a yard and not in a book, and about four men on the rope have said since that a man of sixty-six has been waiting years for somebody to say that to and that the somebody had a limp and a stick and did not know he was it.**

---

**And he folded it in four and put it in the inside pocket of a coat that already had a page in it, and that is all he did with it, and four people in that city have said since that a man came out of that yard with a refusal in his coat and carried it up the lane the way a man in a county four hundred miles off carries a fair copy and a working and a thing he made himself behind a kiln in a bag, and that in this city the possession of that paper is the only thing he has got that anybody in it knows about, and that it is a refusal, and that he has not shown it to a single person in the hour since he came out of that yard and has not said one word about it to anybody, and that he has taken it out twice.**

He took it out twice. Once at the bend in the lane where the wall comes up on the river side, and once in the room he was sleeping in, and the second time he put it on the bench beside the bench he was sleeping on and looked at it for two minutes, and a few people in that city have said since that a man who lays a piece of paper out on a bench to look at it is a man who is trying to see whether it is still true, and that it was still true, and that it had not changed in the two hours he had been carrying it, and that a form is not a thing that changes, and that this is the first thing anybody in that city has ever handed a stranger that the stranger did not immediately try to give back.

**And there is a second asking in him and he has not made it, and about four people in that city have said since that the man with the stick has not come back down the lane, and that a man of sixty-six has not mentioned a second asking to anybody, and that the man at that bench works from the second hour until the ninth and that it is the sixth day of a count the man with the stick does not have, and that a man who has been refused once and comes back is doing one of two things and that everybody in that yard has an opinion about which and that the man of sixty-six will not have it either.**

**And the yard at the end of that day, and what a yard it is, and four people in that city have said since that a man who has been in a glass yard and a bindle yard and a long room with a table in it has been in three of the nine places anybody in this city can be got into, and that the other six are a market, a quay, a wharf, a gate, an oven and a lane, and that a man of thirty-two got into nine of them in five days without being asked to and without being made to, and that this is not an achievement and is the ordinary way a man of thirty-two gets into a place.**

Then the rope came off the drum at the top end of the yard and two men walked it out to the rack and coiled it, and the coil went on the rack the way it had gone on the rack the day before, and the man of sixty-six put the board back over the book and covered the pot of glue and did not say goodnight to a stranger who was still standing in his yard, and about four people in that city have said since that the man of sixty-six says goodnight to nine people in that yard and has never once said it to a man who is leaving.
