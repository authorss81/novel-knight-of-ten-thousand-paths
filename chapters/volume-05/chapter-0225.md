# Chapter 0225

**The twenty-fifth day of a count of days that this city keeps from the last renewal, and the fourth hour, and a yard at the end of a lane past two gates and a slope, with a bench under a roof and a book on it that is about two inches thick, and a man of sixty-one at the bench with a pen, and a man of thirty-two with a limp and a stick at the mouth of that yard who came up a slope he has not climbed since the fifteenth day and has come up it because a figure went out of a city on the twenty-third in a bundle of cord and reed, the man of sixty-one has not put his hand on that book and gone back to work in a way anybody had seen before, and the reason has been in the city for two days and has not come down this lane, and there are about nine thousand people in it and a few of them have worked it out and three of the four are in a different yard and the fourth is a man with a limp.**

He had been at that bench nineteen years and the book had been in that yard for longer than he had. A man of sixty-one who has worked out in a year what a book is for and has never once been told it by anybody is not a man who is waiting to be told, and on the twenty-fifth day he was not waiting to be told anything, and he had a reason and the reason had been in a yard nine hundred yards off for two days and had come to him the ordinary way, which is that a man of thirty with a barrow and a mule had been down the lane on the twenty-third and had said one sentence at the top of a hill, and a man of thirty-four with lime had heard it from a man of about fifty at a cart and had said it to a woman of about thirty with a pail, and the woman had carried it four hundred yards down a lane on the twenty-fourth without knowing what she was carrying.

**And a man of sixty-one put the pen down about nine inches from the book and did not say anything for a minute and a half, the minute and a half is the longest a man of sixty-one has ever sat at that bench without doing anything, and about nine hundred children have put a hand on that bench in nineteen years, and he has not had a minute and a half like this for any of the nine hundred.**

“Do you know what a figure in a return is,” said the man of thirty-two with a limp.

“**No,**” said Osmund Cusk.

“**Then you do not need me to tell you and I am not going to. It went out of this city on the twenty-third in a bundle of cord and reed on a barrow, and there are four figures on it and all four of them are nine, and it is two hundred miles off now or near it, and there is a man in a town there who will read four figures off a printed sheet and put them in a book, and he will not know what they are, and neither will I, and I have not come up this slope to ask you a question about it.**”

**And a man of sixty-one at a bench turned the book round, and it went round about the width of a hand, and the second column came up off the bench. A man of sixty-one refused that column in four words on the fifteenth day of this count to a man of thirty-two with a limp and would not say why, and on the twenty-fifth day he turned it round without being asked twice and without saying why then either, and nobody in that yard has said a word about the ten days between the two, and the ten days are all of it, and a figure of refusals went out of a yard nine hundred yards off on the twenty-third and came up this lane on the twenty-fifth by a mouth.**

He put his hand flat on the second column the way a man puts a hand flat on a thing to see whether it is warm.

“**That is a hand,**” he said. “**And that is a day. And that is the third thing and I have never told anybody what the third thing is and I am going to tell a stranger because you did not ask me for it and because you have been refused twice in this city and came back and asked again on purpose, and because a figure went out of here on the twenty-fourth, which I know because a man of thirty with a barrow said so on a hill, and there is no version of what I am about to do where that is not the reason.**”

---

**And the second column is in two parts. A few of the people who have been in that yard did not know it was in two parts until the twenty-fifth day, and it has been in two parts for four hundred years, and a man of sixty-one has looked at it every working day for nineteen years and has not split it, and the reason a man does not split a thing he has looked at for nineteen years is that a man who splits a thing has admitted that it is a thing and not a habit.**

The first part is a hand. A mark, a line and a bar and a figure, the same as the mark that goes on the bench, and there are eleven of them and they are the same size and the same shape and they are not the same thing. A man of sixty-one has never in nineteen years written that sentence in a book and has said it out loud once and is not going to say it again.

The second part is a road.

**And the second part is written the way a road is written, which is from here outward, and a man of thirty-two with a limp who has spent four years with a road on a river four hundred miles off read eleven of them in a minute and a half. The eleven of them are not the same eleven, and he is right, and the routes are different, and every one of the eleven of them goes out of this valley and down the eastern side of it and across about a day of country and then into a place, and not one of the eleven names a place that anybody in this city can see from where they are standing.**

“Elven,” he said. “Eleven hands and eleven roads and four hundred years, and I have known for nineteen years that there were eleven of something and I have never known what, and I have not asked, and I am not going to tell you why I have not asked either.”

“Why not.”

“**Because a man who keeps a book for nineteen years and asks what is in it has admitted that he has not known what he was keeping,**” said Osmund Cusk, “**and then somebody gives him the answer, and then he has to decide whether to go on, and I have not got anybody in this city who could take it off me, and I have said that to you once and I am not going to say it twice.**”

---

**And then he said the other half of it and it is nineteen words and it is the second thing he has said out loud in nineteen years that was not about a book. Both of the two things he has said out loud in nineteen years were said to a man of thirty-two with a limp, and the man with the stick did not know on the fifteenth day that he was the person they were going to be said to, and he does not know now either, and four people in that yard have worked out that a man who says a thing once to a stranger and once to a second stranger has done it twice and not improved on it, and a man of sixty-one has improved on nothing in nineteen years, and that is the whole of what he has been doing.**

“**Nobody in this city has ever been told what those roads are and I have asked nobody and I am not going to ask you, and you are from off them and you have not come up a slope to tell me about them, and if you had I would not have written it down.**”

**And a man of thirty-two with a limp stood at a bench and did not say one word. A man of thirty-four with lime has been right about four men on a lane for twenty-five days, and the man with the stick has just been right about a man of sixty-one in a yard for the first time, and a few of the people in that yard know the difference between the two kinds of not saying and have not told him which is which.**

---

**And a child of nine years old came up the lane at about the fourth hour and a half and put its left hand flat on the bench and the mark was a line and a bar and a figure and it was not a scar and it was not ink, and the man of sixty-one turned the book round about the width of a hand and wrote one line and took the hand off the bench in the way a man takes a wet thing off a table, and the child went back down the lane, and a few of the people who have been in that yard have said since that the second column was still open on the bench when the child's hand came down on it, and that a man of sixty-one turned a book round and then turned it back and then turned it round again inside a minute and a half and that the turning round the second time is the whole of the twenty-fifth day and nobody in that yard has asked him about it and he has not offered.**

The child was about nine and the mark on its hand is the same shape as the eleven on the second column. A man of sixty-one has written eleven of them in nineteen years and a boy of about nine has put down about nine hundred, and the two figures are not being added together by anybody in that yard, and a man of thirty-two with a limp added them in his head in a second and a half and has not written it down and is not going to, and adding a book and a hand is not a thing anybody in this city has ever done because nobody in this city has ever needed to and because a name in a book is a person somebody can be shown and a hand is not a name.

---

**And a man of fifty with a waggon was at a quay at the second hour of the twenty-fifth day and a man of about thirty-four with lime was at a bench in a yard at the same hour, and between them and a man of fifty-two on the east wall of a works three men in this city know the names of seven of the eleven places. None of the three has ever said the name of a place in front of the other two, and a few of the people in a city of about nine thousand have said since that this is the ordinary way a thing travels in a city that has never written it down, and that the thing has been travelling for four hundred years out of about nine thousand mouths and about four hundred ears, and that a few hundreds of people in this city have been to one of the eleven and about nine hundred know the name of one of the eleven, and that neither of those two figures has ever been added to the other, and that a few of the people in this city worked that out on the twenty-fifth day and that four of them are the two men and the man of thirty-two with a limp, and that a man of thirty-two with a limp was in none of the three places and has not been added to anything and is not going to be.**

**And a man of thirty-two with a limp, who has been in this city twenty-five days, has now been given the name of one of the eleven places nine times over by nine people in a market in one quarter of an hour and has been given it a tenth time by a man of about thirty-four with lime in a yard. A man who has been in a city twenty-five days has been told one name ten times and can use none of it, and no man in that city has been told anything at all. Nobody in that city has a book with them in it except a book about two inches thick under a roof at the end of a lane past two gates with a second column in it, and a man of thirty-two with a limp has not been in that yard since the fifteenth day and has not asked for the second column and has been told about it twice, and the man with the stick has now been shown the same book twice in ten days by two men who have never met, and a person can be told a thing once and can remember it, and this city has no arrangement at all for the second telling, and this is why a man of thirty-two with a limp is standing at a bench with a stick he cannot lean on and a page in a coat he has not looked at since the twenty-third and has not written on since the twenty-third.**

**And the first column is a count of days from the last renewal, and the count in that book and the count in a book nine hundred yards off in a yard at the bottom of a lane have a fold in them in the same place, and two men who have known about each other's count for nine years because of a fold have never said so. A man of thirty-two with a limp asked about the first column on the fifteenth day and was told all of it in one sentence, and a man of sixty-one has told a stranger about a book nine hundred yards off that he has never spoken to and cannot speak to, and the fold is the reason, and the fold is four inches from the bottom of a page, and a fold is a thing a man gets by opening a book at the same place every day for about nineteen years, and the two men in that city have been opening two books at the same place every day for nine years and have never once compared them and are not going to.**

**And on the twenty-fifth day a man of thirty-two with a limp went back down the slope at about the fifth hour and stood at the bottom of it for about two minutes. He stood at the bottom of a slope for two minutes the way a man stands at the bottom of a slope when he has been told something he cannot use, and about nine thousand people in this city go up and down that slope every day and four of them have ever stopped on it, and a man of thirty-four with lime has stopped on it twice in nineteen years to look at a barrow that came off it wrong, and those are the only two kinds of stopping anybody in this city has a word for, and neither of them is the kind a man of thirty-two was doing, and he has not told anybody and is not going to.**

**And what he could not use he worked out at about the fourth hour and a half of the twenty-fifth day, and he has not written it down and is not going to, and it is about eleven words, and it is the eleventh time in twenty-five days that something has been said in this city which is about a man and not about a place, and none of the eleven was said to him. Eleven roads in four hundred years is not a great many. It is a little more than one every thirty years, and a city that had a road out of it every thirty years is not a city anybody could find. A man of thirty-two with a limp worked that out in nine seconds at the bottom of a slope, and he also worked out that the number eleven is not a number anybody in this city chose by accident, and a woman of sixty told him why eleven in a room on the sixth day and will not tell him again, and a man of sixty-one in a yard has now told a stranger the same thing in nineteen words about a book and does not know he has said it, and the two of those facts are in two yards nine hundred yards apart on the same day and nobody has joined them and nobody is going to.**

---

**And the lamp was standing where it was standing and a man of fifty-two was going up and down a ladder on the east wall of the works and had not been asked by anybody in this city in nineteen years what the bucket is for, and a few hundreds of people in this city had not worked out that a book in a yard at the end of a lane had been turned round on the twenty-fifth day, and about four of them had, and one of those four is a man of thirty-two with a limp standing at a bench with a line in his palm that a man of sixty-one looked at for a minute and a half ten days ago and said was not one of theirs, and that he has not joined the two, and that he has not been able to stop looking at the second column since the fourth hour, and that a few of the people in that yard have noticed that and none of them has said anything and one of them is a man of sixty-one who has looked at about nine hundred hands on that bench and has never found anything on one of them, and who found nothing on that one either, and who has not said so.**
