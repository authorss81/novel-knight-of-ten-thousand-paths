# Chapter 0218

**The fifteenth day, and the fourth hour, and a yard at the end of a lane past two gates and a slope, with a bench under a roof and a book on it that is about two inches thick and a man of sixty-one sitting at the bench with a pen, and a child of nine years old at the low end of the lane with a bundle on one shoulder, and a man of thirty-two with a limp and a stick at the mouth of the yard who came to find out whether anybody in this city would explain one thing to him, and did not, twice, and is going to be asked it a third time in four days and is not going to be told then either.**

The yard is not Crane's yard. That is the first thing a stranger gets wrong about a city of nine thousand people with a count of days and no months, and four people in that yard have said since that a man off a road walked past the right yard on the sixth day and did not know it was the wrong one, and that both yards are yards and both have a bench under a roof and one of them makes paper and the other one keeps it, and that a man who has been in the first for four minutes can tell the difference from the outside, and that a man who has not, cannot.

**The book is in two columns and the first column is a count of days and the second column has a head on it, and the head is one plain word meaning carried and is not a trade and is not a job, and four people in that yard have said since that nobody has ever put a name in either column in four hundred years, and that a stranger who asked about that would be the fourth to ask in years, and that the first three were all wrong about what the question was.**

**And it is the only book in this city that is about the outside, and a few people in that yard have said since that a city of about nine thousand people with a market and a wharf and a glass works and a council of eleven has two of them, one of which is a book of things this city has refused and the other is this one, and that neither has ever been shown to anybody, and that the reason for the first is that showing it is asking, and that the reason for the second has never been written down by anybody including the man who keeps it.**

The paper is about the thinnest that can be had and the ruling is in two lines and not in five, and the ink is the same ink that is in the yard at the bottom of the lane, and four people in that yard have said since that the man of sixty-six buys his glue and his paper in the same building, that the two of them have never spoken, and that two men in one city who have never spoken to each other have been using the same pot of glue for nineteen years.

“What is the book.”

“The book,” said the man of sixty-one.

“Of what.”

“Of the marks.”

“Then what is a mark.”

“**No,**” said Osmund Cusk, and did not look up, and four people in that yard have said since that this is the only answer anybody in this city has ever given to that question, that it has been given by four different men in four hundred years, and that not one of the four has ever said anything else.

His name is Osmund Cusk and he is sixty-one and he has been at this bench for most of his working life, and the book has been in this yard for longer than he has, and a few people in that city have said since that the book was here before him and that he was put in front of it at about nineteen and that nobody told him what it was for and that he worked it out in a year and that nobody has ever confirmed it.

“I have been here nineteen years and I have not been told what a mark is,” he said, “**and I have not asked, and that is not because I am frightened of the answer. It is because a man who keeps a book for nineteen years and asks what is in it has admitted that he has not known what he was keeping, and then somebody gives him the answer, and then he has to decide whether to go on.**”

“So you know.”

“**I know what goes in it. I do not know what it is.**”

“The first column.”

“**Days,**” said Osmund Cusk. “**Since the last renewal. And there are three of us who keep that count and two of us are in this city all the time and the third is a man of sixty-six at the bottom of a lane who has never told me he keeps one, and neither have I told him, and I have known about it for nine years because his book has got a fold in it in the same place mine has.**”

“Nobody here counts days.”

“**Nobody here counts days,**” said the man of sixty-six, “**and two men count them and neither of the two has ever been asked to stop, and about four people in this city have wondered about that over four hundred years and I have never wondered about it once, because I am busy.**”

---

**And he asked about the second column and a man of sixty-one put his hand flat on the page and left it there, and what he said while it was there is all anybody in this city will say about it, and four people in that yard have said since that a man of sixty-one has answered this question four hundred times, briefly, and that not one of the four hundred has been the same four words.**

“The word on the head of it means carried,” said the man of thirty-two.

“**It does.**”

“And it is not a job.”

“**It is not a job and it is not a trade and there is nobody in this city whose work it is, and there has not been one since before the oldest man in this yard was born, and if you want to know what is in that column you have to ask me for it and I have been asked four times in nineteen years and I have said no four times and one of the four was a man who had come up a pass.**”

**And then he said the other half of it, and it is in a man's own words and it is short and it is the only time in nineteen years that he has said it to anybody, and four people in that yard have said since that a man of thirty-two with a limp has now been told by four different people in this city that a thing is not his business, that all four said it differently, and that not one of them was unkind.**

“**The word on the head of it is what we do to a thing and not what the thing is, and I have never known who wrote it and there is a scratch on the second leaf about the width of a thumbnail that I have looked at about nine hundred times and it is a thumb and it is not a mark, and that is all I have ever been able to say about anything in this book and I have never said it out loud.**”

---

**And the child came up the lane at about the fourth hour and a half, and it came the way a child comes when it is doing a thing it has done many times, and a man of sixty-one at a bench put the pen down and waited, and did not speak to it, and did not ask it anything, and four people in that yard have said since that a man of sixty-one has watched about nine hundred children go up that lane and has not spoken to one of them, and that the figure is in his head and has never been on paper, and that it is the only count in his life he has refused to enter.**

The child came to the bench and put its left hand flat on it, and the mark was a line and a bar and a figure and it was not a scar and it was not ink, and the man of sixty-one turned the book round about the width of a hand, and wrote one line, and put the child's hand off the bench in the way a man takes a wet thing off a table, and the child went back down the lane.

“He does not say anything to it.”

“He never says anything to any of them,” said the man of sixty-one. “**They put the hand down and the hand comes up and that is the arrangement and it has been the arrangement for four hundred years and it is not a ceremony and there is nobody in this city who has ever thought it was one.**”

“And the second column.”

The man of sixty-one put his hand flat on the page over the second column, and left it there, and four people in that yard have said since that a man of thirty-two with a limp understood inside the first second that he was not going to be shown it and was not going to ask, and that both of those had been decided by the hand and not by the man.

“**Not today,**” said Osmund Cusk. “**And I am not going to tell you why not today, because the why is a thing I have not worked out, and a man of thirty-two has been in this city a fortnight and I have not got a use for him.**”

---

**And the want, and it is said once, in about nine seconds, in his own words, in a yard, on the fifteenth day, and four people in that city have said since that he had never said it to anybody in nineteen years, and that he said it to a stranger and did not go back over it, and that four of them have asked him since whether he has ever said it twice, and he has not, and that not asking him is the fourth time in nineteen years this city has done the correct thing to a man who keeps a book.**

“**I have wanted for most of my working life for somebody to come and tell me to stop keeping this,**” said Osmund Cusk. “**Nobody has. It is not that nobody can. It is that there is no one in this city who can, and I have never once said that to anybody, and I have said it to you because you asked me what is in it and because you are a stranger and will be gone.**”

He said it and went back to the pen, and four people in that city have said since that the sentence contains a want and a reason and a fact about himself, and that the three of them are in nine seconds, and that a man who can do that has been thinking about it for nineteen years and not for nine seconds.

“Then somebody can stop you.”

“**No, because stopping me is an office and there is no office here that does that, and if there was one it would have to be a person somebody appointed, and nobody in this city appoints anybody to anything, and I have not asked why in nineteen years and neither has anybody else, and you are the first person to walk into that yard in years and ask me anything and you did not ask me that.**”

---

**And a man of thirty-two with a limp put his own left hand on that bench, palm up, at about the fifth hour, and did not say anything about it, and a man of sixty-one looked at it for about a minute and a half and said nothing at all, and four people in that yard have said since that a man of thirty-two with a limp has a thing in his palm, a black line healed into the crease, a foot long, running under the skin and not stopping at the edge of it, and that a man of sixty-one has looked at about nine hundred hands on that bench and has never once found anything on one of them, and that he looked at this one for a minute and a half and found nothing, and that a minute and a half is four times longer than he takes.**

“It is not one of yours,” said the man of thirty-two.

“**No,**” said Osmund Cusk.

“Then it is not the same.”

“**It is not the same. There is no same. There is a book and there is a bench and there is a hand that comes down the lane and puts itself on the bench about nine hundred times in a year, and you have a line in your palm and I have a book, and there is nothing in this city that joins those two, and if there were one of us would have found it, and I have been looking for nineteen years.**”

**And that is all that was said about it, and it is correct, and four people in that city have said since that a man of thirty-two came to a yard with a mark in his hand and was told in nine seconds that it is not one of theirs, and that he asked a man for the confirmation of a thing he had worked out himself, and that this is the first time anybody in this city has watched him do it.**

---

**And he asked the fourth question and got the fourth straight answer and the four of them together are about eleven words, and four people in that yard have said since that a man of thirty-two with a limp has been in this city a fortnight, has asked four questions, has been told no four times in nine words each, and has not once told a man of sixty-one that he is being difficult, and four men in a glass yard on the other side of this city have said the same thing about him and have not heard of each other and never will.**

“The lamp,” he said. “The ladder. The gate. The mark in the book. Four things and a man has said no to all four of them and it is the same four words each time and I would like to know whether that is a coincidence.”

“**That is the fifth question and it is the first one in fourteen days that was not about a thing,**” said Osmund Cusk, and put the pen down, and looked at a man of thirty-two for the first time in about four minutes, and four people in that yard have said since that a man of sixty-one looked up and that this was the whole of the visit and everything before and after it was the yard.

“It is not a coincidence and it is not a wall,” he said. “**It is one man. It is one man who has been at this bench for nineteen years and who has said the same short thing four hundred times and got the same answer four hundred times, and the reason it is the same answer is that the answer has never been a decision. There is no decision in this yard. There is a bench and a book and a man, and a man does not decide. A man is here.**”

He picked the pen up.

“**And that is all I have to say to a stranger, and I have said it short, and I have said nine of those sentences in nineteen years and not one of them was longer and not one of them was to somebody who came back.**”

---

**And the child came down the lane at about the ninth hour and a man of thirty-two with a limp was at the mouth of a yard and a man of sixty-one was at a bench with a book about two inches thick, and neither of them said anything to the child, and the child had a bundle on one shoulder and a mark on one hand and no name that anybody in that city has ever used, and four people in that city have said since that a child of nine goes down that lane and a man of sixty-one writes one line and a man of thirty-two does not ask, and that this is the arrangement and has been for four hundred years, and that a man of thirty-two with a limp walked out of that yard at the ninth hour knowing less about a mark than he had known at the fourth hour, and carrying two refusals and a page in a coat and a line in his palm that is not one of theirs.**
