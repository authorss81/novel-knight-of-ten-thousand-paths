# Chapter 0214

**The sixth day, and the fourth hour, and a lane that goes up and not down, and a building at the top of it with one door and a step worn in the middle of it, and eleven people in a long room on the ground floor, and a man of thirty-two with a limp and a stick standing on the step at the fourth hour of the sixth day of a count of days that this city keeps from the last renewal and that has not given him a number, and he is making the first asking of his life in a city that has been asked nine times in four hundred years and has said no nine times, and he does not know that, and nobody in the room is going to tell him, and about four of the eleven are going to notice that he does not know it.**

The step is worn in the middle because the door is the door and people have gone through it for a very long time, and the wear is a hollow four inches across and two deep, and a man of thirty-two looked at it and understood that it was not a step anybody had built and that it was a step everybody had made, and a few people in that city have said since that a man who looks at a worn step instead of at a door is a man who has been counting things for four years and is going to have a bad morning.

Nobody at the door asked him anything. That is the first fact about the room and the whole of that morning and four of the eleven in it have said since, and none of them said it in the room, that in four hundred years the door of that room has been the only door in this city that has never once asked a man his name, and that it does not do that out of manners, and that the reason is that a door that asks is a door that remembers.

> *Nothing here is owed to you and nothing here has refused you.*
> *You have not been told which of the two you are in.*

He did not write it down. He has had four of those in four years and has written one down, and the one he wrote down has no hour against it and no name in it and he cannot account for where it came from and nobody four hundred miles off can tell him, and there is nobody in that room he could ask.

---

**And there are eleven, and eleven is not a bench, and the room is a long room with a table down the middle of it and eleven chairs on the long sides and nothing on the walls, and a clerk of forty at the near end with a book and a pen and no ink pot open, because the ink pot is shut and has been shut since he came in.**

He was not asked to sit down. He was not asked to stand. He was left standing at four feet from the end of the table for two minutes while a woman of about sixty finished a sentence she had started before he came in, and about four of the eleven in that room have said since that the two minutes were the correct two minutes and that a man who interrupts a woman of sixty in that room has been told more about that city in two minutes than a man who waits gets in a fortnight, and that he waited.

Then the woman of sixty looked up and said, “Well.”

“I have come down a road to ask for work,” said the man of thirty-two.

“**Everybody who comes up that lane says that,**” said the woman of about sixty, “**and about half of them have not, and the half that have not say something else and I have never once been told which is which and I have stopped keeping the count of the two because it is a count of refusals and not a count of people and the two are not the same and a clerk will not put either of them in a book.**”

And the clerk of about forty said, “That is entered,” and entered it, and a man of thirty-two with a limp stood in a room with eleven people in it and watched a sentence about refusals go into a book, and four of the eleven have said since that the clerk entered it because the woman told him to and that the woman tells him to enter things that are not about the business of the morning about four times a year, and that this is the fifth, and that nobody in that room has ever asked her why.

**And the book on the table is not a minute and the clerk of forty has never called it one and has been asked twice in nineteen years and has said no twice in nine seconds each, and the difference is a thing anybody in that room could explain in a sentence and about four of the eleven have said since that not one of them has ever explained it, and that the explanation is that a minute holds what was said and this book holds what was asked, and that a man who is in a room being asked something is a different animal from a man who is in a room having said something, and that the second one can be got at in forty years and the first one cannot.**

The clerk did not read his four lines back. A clerk of about sixty in a county four hundred miles off reads a minute back twice, and the second reading is slower and not better, and has done it for nineteen years, and about four of the eleven in that room have said since that a man who came down a road to a room and was not read back to is a man who has been in a hall somewhere and has not been in this one, and that the difference between the two rooms is what a man learns in four hundred miles.

**And what was in the book, and what was not in it, is the ordinary working of that room and is not a mystery to anybody in it. In the book: the day, put in as a count and not as a date, because the clerk has a column with a head on it and the head is a number, and the number on this day was the same as the number on the day before and a man of thirty-two with a limp did not know what it was and did not ask. And what was asked, and who asked it, and nothing else.**

“And the day,” said the man of thirty-two. “You have put a number against today and I do not know what it is.”

“**It is the sixth,**” said the clerk of forty, “**since the last renewal, and there are three places on a wall in this city that have it on them and one of them is in this lane and you walked past it and did not read it because a man who is going to be asked something does not stop to read a number on a wall, and I have never once in nineteen years seen a man stop.**”

“And the renewal.”

The clerk shut the ink pot, which was the end of that subject and was the end of the morning, and a few of the eleven have said since that a clerk of forty shuts an ink pot at the point where a question is going to cost more than it is worth and that he has done it nine times a week for nineteen years and that it is the only speed in that room.

“How many of you are there,” said the man of thirty-two.

“Eleven.”

“Why eleven.”

**And the woman of sixty put her pen down, and four of the eleven in that room have said since that she put it down and not the clerk, and that it has been the clerk's pen all morning and hers since two heartbeats before he asked, and that the two seconds were all it cost her to answer and that she gave them out loud.**

“**Because nine is the number you use,**” she said. “**Where you are from, where anybody is from, a thing that counts is nine. Nine men, nine days, nine of something, and it is a good number and it is our number, and any office anywhere that wants to sound careful counts to nine and it does not mean a thing. We had nine for a hundred and forty years and every man who came up this lane knew exactly what nine meant, and every man who came up this lane wrote nine in a book when he got home, and after forty years there were nine men in four counties who had written the same number for the same reason and not one of them knew that the number came from us. Eleven is a number that means eleven.**”

She picked the pen up.

“**And that is all there is to it and I have said it once and I am not going to say it again, and you are a stranger and you are the fourth one anybody here can remember and the other three did not get this far.**”

---

**And he asked about work and he was not answered about work, and he was not refused about work, and both of those happened in four minutes, and a man of thirty-two with a limp understood inside nine seconds that nothing in that room was going to be an answer and that the room was not built to be a place where answers are given.**

They did not refuse him. That is the thing about the sixth day and four of the eleven in that room have said since, and none of them said it in the room, that nobody in that room had ever refused anybody in it, and that the reason a man is put down for refusing a thing is a form and not a minute and that the form is in a different building and is made by a man who is not one of the eleven and does not sit at that table and cannot be asked anything by anybody in it.

The woman of about sixty said, “We do not answer things.”

“Then what do you do.”

“**We are the only eleven and we are here so that a thing can be asked in a room and not in a market, and that is a real service and I have done it for nineteen years, and what comes out of that room is not a yes or a no, it is a form, and the form is correct, and it is made by a man called Crane and he is not one of us and he does not sit at this table and I have never once in nineteen years been asked to explain his forms and I have wanted to four hundred times.**”

“**And you cannot ask me to explain them, either,**” said the clerk of about forty, to nobody, in the voice of a man who has said that sentence before in that room and is not complaining, and said it short.

---

**And a man of thirty-two with a limp asked the wrong question twice in that room and got the correct answer to it both times, and about four of the eleven have said since that the two wrong questions were the two questions any stranger asks, and that the correct answers were short, and that neither of the answers was about the lamp or the ladder or the light, and that a man who asks about the light in that room is a man who has not understood that the light is not the city's business.**

“What is it for,” he said. Meaning the road he had come down, meaning the standing hour, meaning the market street and the four descriptions in it.

“You are asking me what a thing is for,” said the woman of about sixty. “**I have been asked that in nineteen years by nine people and I have got an answer ready for it and the answer is that it is not for anything, and that a thing which is not for anything is kept going by the people doing it and not by the reason for it, and if the reason goes the people are still there, and if the people go the reason is worth nothing, and I have never in nineteen years found out which of those two happens first and neither have you.**”

“And if I do not work.”

“Then you will not be fed by a man with a waggon, and you will be fed by a woman with a loaf, and that is not an insult, and it is a worse arrangement for you and a better one for us and both of those are the ordinary way round.”

**And the man of thirty-two with a limp stood in that room and found that he had been given all of it in one breath by a woman of sixty who had not once raised her voice and had not once improved on anything, and a clerk of about forty entered four lines and did not read them back, and about four of the eleven in that room have said since that the man with the stick went out of that door at about the fifth hour and stood on a step with a hollow worn four inches across in it and did not go down the lane for about a minute and a half.**

---

**And on the way down the lane a boy of fourteen who was sitting on the step said, without getting up, that the man who kept the forms was at the bottom of the lane past the works and round by the yard with the bindles, and that his name was Crane, and that it was the fifth hour now and that the man was at his bench and would be there until the ninth, and that there was a board over a thing on that bench and that nobody was to touch it.**

“Thank you.”

“**Do not thank me,**” said the boy of about fourteen, and went back to what he was doing, which was nothing, and a few people in that city have said since that a boy of fourteen on a worn step will tell a stranger where the man who keeps the forms is and will not shake his hand, and that both of those are the ordinary courtesy of that door and that the door has never once asked him for anything back.

---

**And he went down four hundred yards of lane with the light not moving and stopped at a bend where a wall came up on the river side, and he took a page out of the inside pocket of a coat, and the page was torn out of something and folded in three and soft at the folds, and there were nine lines on it and an hour against each of the nine, and the seventh line said a person and had been looked at twice and had not been finished.**

He did not finish it. That is a fact of the sixth day and a few people in that city have said since that a man stood at a bend in a lane for four minutes with a page in his hand and did not write on it, and that nobody in that city has ever seen him write, and that a man who carries a page in a country that does not keep pages is the only person in it holding anything that could be used against this city by anybody who ever gets hold of it, and that nobody has got hold of it, and that this is the only protection this place has ever had and it is a man being careful.

He put it back in three folds. The word on the page was a place, and the place was four hundred miles off, and the page had a date on it in a fair hand that he did not look at and did not write down, and it was thirty-five days old on the day a child handed it to him, and he had read all of it once in under half a minute in a lane in another county and had put it away in a hurry, and about four people in that county know the word and as many have not and nobody in this city knows it at all, and no one in this city is going to be told it by him.

**And four questions went down the lane with him and he did not ask one of them in a room with eleven people in it, and about four of the eleven have said since that the stranger with the stick did not ask about the lamp, and did not ask about the ladder, and did not ask what the city was for, and did not ask why the door does not ask names, and that the four of those are the four that every man off a road has asked in that room in four hundred years, and that he had had four hours with eleven people and had not asked one of them, and that the clerk of about forty wrote that down because the clerk writes down everything and it is in the book on the table on the side where the number is.**
