# Chapter 0211

**The first day of a count that this place keeps from the last renewal, and the second hour, and a road coming down off the eastern side with a man of thirty-two on it with a limp and a stick, and about nine thousand people on a river about a mile and a half off, and no night in it, and the light has come up out of the east and is standing four fingers above the ridge of the far hills and has not moved since he came over the last of the rise, and it is going to stand there until the end of the day, and there is no part of this sentence that anybody in that city would put in their own mouth.**

He came down the road for about a mile with the light in his face and got the hour wrong twice before he stopped trying, and both of the times he got it wrong were because he tried to work it out of the sky, and there is nothing in that sky to work it out of. That is the first thing and there is nothing else on the first day that comes before it. Sixteen weeks and something in a valley where there was no hour to work out and no way of being wrong about one, and a man whose hand had been trained for a year and a half in a place that kept no hours walks into a place that keeps a standing one, and tries to read an hour off a light that does not move, and is wrong twice before anybody tells him anything.

**The first of the two was about a quarter of an hour down the hill, and he had the ridge on his left and the sun, or what he took for the sun, high and forward and a little to the right, and he made it the ninth hour because a man going into the ninth hour has a long shadow in front of him and he had a long shadow in front of him. It was the second hour. A carter coming the other way with a load of reed told him it was the second hour by pointing at a bread oven two streets off with a handle on a pole, and the oven was hot, and the man did not stop his animal to do it.**

The second of the two was at the bottom of the hill at the foot of the paved lane, and he made it the fourth hour out of pure habit, because the fourth hour is the hour a road gets walked and not the hour a man gets told anything, and a man with a limp who has come down a hill in about an hour and a half is at the fourth hour in any country that has one. It was the third hour and the shade under the eaves on the east side had not gone anywhere near the pavement, and a woman carrying a pail told him the hour by saying the bread would be out in a minute and that he could wait for it if he liked, which is a thing a woman says at the third hour and not at the fourth.

The man who told him was a man of about thirty with a barrow and a mule and a string of small glass things in straw, and he had been coming up the road for an hour and a half in the other direction, and he stopped the mule about ten yards off and looked at him and said, “You have been walking since the light came.”

“No,” said the man of thirty-two with the limp. “I have been walking since the fourth hour.”

“What is that.”

“That is the fourth hour.”

**“We do not keep hours here,” said the man with the barrow, and he said it the way a man says the price of something. “We keep days. From the last renewal. It is on the wall in three places and none of them is for you. A day is a day and that is the whole of it, and if you want a number, go and ask somebody, and do not ask me, because I do not know the number and neither does my mule.”**

And then he said the other thing, and he said it without being asked and in the same tone, and a few of the people who came up that road in the two hours after it have said since that the two of those sentences said on a hill road with a barrow standing still, is the ordinary speech of that place and that the man with the barrow did not think either of them was worth stopping a mule for.

“**And there is a book in this city with a figure of months written in it,” he said, “and it is not ours, and whoever wrote it had months where he came from, and it is the only page in four hundred years that has got the word on it, and we do not use it and we do not argue about it.**”

Then he took the mule past him and went up the hill, and the man of thirty-two with the limp stood in the road with a stick in his hand that is a ferrier's stick, ash, four feet nine, cut out of a fence rail in a county that is not on any map, for four hundred yards of road and nothing else, and watched him go.

---

**And the light has not turned and it is not going to, and it has not turned for four hundred years, and there is a word for it in this city and the word is short and it is the plainest one there is and it is a thing a man would say about a lamp in a house, and nobody here has ever made it into anything else.**

The word is the lamp.

A woman of about fifty at a doorway on a lane said it to him before anybody had said anything else to him at all, and she said it because he had stopped in the lane and was looking up, and what he was looking up at was the light, and she came out and said the word as though she had been asked.

“**That is the lamp,” she said. “It comes up in the east and it stands. That is all it does and that is all it has done as long as anybody can go back on a list. Do not stand in that lane looking at it, you are holding up a barrow.”**

And she went back in, and there were four people on that lane at that hour and two of them had not looked up at all, and a few of them have said since that the fact that two people on a working lane had not looked up at the light is the only fact anybody has ever had about the light that is not a story.

**And the light does not turn, and the reason it does not turn is not the question anybody in that city asks about it, and the question has been asked from outside, and every man who has asked it has been told the word and not the reason, and one of them put a jar on a bench in a town two hundred miles off and was told to take it away and took it away and came back twice, and neither of those two things is a thing that happened here.**

The lane is paved. That is the second thing and it took him about a quarter of an hour to understand it and he never said so out loud. Four years of gravel. Five months of a bridge deck and a lane over a bridge that carries salt twice a day. A mile of paved lane with a kerb on each side and a drain running under the edge of it and a man who could put a barrow anywhere he liked and could not make it go fast anywhere at all.

**And a man who has spent four years learning where a wheel catches has nothing to learn and a man who has spent four years learning where a foot slips has about nine days of it in a place that is flat, and by the ninth day in that city he was walking the way he walked on a gravel road, which is to say he was putting his weight down before he had decided to, and about four people in that city noticed that in nine days and not one of them said anything about it, and one of them, a woman of about thirty at a stall, said a thing about it on the fourth day that was not about his leg and that he did not understand until the sixth.**

The stick was the difficulty and it was a real one and it was not a joke at anybody's expense, and there is a trade fact in it and a few of the men at the top of that lane saw it in about a minute and a half and one of them said it out loud to the next one and the next one did not improve on it.

“**He is off a road,**” said a man of about forty at a cart. “Look at the stick. Nobody off a road carries a stick like that. A man off a road carries a stick because a stick is for four hundred yards of gravel and there is not one yard of gravel in this city. It is a description, that is what it is. **You can tell where he is from with that thing hanging off him and you would not need to ask, and I have never in my life wanted to know less about anybody than I want to know about that stick.**”

“Which is where he is from,” said the second man.

“Which is nowhere I have ever been,” said the first man, and went on with his cart.

**And he could not put it down. That is all the lane did to him and it took him until the second afternoon to admit it to himself and he has told nobody. Leaning on it puts the weight through the wrong hip, and so it is a thing to be carried and not leaned on, and a man on a lane in a city cannot carry a stick for eleven hours, and if he puts it down in a street then a stranger can pick it up, and if a stranger can pick it up then a stranger has something of his that is worth four hundred yards of somebody else's country, and about four men in that city on the first and second days said out loud that a man carrying a stick like that in a paved lane looks like a man carrying somebody else's road into a place that does not want it, and not one of them said it unkindly and not one of them said it twice.**

---

**And the river is a mile and a half off and it is used and it is not beautiful, and there are wharves on it and the wharves are working, and a barge came up at the second hour and was three-quarters unloaded by the third and the men on it were doing a job and did not look at a stranger.**

That was the third thing and it is the thing a man from a valley notices first and a man from a county does not notice at all. **Nobody on that wharf stopped. Not one man in a hundred looked up. In a market town a stranger is a thing that happens twice a day and there is a man who does nothing but look up. Here a stranger came up the middle of a quay carrying a stick and about four hundred people worked past him and not one of them turned their head, and a few of the men on that wharf have said since that it is not that they do not notice things, because they notice everything in a boat-load, it is that a stranger is not a thing that requires a decision and they have four hundred years of not having to decide about one.**

A waggon went up past him in the lane with nine hundred feet of glass rods in it under sacking, and the driver did not slow down, and a boy of about ten on the corner said to another boy that the rods were for the works and that the works had a furnace in it that had not gone out in about four hundred years, and neither of them said anything else about it and they were gone before he could have asked.

**And the wharf was doing four jobs at once and none of them was waiting for anybody, and a man of thirty-two who has spent five months on a bridge with forty men on it stood on that quay for about an hour and a half and understood three of the four things that were going on, and the fourth one was a boat.**

The boat was the one that got to him. A flat-bottomed thing with a rope off the bow and nine men on it, and it came in under the quay and the rope went out and the nine men did not go up on the quay, they went along the side of the boat and worked in the standing water, and they did it twice while he was there, and the second time was faster than the first, and one of them, a man of about fifty with a bad shoulder, looked up at a stranger on the quay and said, in a voice that carried about as far as a voice carries on a wet stone, “You are going to stand there all morning.”

“Yes,” said the man of thirty-two.

“**Then stand where the rope is not,**” said the man of fifty, and went back to it, and that was the whole of the conversation and four of the men on that quay have said since that it is the only sentence anybody said to a stranger in that city on the first day that anybody in the city has repeated.

**And the bread oven was hot at the second hour and hot again at the ninth, and the same people were on it both times, and about four of the people in that lane knew that and about four hundred did not, and the way they knew it was that they had bread to eat at both of those hours and at none of the other six.**

The woman at the doorway, whose name he did not get and did not try, put a loaf on the sill of a window in front of him at the third hour, and did not ask him anything, and he had understood most of why she had not by the fourth day and the last of it took him until the second market.

---

**And he slept in a room over a shop on the edge of the lane for a night, and paid for it, and the money was footed on the sill of a bench in a room the size of a cart and the addition was done by the woman who owned the room and not by him, and neither of them pretended it was a complicated sum.**

The room was three pence. The loaf was a penny. A second loaf, because he had not eaten since the road, was a penny. Three and one is four, and four and one is five pence.

“**Five pence, and the room is paid at the second hour and not the ninth, and I do not want the ninth money from a man with a stick, and you have got a foreign thing in that purse and I have weighed it and it is good and I would have taken it in three counties and there is nobody in this one who would.**”

And the coin was put down and weighed on a small stone balance and the weight went into a column in a book and the book was the second one he had been in since he came over the rise, and both of them had a column in them, and he did not think anything about that at the time and thought about it for about a week afterwards.

**And the lamp was standing where it had been when he went in and it was standing where it had been when he came out at the ninth hour, and he had been in the city for nine hours and the light had not moved at all, and the boy who brought the loaf at the ninth hour said the same sentence the woman had said at the second, word for word, in the same tone, and neither of them was being ironic, and he got used to it inside four days and it never once stopped being wrong to him.**

He lay on a bench that was too short for him with the stick standing up in the corner where he could reach it, and he put his left hand flat on the blanket, and the hand was warm at about the fourth hour every day and had been warm at about the fourth hour every day for four years, and there was no hour in this place to be warm at, and he had noticed that on the first evening and had not written it down and did not write it down then.

He slept. That is the thing about the first night and a few of the men in that lane have said since that a man who has come down off a road with a limp and a stick and has slept nine hours on a bench that is too short for him has come off a road that was doing something to him and not only to his leg, and that is a thing four hundred years in this city is very good at, because there is nothing to get up for.

**And at the ninth hour the loaf came out and it was the same loaf and it was the same two people and the boy said the same sentence, and the man of thirty-two with the limp sat on the edge of a bench that was too short for him and ate a piece of bread that cost a penny and did not move for about forty minutes, and he counted forty of them, and he had not counted anything out loud anywhere in a valley in five months and in a town before that for two years, and there was nobody in that room to hear him do it and he did it anyway and the doing of it was the only thing in the nine hours that had been his.**

**And nobody in that city asked him a single question on the first day, and about four of the people he was in front of have said since that they wanted to, and that the wanting was everything they had, and that a man who wants to ask and does not ask is the ordinary shape of a good day in a place that has decided not to be met, and that nobody here has ever been told that is what this place is.**
