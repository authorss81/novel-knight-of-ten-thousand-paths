# Chapter 217

**The twelfth day, and the second hour, and the west gate of this city, which is a gate on a bridge with a barrier of three bars and a hut eight feet by ten with a stove in it and a man in it of fifty-five, and a woman of twenty-eight standing on the near side of the barrier with a bundle on one shoulder, and a man of thirty-two with a limp and a stick who came down four hundred yards of lane and one river bridge to find out what a gate is for, and has found out, and is not going to be able to put it down again in nine days.**

The gate opens at the second hour and shuts at the ninth and it is not shut against anybody in particular, and a man of thirty-two stood on the near bank for about an hour and a half and watched about four hundred people go through it and come back through it and nine of them be turned back, and four people in that hut have said since that nine in a day is a good day and that the worst was forty-one and that there has not been a week in years with more than nine a day and that a man who counts what a gate turns back is a man who has understood the gate in about an hour and a half and a man who understands the gate in an hour and a half will ask somebody what it is for, and nobody in that hut has ever told anybody.

“What have you got,” said the man in the hut, to the woman of twenty-eight.

“A bundle.”

“Whose bundle.”

“Somebody else's.”

“**Then turn round,**” said the man of fifty-five, and did not raise his voice, and the woman of twenty-eight turned round, and a man of thirty-two with a limp and a stick watched a person of twenty-eight walk away from a gate on a bridge with a bundle on one shoulder and nine people in the lane behind her going through without stopping, and four people in that hut have said since that this is the ninth one this week and the fourth one of hers and that the ninth one is a man with a boat who comes back with a different boat.

---

**And the gate is a gate and it is not a mystery and it is not cruel, and about four people in that hut have described it in about four minutes each, and the four accounts do not agree, and all four of them are true, and the one they all have is that the man in the hut has been in it for nineteen years and has turned back about four hundred people and has not once had to raise his voice and has not once had to be told by anybody above him what to do.**

He asks two things. It is one question. Whence, and for whom, and a bundle is for somebody, and a man who has come in off the eastern road with nothing on him is a man who is not from here and is not going to be asked anything else, and there is a fourth thing that nobody in that hut will say and that about four people on that bridge have worked out separately, which is that a person going out is not turned round, and that a person coming in is, and that a person coming in with something for somebody else is turned round hardest and fastest and with the least trouble to everybody.

**And the reason is given by a man of about forty at the far end of the bridge, who repairs the winch that lifts the barrier, and who gave it in one breath and has given it to four strangers in years and improves on it every time and has never once been asked to.**

“**You cannot ask a man at a gate where he has been,**” he said. “**That is not a gate. That is a court, and a court wants a reason, and a gate does not, and a man at a gate who wants to know where a man has been has stopped being a gate and started being a question, and a question at a bridge in this city is the one thing that has never been allowed to happen, and I have been at this end of it for twenty-two years.**”

“And if they lie.”

“Then he goes through,” said the man of about forty, “**and that is what a gate is for. A gate is a machine for letting liars through, and it is cheap, it is one man and three bars, and it has been one man and three bars for four hundred years, and if the four hundredth man who has ever come over that bridge said he was from a place with a name on it, then this city has been in a book for four hundred years, and it has not been, and that is not because we are clever. That is because it costs nothing to let a liar through and everything to stop one.**”

**And the man of thirty-two with a limp stood at the far end of that bridge and found that a man of forty had just told him, in trade terms and without improving on it once, all of what a city that has decided not to be met actually does at a gate on a working morning, and that none of it was a mystery and none of it was a wall and that all of it was a machine for letting liars through, and that a city four hundred years into a standing hour had spent none of it on anything else.**

---

**And her name is Nesh Karrow and she is twenty-eight and she has been carrying for four years, and the carrying is not the ordinary carrying of a woman with a bundle and it is a post and it has no other name in that city than carrying, and four people on that bridge have said since that a woman of twenty-eight who is turned back at three gates four days a week for four years is the most reliable person in that city and that not one of the eleven in the room upstairs has ever asked her a question about it.**

She was turned back and then she did not go. That is the part of it about four people on that bridge have not got used to in four years: she turned round at about the fourth foot of the bridge and then stopped and stood on the near side with the bundle on her shoulder and faced the water, and nine people went past her and nine people did not look, and a man of thirty-two with a limp and a stick stood on the other side of the road and did not go up for about a minute and a half.

Then she turned round again and came back up the bridge, past him, and stopped.

“You are the man with the stick,” she said.

“Yes.”

“**You are the man who is carrying two of Dermit's. I have not read them. I have seen you carry them past the gate eleven times and I have counted because I count, and that is all I know about you.**”

“You know my name.”

“I do not have your name,” said Nesh Karrow. “**You have not said it and nobody in this city is going to say it to me and I am not going to ask you for it, and if I asked you for it you would be right to say no, and I would be a woman of twenty-eight who has just made a man of thirty-two careful, and I have been doing this four years and I know exactly how many people have made a man careful and it is not nine hundred.**”

“Who is Dermit.”

“The man who makes the things that say no. He is sixty-six and he has a yard at the bottom of a lane and two hundred bundles of cord in it, and he has made about four hundred of the eleven-word things and he is the only person in this city whose job is to be right, and I have never in four years carried one that was wrong, and that is the best of it and it is not the part I came up this bridge about.”

---

**And she said what she wanted, and she said it once, in one breath, in her own words, on the near side of a bridge with nine people going through a gate behind her, and four people in that hut have said since that they have all heard it and that none of them has ever been able to answer it and that one of the four has been asked about it twice in four years and has said the same words both times.**

“**I want it to be a letter,**” she said. “That is all. A letter goes into a hand and the hand goes away and nobody has to be turned round at a gate for four years over it, and a letter is not a thing anybody can be shown, and this is not a letter, and I have known that for four years and I have never once been told what it is.”

“Then what is it.”

“**I am not going to tell you, and I have not told anybody, and there are four people in this city who have asked me and I have given four answers and four of those answers were right, and I have never once been asked it by somebody who could have done anything with the answer.**”

“Will you tell me.”

“**No,**” said Nesh Karrow, “**and I would like it noticed that I did not pause, and that the four people who asked me are not going to ask you either, and that you have been in this city twelve days and not one of the four has looked at you twice.**”

Then she shifted the bundle and said the other two things, and both of them were about work and neither of them was about the work, and four people on that bridge have said since that a woman of twenty-eight who has been refused at three gates for four years can put a sentence together that a woman of fifty-eight in a room upstairs would take nine minutes to construct, and that the difference is not cleverness and is four years of being turned round.

“**I go out at the second and I am back inside by the ninth if I am not let in and by the tenth if I am, and there are nine of us and half of us are women and none of us is paid, because the only thing this city sends out is a thing that is carried, and a thing that is carried is not work, and a person who is not working is not a person anybody has to answer for.**”

“That is the arrangement.”

“**That is the arrangement and it has been the arrangement for four hundred years and I did not make it and I would like to go on carrying,**” said Nesh Karrow, “**and four people in this city have said since that a woman of twenty-eight saying she would like to go on carrying in front of a stranger is the only sentence anybody in this city has said in four hundred years that is about a person and not about a thing, and that nobody here has said one back to her, and that this is not because nobody agrees with it.**”

Nobody improved on it. A man of thirty-two with a limp and a stick stood on a bridge in a city that has been keeping a standing hour for four hundred years and did not say a second thing about what she was carrying, and four people in that hut have said since that the man with the stick did not ask, and that in four years the only two people who have stood on that bridge and not asked are a woman of twenty-eight and a man with a limp, and that they have never spoken before and never will and that this is the ordinary way this city keeps a thing.

---

**And then a child of nine came down the lane on the far side, at about the second hour and a quarter, with a bundle on one shoulder, and it came up the bridge past the hut, and the man in the hut did not stop it, and did not look at it, and did not ask it anything, and it went through the barrier and up the far bank and away, and nine people in that lane went through behind it and nine of them did not look at it either.**

A man of thirty-two with a limp and a stick watched a child of nine go through a gate with a bundle on one shoulder and watched a woman of twenty-eight get turned round at the same gate in the same quarter of an hour for the same reason, and the reason was on both sides of that bridge and it was not on anybody's paper, and nobody in that hut had said a word about either of them, and nobody in the lane had said a word about either of them, and four people in that hut have said since that the gate is not for people and that it is for bundles, and that this is true and that it is the answer to nine turnings back out of ten and that the tenth is the child and that nobody in that hut has ever counted the tenth.

**And the man of thirty-two with a limp did not ask the child anything, and did not go up the far bank after it, and did not say the word to anybody on that bridge, and has not said one word about that child to any of four hundred people in a city of nine thousand in nine days, and four people in that city have said since that the man with the stick is the only person anybody has been able to be near for nine days without asking a child a question, and that this is the second time anybody has ever noticed it and not asked, and that neither of the two has ever told the other.**

---

**And she went back down the bridge with the bundle at about the fifth hour and was turned back at the road gate at about the sixth hour and at the river gate at about the seventh hour, and a man of thirty-two with a limp did not follow her to either of them and did not stand at the lane watching her be turned back, and four people in this city have said since that a man who has been turned back once and then follows a woman to two more gates to watch her be turned back is a man who has not worked out that being turned back at three gates is not three things, and that the man with the stick worked it out at about the fifth hour of the twelfth day standing on a bridge and did not follow, and that this is the fourth of his nine days on which all of what he had come for turned out to be that he could not do a thing and that the fifth of the nine is a yard at the bottom of a lane.**

He went to the yard at the bottom of the lane at about the fifth hour of the twelfth day, and the door was shut, and there was nobody in it, and a board was over a bench under a roof with two hundred bundles of cord on racks behind it, and a man of thirty-two with a limp and a stick stood in a yard that was empty for the first time in nine days and understood that a man of sixty-six was somewhere in a city of nine thousand people and that a man of twenty-eight was on the other side of three gates with a bundle, and that neither of those two facts is a fact he can do anything with, and that there is nobody in this city he could tell either of them to, and four people in that city have said since that the man with the stick came to an empty yard four days early and stood in it for about a quarter of an hour and did not go in, and that the door of that yard is not locked and has never been locked, and that the only thing in the world he wanted out of it was a man with a nine-inch rule, and that he did not have it.

---

**And there is a fourth question he has not asked and has now carried past three gates and a bridge and nine days without asking, and it is the one that has a child's hand in it, and about four people in that city have said since that the man with the stick has been in nine places in nine days and has asked four questions and got four straight answers and that the one he has not asked is the only one anybody in this city would have had to stop a person for, and that he has not asked it, and that nobody here has noticed him not asking it, and that the woman of twenty-eight has not noticed him not asking it, and that the two of those facts are the only two pieces of good fortune he has had in this city and that he did not arrange either of them.**

Then he went back up the lane and past the works and did not go into the yard, because the yard was empty, and he slept nine hours on a bench that was too short for him with the stick standing up in the corner where he could reach it, and the light was standing where it had been standing since he came, and the left hand was warm at the fourth hour and the fourth hour is an hour this city keeps by a furnace, and he did not write any of it down, and there was nobody in that city to tell it to and nobody four hundred miles off who could have used it.
