# Chapter 0219

**The nineteenth day, and the third hour, and a city of about nine thousand people on a river with a count of days and no months, and a child of nine years old going down a lane with a bundle on one shoulder, and a man of thirty-two with a limp and a stick forty yards behind it who has been behind it since the second hour, and the reason he is behind it is that the child goes out four days in seven and this is one of the four, and he has worked that out in about nine minutes on the second day and has done nothing about it for seventeen days.**

He had not asked it one question in nineteen days. That is a fact of this city and it is not a fact of any other place he has been, and four people on that lane have said since that a man of thirty-two with a limp is walking forty yards behind a child of nine and that the child has not once looked round and the man has not once closed the distance, and that this is the ninth day of the arrangement and that nobody in this city has asked either of them about it, that four of the people who could have asked have decided not to, and that four hundred have not noticed.

**And there is a page of foolscap in a coat with nine lines on it and an hour against each of the nine, and the seventh line says a person and was looked at twice and has not been finished, and a man of thirty-two with a limp has had that page in a coat since the sixth day of a count he does not have, and the seventh line is the only one of the nine that is about anybody, and he has not taken it out since the ninth day and has not looked at it and knows what it says.**

The mark is a line and a bar and a figure. That is all it is and it has been all it is in four hundred years in a city of about nine thousand people, and a man of thirty-two with a limp saw it for about a minute and a half on the nineteenth day in daylight at forty yards and from six feet and did not ask what it was, and a man of sixty-one had already told him on the fifteenth day that he would not be told, and a man of sixty-six had told him on the sixth day that the word for the light in this city is the plain one and the word for that is not a word anybody has, and a man of about forty in a glass yard had put his trowel down on the fourth day and refused him the reason and not the word, and there were four men in this city who had each refused him one thing and not another and none of the four had been unkind and about four of them had improved on the answer the one before.

---

**And the child is not named, and the reason it is not named is the city's, and the reason the city's reason is the volume's is that the child is the post, and four people in that city have said since that a child of nine years old carries out of a city four days in seven and that nobody in nine thousand people has ever used its name at a gate or in a yard or in a room, and that this is not an accident of manners and is written on a wall in one place and nowhere else.**

“Nobody calls it anything.”

“Nobody calls it anything,” said a woman of about thirty with a pail, on the nineteenth day, at the third hour, at a door, “**and I will tell you why short and then I am going to work. A name in a book in this city is a person somebody can be shown. That is the whole trade. And there is exactly one person in this city who goes out of the gate, and if that person has a name then the name is a thing that can be carried out, and if it cannot be carried out then it cannot be a name, and the child is not a name. The child is a post.**”

“And if somebody writes it down.”

“**Somebody has. There is a yard at the end of a lane past two gates and there is a book in it and there is a line in the book and there is no name on the line, and that has been the arrangement since before the book was old, and four people in that yard could tell you the arrangement and not one of them could tell you who set it.**”

She went in with the pail. And four people in that city have said since that a woman of thirty told a stranger the whole of the post in one breath at a door and then went to work, and that she has told it four times in years and three of the four times were to men who came up a road with a stick, and that this is the fourth, and that four of the men she told it to were still in the city the next morning and four of them had gone.

---

**And the child came down the lane at the fourth hour and stopped at a yard where a man of about thirty-four with lime on his hands was batching, and the child took a hod and carried it eleven times and the man of thirty-four did not count them, and four people in that yard have said since that a man of thirty-four who does not count a child's hods is a man who has decided that counting them is a thing a person can be put in a book for.**

Then the child came out and stood in the lane and looked at a man of thirty-two with a limp, and did not go, and a man of thirty-two with a limp did not go, and nine people went past the two of them, and four people in that lane have said since that two people standing in a lane not leaving is the ordinary shape of a thing about to be asked and that the man did not ask it, that the child did not leave, and that four of the nine went past without either.

“Is that the far one,” said the child.

The man of thirty-two with a limp answered it in about three words, the way he had answered it once before, four hundred miles off, in a lane, on a morning when he had understood the question and had not known the answer.

“That is the far one.”

**And the child looked at him for about as long as it takes to count nine, and then it looked at his left hand, which was hanging at his side, and it looked at it, and a man of thirty-two with a limp did not put it behind him and did not open it, and a man of sixty-one had told him on the fifteenth day that there is nothing in this city that joins a book and a bench and a hand, and the child looked at the hand and the hand had a black line healed into the crease of it about a foot long that ran under the skin and did not stop at the edge of it, and the child's hand had a line and a bar and a figure on it, and four people in that lane have said since that a child of nine looked at a man's hand and said nothing about what was in it and that neither of them said anything and that this is the only time anybody in this city has looked at a mark in another person and that nobody here has ever worked out what was in the look.**

Then the child put its bundle back on its shoulder and went down the lane and did not turn round, and a man of thirty-two with a limp stood in a lane in a city of about nine thousand people and had been about to ask a child of nine years old a question at the third hour of the nineteenth day, and had not asked it, and has not said why, and is not going to.

**And the child eats, and works, and is nine years old, and nobody in that city has ever called it a symbol or a sacrifice or a post except a woman of thirty at a door who used the last of those three in a sentence about a book, and four people in that city have said since that a child that goes out four days in seven has a day like anybody else's and that the day is made of carrying a hod eleven times in a yard and a piece of bread at the second hour and a piece at the ninth and a lane four hundred yards long twice, and that there is no ceremony in any part of it and no assembly and nobody who comes out of a door to see it off.**

A woman of about thirty with a pail gave it a piece of bread off a sill at the third hour and it took it and did not say thank you and she did not expect it to and four people in that city have said since that a child of nine who does not say thank you for a piece of bread is a child of nine who has been given a piece of bread about nine hundred times and has never once had to say anything, and that the not saying is the politest thing about it, and that it is nine years old.

**And it is not pitied, and that is not because anybody in that city has decided not to pity it. It is because pitying a thing requires knowing what the thing is for, and four hundred people in a city of about nine thousand know what the child is for and nine hundred know what it carries and not one of the two figures knows the other one, and a person who cannot say what a thing is for cannot get the face they would need, and so the child is carried the way a hod is carried, and four people in that city have said since that this is not a decision anybody took and that it is four hundred years of nobody finding out.**

---

**And the reason is not a decision and it is not a rule and it is not anything that could be put in a page. Four people in four counties have noticed the not asking and none of them has asked him, and the fifth of those is a city that has made not asking a trade, and the fifth of those is the reason he has had nineteen days of it in a row and the other four are the reason he has it in all the rest of his life.**

He had a question ready. That is a fact of the nineteenth day and it is the thing he has not written down and will not write down and does not tell anybody, and the question was ready at about the fourth hour of the sixth day of a count he does not have and it has been ready every day since, and it is four words long, and four people in that city have said since that a man of thirty-two with a limp has been walking behind a child for seventeen days with four words ready and that this is the longest anybody in this city has ever been in a lane with a thing unsaid, and that the lane is about nine hundred feet and that he has walked it about forty times.

**He did not ask it because of what the seventh line of a page of foolscap says, and what it says is *a person*, and it says a person and not a name, and a name in a book in this city is a person somebody can be shown, and a person who is asked what a mark is by a stranger in a lane at the fourth hour is a person who has been asked, and four people in that city have said since that this is the whole of why and that the man did not work it out and that he did it anyway, which is the difference between a man who obeys a rule and a man who cannot be got to see a rule, and that this city has been telling the difference between those two for four hundred years and has never once had to act on it.**

---

**And then the child went down four hundred yards of lane past a glass works with a furnace in it and round by a bindle store and into a yard with a roof on two sides and no roof on the other two, and there were two hundred bundles of cord on racks, and a man of sixty-six was at a bench under the roof with a rule nine inches long and a strip of whalebone and a pot of the thinnest glue, and a child of nine years old came into that yard at about the fourth hour and a half of the nineteenth day and stopped at the bench, and a man of thirty-two with a limp came to the mouth of the yard forty yards behind it and did not come in, and four people in that yard have said since that the man of sixty-six looked up, and that this is the fourth time in twenty years he has looked up at a child, and that the first three were a child that wanted to know if the door was open and two that wanted something to eat, and that this is the first one that has wanted nothing and has come anyway.**

The man of sixty-six put the rule down.

“**You have not been out since the twelfth,**” he said, and the child did not answer him, and he did not appear to want an answer, and four people in that city have said since that a man of sixty-six opened a conversation with a child of nine by checking a date in his own head and that this is the ordinary way he begins a thing and that there are about nine hundred of them in four hundred years and that he has never once opened one with a question.

Then he turned his head about forty degrees and looked at the mouth of the yard, and a man of thirty-two with a limp was standing there with a stick and did not come in, and the man of sixty-six looked at him, and four people in that yard have said since that it is the longest a man of sixty-six has spent on a man standing still, and that what went on in them was not suspicion, and that it was a man of sixty-one in a yard at the end of a lane working out that a man who is at the mouth of a yard and does not come in is a man who is being careful, and that being careful is the one thing this city cannot teach anybody and can only recognise.

**And the ninth asking of four hundred years was about to be made in that yard by a man who makes things that say no, to a child of nine years old who had come four hundred yards to be asked it, and the man of thirty-two with a limp stood forty yards off at the mouth of the yard with a stick he cannot lean on and four words ready that he had had ready for thirteen days, and four people in that city have said since that a man who has a question ready for thirteen days and does not ask it and then stands forty yards off while somebody else asks it is the whole of what has happened to him in nineteen days, and that nobody in that city had worked out that it was going to be him who stood there, and that nobody has worked out anything else about it since, and that the ninth asking took about nine seconds and that a child of nine years old took the time it takes to draw one breath to answer it and that four hundred years of a city turning things down ended with the shortest answer anybody in it has ever given.**

---

**And a man of thirty-two with a limp wrote in a notebook on the nineteenth day at about the seventh hour, in a room with a bench too short for him in it, and the notebook is a thing he has carried for four years and has not written in since a lane four hundred miles off, and what he wrote on the nineteenth day is a count of days and a line about a hand, and the line about a hand is the second thing he has ever written about one and the first thing he has written about anybody's.**

He wrote, *a child of nine carries out of this place four days in seven and the mark on the hand is a line and a bar and a figure and nobody here will say what it is, and the man of sixty-one keeps a book of them and the second column of the book is not a column of names.*

Then he stopped, and four people in that city have said since that the man with the stick keeps a book as well and that nobody has asked him what is in it and that this is the second correct thing he has done in nineteen days and that the first was not buying a name in a market, and that a man who writes down what four people have said to him and does not write down what he thinks of it is a man who has been on a bridge for five months and has not been on one for nineteen days and has not noticed the difference.

**And he did not write the seventh line, and the seventh line was in a coat on a hook about four feet away and it says a person and has said a person since a lane four hundred miles off, and he did not write it and did not go and get the page and did not look at it, and four people in that city have said since that a man who has had a line unfinished for thirteen days and has not looked at it in thirteen days is a man who is frightened of what he would write under it, and that this is the only thing anybody in this city has ever seen a man be frightened of, and that nobody has said so to him, and that a city of about nine thousand people and a council of eleven and a gate and a form and a book of marks have all been in front of that man for nineteen days and not one of them was the thing.**

He put the pen down and lay nine hours on a bench that was too short for him, and the lamp was standing where it had been standing since he came down the eastern road on the first day, and the left hand was warm at the fourth hour, and the fourth hour is an hour this city keeps by a furnace, and he did not write it down then either, and he has four of the things that come in him in four years and has written one down.
