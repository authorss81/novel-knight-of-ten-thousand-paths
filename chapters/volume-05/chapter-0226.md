# Chapter 0226

**The twenty-sixth day of a count of days that this city keeps, and the second hour and the ninth hour, and a city of about nine thousand people on a river that has been told by a man in a yard on the previous day that there are eleven roads out of it, and not one of the nine thousand has been told anything about that, and about four hundred of them had already worked out some of it on their own, in the ordinary way, by having been to one of the eleven places and by having been paid in a loaf for going, and the two of those facts have never been in the same conversation in this city in four hundred years and were not in the same conversation on the twenty-sixth day, and a man of thirty-two with a limp was in four of them in one day and worked out what the city knew and what the city did not know and the difference between those two things is the whole of what happened to him on the twenty-sixth day.**

The city knows. That is the first fact of the twenty-sixth day and it is not a secret and it is not a discovery and a few hundreds of people in this city have said since that a city under a light that has not turned for four hundred years knows exactly where it is and has known the whole time, and that the knowing is done by carrying, and that the carrying is not a trade and is not a job, and that nine people go out of a gate four days a week and about nine hundred people in a city of about nine thousand have been out of the gate, and that not one of those two figures knows anything about the other one.

A man of fifty with a waggon had been to four of the eleven and had been to two of them twice, and a few of the people at a wharf have said since that he says the names of them the way a man says the names of four customers he has been going to for nineteen years and that he has never in nineteen years put two of them together in one sentence, and that a man of fifty with a waggon is the only person in this city who could put two of them together and has not and is not going to and does not know why.

A woman of about thirty with a pail had been to two of them and had a sister at one of them, and a few of the people in that lane have said since that this is the third time in years she has said so to somebody and that the other two were to men who came up a road with a stick, and that she has never said it to a man of this city, and that a woman of thirty in a city of about nine thousand has a sister somewhere and has told nobody here, and that this is not a refusal and is a habit four hundred years old, and that the habit and the road are the same age.

A boy of fourteen on a step at the top of a lane had been to none of the eleven and had heard of all of them, and a few of the people in that lane have said since that a boy of fourteen has names for eleven places he has never seen and that he gets them from nine people and that the nine people have got them from about four hundred, and that there is no book in this city with the names of the eleven in it except a book about two inches thick under a roof at the end of a lane past two gates, and that the boy of fourteen has never been in that yard and would not be allowed in it and does not know that it is there, and that this is the ordinary way a fact travels in this city and has been for four hundred years.

**And a few hundreds of people in this city have said since that nobody in this city has ever asked how a road out of it and back again is made, and that a man of fifty with a waggon knows four of the answers and has not been asked and has not said, and that a woman of thirty knows two and has said them twice to strangers, and that the not asking is not a rule and there is nobody in this city who could make a rule about it, and that a city that has not been asked a question in four hundred years does not get to choose what it is asked and so the not asking is not a decision anybody took, and that this is the same fact as the child and it is a different one and the two have never been in a sentence together.**

---

**And a man of thirty-four with lime on his hands said a thing at the second hour in a yard that a few of the men in that yard have said since is the ordinary way a man in this city talks about one of the eleven, and that he said it while he was working and did not stop and did not look up, and that it is in the plainest register anybody in that city has for a place four hundred miles off and it is the register of a man talking about a customer.**

“I have been to the one they call the low crossing,” he said, “**twice, and both times in the winter, and the going is about a day and a bit and the road is worse in the middle of it than it is at either end, and I do not go there in the summer because the second day is four hours of standing water and I am not going to argue with four hours of standing water, and that is the whole of what I know about it and I have never once been asked.**”

Nobody improved on it. And a few of the men in that yard have said since that a man of thirty-four with lime has said nine sentences in twenty-five days in the plainest register there is and that every one of the nine has been about work and that not one of them has been about the eleven, and that the nine of them are the only sentences anybody in that yard has said about a place that is not this city in four hundred years, and that a man of thirty-two with a limp was in that yard on the fourth day and on the twenty-fourth and has not been in it on the twenty-sixth and has not asked for the other seven.

---

**And at the fourth hour of the twenty-sixth day a man of thirty-two with a limp was at the mouth of a yard at the end of a lane past two gates, and a man of sixty-one was at a bench with a book on it, and the second column was open, and a few of the people in that yard have said since that a man of sixty-one did not turn the book round this time, it was already round, and that it had been round since the fourth hour and a half of the twenty-fifth day and had not been shut and had not been squared up at the back of the bench, and that a man of sixty-one has left a book open on a bench for about a day and a half and that is the longest anybody in that yard has ever known him to leave anything.**

He put his finger on the last line and did not read it out and moved it along, counting, and a few of the people who have been in that yard have said since that a man of sixty-one counted eleven lines with a finger and stopped on the eleventh and that the eleven of them are not in order, and that the oldest of the eleven is the third line and the newest is the eleventh, and that a man of sixty-one has four hundred years of a count of days in the first column and eleven lines in the second and there is no order in the second because nobody has ever wanted one.

Then he said, “**This one. Fourteen years ago.**”

And a few of the people who have been in that yard have said since that a man of sixty-one said a figure out loud in that yard for the first time in nineteen years and that the figure was fourteen and that he said it in the tone of a man saying a price, and that a figure of fourteen years is not a price and nobody there improved on it.

---

**And the eleventh line has two marks on it, and the first of the two is a woman's and the second of the two is a boy's, and the second mark is the same shape as the mark that a child of nine years old put on that bench on the nineteenth day of this count, and a few of the people who have been in that yard have said since that this is the first time in four hundred years that a man of sixty-one has found two marks on one line of that column, and that the other ten lines have one each, and that a man of sixty-one has never told anybody what the two marks on one line mean and has not been asked and has not said, and that he said about eleven words about them on the twenty-sixth day and that a few of the people in that yard have repeated those eleven words since and that none of the four has been able to say what they mean either.**

“**A woman and a boy and about a year between the going out and the coming back, and the second mark is a boy's and he would have been about nine, and the first one is a woman's and I have never seen her and I have never had her name and there is no name in either column of this book and there has not been one in four hundred years.**”

**And then he said the other thing, and he said it before anybody asked him it, and a few of the people who have been in that yard have said since that this is the third time in twenty-five days that a man in this city has said a thing out loud before anybody in that yard had got to the question, and that the other two were a woman of sixty in a room and a man of sixty-six in a yard, and that all three of them have done it and that not one of the three has ever said they were doing it, and that in this city where nobody asks anybody anything, the three people who do the asking are the three people who answer in advance, and that this is not a policy and is what happens when four hundred years of not being asked has taught about nine hundred people one habit.**

“**And I know what you are going to ask and I am going to answer it now so that you do not have to stand there and decide. There is no third column. There are eleven lines and there is a hand and a day and a road and there has never been anything else on any of them in four hundred years, and there is no column for what became of anybody, and there is not going to be one, and I have not written one and I could not fill one in.**”

Nobody asked him. A man of thirty-two with a limp was nine feet off with his hands at his sides and did not ask him and a few of the people in that yard have said since that the not asking was four seconds long and that a man who has had a question ready for fourteen days and has not asked it in four hundred other chances was four seconds from asking one and did not, and that the four seconds are the whole of what a man of thirty-two with a limp did in this city on the twenty-sixth day, and that a page of foolscap in a coat has a seventh line on it that says a person and has not been finished since a lane four hundred miles off, and that he did not take it out and did not look at it and did not finish it, and that a few of the people in that yard have worked out that the seventh line and the four seconds are the same thing and none of the four has said so and one of the four is the man of sixty-one.

---

**And then a boy of fourteen came up the lane and stopped at the mouth of that yard because there were two men in it and one of them was a stranger, and he said what he said in the plainest register there is, and a few of the people who have been in that yard have said since that it is the only time in four hundred years that anybody in this city has said anything true about a book in that yard out loud and that it was said by a boy of fourteen who does not read books and cannot.**

“That line is older than me,” said the boy of about fourteen. “**You are talking about a thing that is the same age as me and you have been talking about it as though it were a thing from somewhere. It is a line in a book and the book is about the same age as me and everything else in it is older.**”

**And a man of sixty-one at a bench put his hand flat on the second column and left it there, and a few of the people who have been in that yard have said since that a boy of fourteen is the only person in this city who can be right about a number in that yard, and that a man of sixty-one has kept a count of days since before the boy was born and has never once converted one of them into a year, and that a city that keeps a count of days and a boy of fourteen who keeps a count of years are the only two kinds of counting anybody in that city does, and that the two have never met and the eleventh line is the only thing in this city that both of them can be the age of, and that it is fourteen years old, and that a child of nine years old came down that lane on the twenty-sixth day and did not come in, and that nobody in that yard has said one word about that and four of them have thought about it.**

---

**And a man of thirty-two with a limp went down the lane at about the fifth hour of the twenty-sixth day and stood in a market on about two hundred yards of street where a few hundreds of people were, and about four of the people in that street have said since that a man of thirty-two with a limp stood in the middle of two hundred yards of street for about a quarter of an hour and did not buy anything and did not go to the man of thirty at the corner, and that nine people in that street said the name of one of the eleven places out loud in that quarter of an hour without being asked and without lowering their voices, and that four of the nine said the same name, and that a man of thirty-two with a limp stood in the middle of it and got the name of one place nine times in a quarter of an hour and could not find anybody to ask what it meant, and that a man of thirty who says anybody's name in that street for a penny looked at him for four seconds on the twenty-sixth day and did not come over, and that a man of thirty has been at that corner for about four years and has never once gone to anybody, and that a few of the people in that street have noticed that the man of thirty did not go over and have not asked him about it.**

And a man of thirty-two with a limp wrote the name down that night, in a notebook he has carried for four years, and he wrote down nine of them and a few of the people in that room have said since that a man who has written down nine names of places he has been told by nine strangers in a market has done the ordinary thing in a market and has not been told anything and cannot use any of it, and that the seventh line of a page of foolscap in the same coat says a person and has not been finished, and that the two of them are in the same pocket about four inches apart and have not met and are not going to.
