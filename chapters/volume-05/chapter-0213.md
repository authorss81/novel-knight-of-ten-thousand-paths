# Chapter 0213

**The fourth day, from the second hour to the ninth, on a wharf and in a yard behind it where there is a glass works with a furnace in it that has not gone out in four hundred years, and about nine thousand people on a river in a valley with no night in it, and a man of thirty-two with a limp and a stick who is fed in exchange for carrying and who has now worked out in about four days that the two arrangements are not the same thing, and the difference between the two is the thing a man has to work out in four days.**

The wharf is not a view. That is the first thing a man with a stick gets wrong about a river town and the second thing he gets right about this one. The river is a hundred and sixty feet wide at the quay and it is doing four jobs, and none of the four is being beautiful, and a man of thirty-two stood on it for an hour on his first day and thought he was looking at something and was in fact standing in a place of work with a bag of lime in his coat.

The four jobs. There is a quay of about nine hundred feet of it with a crane on a leg every ninety feet, and the crane is worked by a man and a boy and a wheel, and the wheel is old and the boy is eleven. There is a lighter that comes up under the quay and is worked from the side in the standing water, and it went twice in an hour and a half and both times faster than the time before. There is a rope walk at the top end where nine hundred feet of rope comes off a drum a day and goes onto a coil, and the coil is the only thing in that river town that is made by a machine and counted by a man. And there is a boat building berth at the low end with a hull in it about two thirds done, and the hull has been two thirds done for about eleven days and will be two thirds done tomorrow, and a man of about sixty who owns it said that the reason is that he is sixty and there is nobody behind him, and said it to a stranger without being asked and in the tone of a man who has said it to about four hundred people.

**And the crane was the thing he watched, and a man of thirty-two with a limp does not stop watching a crane, and there is a moment in the working of a crane on a leg where the leg takes the load and the wheel goes slack for a second and a half and the man at the wheel takes his hands off it, and a man with a limp watched a man take his hands off a loaded crane for a second and a half as many times a day as you would like to watch, and after the first day of it he understood that this was the whole of the trade and that the man doing it was not showing off and was not careless and had been doing it since he was eleven.**

“You have done that all morning,” he said, on the fourth day, to the man at the wheel, and the man said, “Yes,” and did not say anything else, and then after a while said that his name was not anything and that he had not been asked for it and that in all the time since he took the wheel nobody had asked him for it and he was not expecting it, and that a man who did not ask was easy to have on a quay.

---

**And his leg is the same leg it was in the spring and it is slower, and nine men in that yard found that out on their own in four days without being told and without a word about it being said, and none of them said it, and the yard is a stone floor about two hundred and forty feet long and there is nowhere on it to sit down and nothing to lean on.**

**That is the whole of the difficulty and it is a trade difficulty and not a joke, and about four of the men in that yard have said since that a man with a bad leg in a stone yard is a man who is slower at the end of a run than at the start of it, and that the only thing that fixes it is a bench, and that a bench in a glass yard is a thing nobody has because a man who is sitting down is a man not carrying.**

He carried for nine days. He was not slow in a way anybody could see and he was not fast in a way anybody would thank him for, and he was slower at the second hod than at the first one, and nine men in that yard noticed that and worked it out between them over four days without discussing it, and one of them, a man of thirty-four, solved it on the fourth day by putting his own hod down at the end of a run six feet short of where the stranger was going and picking it up again and carrying it the last six feet, and he did that eleven times in an afternoon and did not once say why and did not once look at him while he did it.

**And a man of thirty-two with a limp noticed, on the fourth day, that a yard had worked out that he needed a bench and had given him six feet of one man's carry instead, and did not offer it, and never mentioned it again, and that this was the second time in four days that a place had arranged not to be in the way of something it had worked out about him, and never said, and that he had not expected to be arranged for, and that he has three things open behind him that he cannot put down and that the arrangement of six feet in a stone yard is the smallest of the four things he has been given this month and is the one he has not been able to put down since.**

---

**And the glass works is at the top of the same lane and a man of thirty-two with a limp went into its yard on the second day and was fed for carrying and went back on the third and the fourth, and nine of the men in that yard worked out within a week that the man with the stick was faster on a hod than a man of thirty-four and that he was slower getting out of the way of the second hod, and the nine of them have said since that the second of those two facts is the whole of the trade and that a man who carries well and stands badly is a man who has been on a bridge.**

The yard is about four hundred feet by a hundred and the heat comes off the furnace wall in a sheet you can see from the gate, and the glass is not blown there and the rods are not drawn there, and what is done there is the batching, and about four of the men in that yard could describe what batching is and have described it to him in nine seconds each and gave four different answers and all four of them were right.

“Four of us have said four things to him and he has not once said one of us was wrong,” said a man of about thirty-four, on the fourth day, at the eighth hour, to nobody, and nine men in that yard heard him and one of them said that the man with the stick had not said it because he had not been asked a question that four answers would finish.

---

**And the lamp is above all of it and it does not turn, and all of the fourth day happened underneath it in a light that did not change by one finger's width between the second hour and the ninth, and about four men in that yard have said that working under it is like working under a roof and that the roof is up there and you cannot see the edge of it and that the men who came here before you did not put a lamp up and did not take one down either.**

“Where does it come from,” said the man of thirty-two, to a man of about forty in the yard, on the fourth day, at the seventh hour, when the two of them were on the wall together and there was nobody within thirty feet.

“**It is a word,**” said the man of about forty, and put his trowel down, which is a thing nine men in that yard have described as the first thing that made them believe the man with the stick was going to be all right.

“**It is a word and I am not going to say the word to a man off a road,**” he said, “**and I want you to understand that I am not refusing you the word. Anybody in this yard will say the word. I have heard it said to a butcher. I am refusing you the reason, and I do not know the reason, and neither does the man on the ladder, and the man on the ladder has not been asked either and if he were he would tell you the same and he would be telling the truth.**”

The man of thirty-two put his hand flat on the wall and took it off.

“Then I will not ask.”

“**Everybody off a road says that,**” said the man of about forty, and picked the trowel up, “**and about four of them mean it.**”

---

**And there is a ladder, and the ladder goes up, and the ladder comes down, and nobody in that city has ever explained it to anybody including the men who use it, and about four of the men in that yard have said that the thing about the ladder is not the ladder and that anybody who has been in the yard for a year does not see it any more, which is the ordinary way that anything becomes a piece of a place.**

The ladder is against the east wall of the works and it goes up past the roof line thirty feet, and it is not a ladder for a roof, because there is nothing on that roof, and the top of it goes past the parapet and stops, and a man goes up it at the second hour and comes down at the third, and goes up at the ninth and comes down at the tenth, and there are two men and one of them is forty and the other one is not.

He counted that on the fourth day because he counts things, and he had got it wrong twice in four days, and about four men in that yard have said since that the man with the stick stood at the bottom of that ladder at the second hour and at the ninth hour on nine days running and did not say one word to either man, and that on the fourth day the man of about forty came down and looked at him and said nothing, and that the looking was four seconds long and that it is the only time in that yard that anybody has looked at a stranger for longer than it took to work out what he was for.

**And it goes in shifts, and the shifts are on a piece of paper, and the paper is in this city, and it has been kept by one man for a long time, and nobody has ever asked to see it, and the reason nobody has asked to see it is a reason that everybody in that city agrees with and has agreed with for a very long time, and it is the same reason a name is a penny in a market, and it is the same reason a man of thirty who sells names on a corner of a street will not sell one to a stranger.**

He did not ask. He had built four things in four years and had learned in about eleven weeks that asking is a road, and he had not asked, and about four men in that yard have said since that the stranger did not ask, and that this was the third thing about him that four of them noticed and none of them said anything to, and that the three things were that he carried well, that he stood badly, and that he did not ask.

**And he had four questions he had brought down the hill with him on the first day, and he got through the fourth day without putting one of them, and he wrote them out in the notebook that night against the count of days, and the four of them were why the light does not turn, who is on the ladder, why a name in a market is a person somebody can be shown, and whether anybody in this city has ever been refused anything. Four questions in nine days and a man who has built four things in four years did not ask one of them on the fourth day, and a few people in that city have said since that the stranger with the stick is the only man anybody has ever been able to sit next to for nine days without asking a question, and that he did not do it out of manners, and that it was not manners, and that nobody in that city has worked out what it is and that this is a good sign and not a bad one.**

His left hand was warm at the fourth hour on the fourth day and has been warm at the fourth hour every day for four years, and the fourth hour is an hour that this city keeps by a furnace and not by anything else, and he wrote one line about it in the notebook and did not write what it meant, and he has written one of those in four years and it has no hour against it and no name in it.

---

**And the hour is not in the sky here and is kept by other people, and a man of thirty-two with a limp who had spent sixteen weeks in a valley with no hours at all got it wrong three times in four days, and the third time was at the ninth hour on the fourth day in a yard with a furnace in it, and the thing that told him was nine men putting a lid on something at the same moment.**

He had it at the eighth hour, because the light had been at the height it was at and the light was always at the height it was at, and he worked it out, standing at the foot of a ladder, by a thing that had nothing to do with the hour at all, which is that a furnace yard is loud and a furnace yard at nine men all stopping at once is quiet.

“Ninth hour,” said the man of about forty, from about thirty feet up, without being asked, and then went on up.

**And then a man of fifty with a waggon came through the gate and said his name was not wanted and asked him what he wanted, and the asking was the ordinary asking at the end of an ordinary day, and the answer he gave was work, and the answer he got was about eleven words long and it was correct and he has never improved on it and that is the whole of what happened to him in four days.**

**“I cannot give you work,**” said the man of fifty. “**Not because of your leg. Because of the stick. A man I pay is a man I have had to write somewhere, and a man I write somewhere is a man this city has held, and a city that has held a man has had to put him in a place, and a place is a thing that can be asked about from four hundred miles off.**”

“Then I will not be paid.”

“**You have been fed four days and you have carried about nine hundred hods and neither of those things is a wage, and a man who understands the difference between being fed and being paid is the only man off a road who ever has,**” said the man of fifty, “**and I am not going to explain the rest of it to you in a gateway at the ninth hour because there are eleven people in this city who do it properly and one of them keeps the forms.**”

“Where do I ask.”

“Not here.” The man of fifty put a hand on the gate post and took it off. “**You do not ask me and you do not ask the man on the ladder and you do not ask nine men in a yard. There are eleven of them and they have a room, and the asking goes in a room and not in a yard, and I have been in this city thirty-one years and I have never once been in that room and I have twice been told what comes out of it.**”

**And the man of fifty said that last part, and then stopped, and went out of the gate, and about four people in that yard have said since that a man who has twice been told what comes out of a room and has never been in it has just told a stranger the address of it, and that he is not a careless man, and that he is a man who has fed a stranger four days and has decided the stranger is to be put down somewhere before he goes back down the lane.**

**And that is the fourth day, and the ninth hour, and the light had not moved, and about nine thousand people had eaten and had gone to bed in about four hundred houses and the river was doing the same four things it had been doing at the second hour, and a man of thirty-two with a limp was standing at the foot of a ladder in a yard with a stone floor, holding a piece of glass rod he has no use for, with four days of a count of days in a notebook, and a man of fifty with a waggon came through the gate and said his name was not wanted and asked him what he wanted.**
