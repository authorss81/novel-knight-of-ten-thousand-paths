# Chapter 0352

**The second day of Rainmonth, YR 317, a Monday, and not a market day, and the second hour and the fourth hour and the sixth hour, in a town of about two thousand people on the salt, called Marl, where there are about nine crossings in forty miles of flat country around it and one of them runs a bar and the bar goes down and comes up, and a woman of about fifty who has kept one list of the nine for nineteen years came out of a door in that town and did not know that anybody had come nine days to ask her about a book she keeps.**

Rainmonth on the salt is the month the flats dry enough for a cart to leave a rut and the month a person who works on a crossing begins to be able to count how many come. Marl has a market on a Saturday and none on any other day in the week. About two hundred of about two thousand people were out of their houses. **Nine days is a cart on a made road and eleven days is a cart on the flats, and the second man came in nine and the man of thirty-two with a limp came in eleven and a half, and neither of them counted the days and both of them have said since that the crossing they came by was under water at a ford four miles short of the town.**

**And a woman of about fifty came out of a door in that town at about the second hour with a list under her arm, because a list is not a thing that belongs to her and a list is a thing she keeps, and that is the whole of the difference between a person and an office and it is the only difference there is.**

---

**And a town of about two thousand people on the salt has about nine crossings within nine days of it, and four of the nine are bars and about two of the four are bars that go down and come up and about two of the four are places where a man has to take his cart apart, and nobody in that town has a word for a crossing that is a man taking a cart apart, and the list calls all nine of them by name and about four of the nine names are words a person would use for a piece of road.**

The list is nineteen years old. It is a page of nine lines on one side of a board and the lines are ruled and the ninth has been folded across the top and about a half inch, which is a thing people did to a page about nine years after it stopped being a list and before anybody decided to write anything about it.

**A woman of about fifty who has kept that list for nineteen years is Ilsa Roke, and about two thousand people in that town know the name and about four thousand people nine days off know nothing about it.**

“**You are the keeper of it,**” the man of thirty-two said, at about the fourth hour, at the one table in that town where a stranger can sit down without being asked to leave.

“**I keep it,**” she said. “**I have kept it nineteen years and I did not make it and the man who made it is dead and he was not from here.**”

“**Nine crossings.**”

“**Nine crossings.**”

“**And you have never taken one out.**”

Ilsa Roke put the board on the table with the ninth line facing up, and did not answer that, and about two people in that town have worked out a woman who keeps a list for nineteen years and has never taken a line out of it was asked a question that was not a question and the only way to answer a question that is not a question is to put the board on a table.

“**The ninth one has not been a crossing since before I came,**” she said. “**It is a line on a page and it has been a line on a page for nineteen years and it is in my book and it is in the book in the same hand and the hand is not mine.**”

“**Why is it in the book.**”

“**Because a man who kept it before me put it in and I have not found out from anybody what it was and I have had nineteen years to ask,**” she said, “**and I have not asked, because the day I take that line out is the day there are eight, and I have never wanted there to be eight.**”

---

**And a rider used to come once a month along that flat and the county paid him four pence a day and he was gone two days and came back on the third, and that is the whole of what the list was ever for and it is the whole of what anybody has ever paid anybody to know what was on nine crossings.**

Four pence a day. Eight pence for the two days of the month he was gone. Ninety-six pence for the twelve months of the year he did it in. **That is eight shillings, and eight shillings a year is what this kingdom knows about nine crossings, and the man is dead and the route he rode is under nine inches of salt in two places and nobody has graded it since and a cart goes round both places by about a mile and a half, which is the reason a cart takes eleven days and not nine.**

“**I asked for one year of it when I came,**” she said, “**and the man at the county said it was a road and roads are not surveyed and a road is graded when a road wants grading. He was not being cruel. He said it the way a man says a thing that has been said to him.**”

“**So you have ridden it yourself.**”

“**I have ridden it myself in every year since,**” she said, “**and in the fourth year after the man died, and it is nine miles out and nine miles back on the same line and it takes a day and a half and I have not charged anybody for it, and there is nowhere in this county to enter that I have not charged anybody for it, and that is not modesty, it is that the list does not have a column for what a person spent keeping it.**”

**And about two people in that town have said since that a list of nine crossings has been kept for nineteen years by one woman at her own cost out of a county that does not survey a road, and that the woman has never once written what it cost, and that if she had there would have been a figure on a piece of paper about nine inches by six somewhere in a building in that town and the county would have had a number in it.**

---

**And the fourth one in that list is a place about four miles north where the water is up to a cart's axle in Rainmonth, and it is the best crossing on that line, and about nine people use it in the four months and about four hundred could, and a woman of about fifty has ridden past it in every month of every year for nineteen years and has never once been asked why nobody uses it, and the reason nobody uses it is that the made road on the far side of it is a mile and a half of soft going and a cart that goes round it comes out at the ninth crossing and not at the fourth, and the list has a note against the fourth and the note is in the hand of a man who is dead and it says one word and the word is *ditch*.**

**And a man of about thirty-two with a limp asked her the question he had come nine days to ask, and it was a bad question, and he knew it was a bad question before he asked it, and he asked it anyway because he had a list of his own and he wanted to see whether hers was the same nine.**

“**The Crown has put a price on nine crossings,**” he said, “**and I have wanted to know whether the nine are yours.**”

Ilsa Roke did not answer for about four seconds, which is a long time at a table where a stranger has paid for nothing.

“**That is a very bad question,**” she said, “**and I am going to answer it, and I want you to notice that I am going to answer it and not notice.**”

“**I noticed.**”

“**There are nine crossings in that list and there are nine crossings in that schedule and they are not the same nine and I have not seen the second of them and I will not go and look,**” she said. “**There is a place about four miles north where the water is up to a cart's axle in Rainmonth and the crossing there is a good one and nobody uses it, and it is the fourth in my book, and if the other people have put a fourth in theirs I would like to know which fourth and there is no way on this earth to know that without somebody sitting in this room and joining two lists, and the whole of what this book is for is that nobody sits in this room and joins it.**”

“**Then how do you know it is not the same nine.**”

“**I do not know that,**” Ilsa Roke said. “**I know that I have not looked, and that is a different thing and it is the only thing I have, and you came nine days to ask me whether a list of mine and a list of theirs are the same nine and the answer is that it does not matter, and that if they are the same nine then somebody built a schedule out of a roll in an office and if they are not then somebody in an office built a schedule out of nothing and I do not know which of those two I would rather and neither does anybody in this town.**”

---

**And she asked him one question back, and it was about his own work and not about hers, and about two people in that town have said since that a woman who keeps a list of nine crossings for nineteen years asked a stranger what a list is for, and that a man who had come nine days to use her list could not answer it, and that he answered a different question and she let him.**

“**What is your list for,**” she said, at the sixth hour. “**You have a page with nine things on it and no heads and no columns and you made it in nine minutes, and I would like to know what it is for.**”

“**It is for me,**” the man of thirty-two said. “**It is for me to be able to say I know what is on nine crossings when somebody tells me a thing about one of them.**”

“**Then it is a list,**” Ilsa Roke said, “**and I will tell you what a list is for, and it is not for that, and I have known that for nineteen years and I have not had anybody to say it to until this afternoon.**”

“**What is it for.**”

“**It is for the ninth one,**” she said. “**That is the whole of what it is for. If I take the ninth one out there are eight and then a man in an office puts a price on eight and I never find out which eight, because there is no ninth line to look down. As long as there is a ninth line folded across the top, anybody who has anything to say about my nine has to say it about nine.**”

**And about two people in that town have said since that a woman kept a list of nine crossings for nineteen years for the sake of one of them, and that the one she kept it for is a piece of ground about four miles north with a ditch against it, and that a man from another county has made nine fresh crossings on a page in about nine minutes and the ninth of his is the fourth of hers, and that neither of them knows that, and that there is nobody in that town or nine days off who could put the two lists in one sentence.**

---

**And the whole of her want came out of her in fourteen words to a stranger at a table, and it came out of her once, and nobody improved on it and nobody has asked her for it since.**

“**I keep nine and one of them is only a line on a page.**”

And the man of thirty-two with a limp wrote nothing down, because he had a hand that could not write on a table and because there is nowhere in that town to write anything down, and about four people in that town have worked out a woman who has kept a list of nine crossings for nineteen years said in fourteen words the whole of what is wrong with a schedule of nine crossings, and that the whole of what is wrong with a schedule of nine crossings is that a schedule has no ninth line folded across the top and about a half inch, and that a schedule is a list that has been told what it is for.

**And she went back to that board and folded the ninth line back across the top and about a half inch, and about two people in that town have worked out a woman who has never taken a line out of that list has now folded it again in front of a stranger, and that the folding is not a decision, and that the whole of what a list can do is be folded.**
