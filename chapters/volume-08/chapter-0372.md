# Chapter 0372

**The twenty-ninth day of Embermonth, YR 317, a Saturday, and a market day, and the third hour and the fifth hour and the eighth hour, in a room over a chandler's shop in a lane in a town of about two thousand people on the salt, where about nine hundred people had come into four hundred yards of stone that morning and the shop under that room had shut at the second hour because the market takes the trade, and a woman of about forty read out an account of a winter, and about two thousand people in that town do not know that anybody from four hundred miles off has ever been in that room.**

The woman of about forty came out eight days behind the woman of about thirty-five and arrived on the twenty-third of that month, and she had walked the same line, and she had not come with her. **She came because about nine hundred people do not send a paper and a person at the same time unless both of them are needed, and about nine hundred people had decided in two winters that both of them were, and the person was needed to hear the answer and not to give it.**

She had been in that town six days and she had not been in the lane until that morning. **A lodging house in another street had taken her bed and her washing and had not asked her a single question in six days, and two thousand people in that town have eaten their breakfast this morning without knowing that there is a stranger in a house at the top of that street who has been awake since before the light with four pages in her hands.**

The four pages were written out fair on both sides and there were nine lines ruled on each of them the way a person rules a page to keep a number in a column. **They are the account of the worst winter in eighteen years, and they are the only copy, and about nine hundred people in a place four hundred miles off have never asked to see them and have never been able to, and the woman who wrote them is the woman who has read them out to whoever is in the room. A community four hundred miles off has a winter that nobody outside it has ever heard described out loud, and a winter described out loud by the one woman who kept the account of it is a different thing from a winter that has been written down, and about two thousand people in that town had never been given either.**

She read all of it and nobody read any of it with her.

---

**The account is a river and a road and a winter. The first page is the winter itself. It is the nights the water stood above the sill at the ford, and the number is nineteen nights, and on four of those nights it stood above the sill and the sill was under eleven inches, and on the ninth of the nineteen it stood four feet over the sill, and on that night the sill was not a thing anybody could see from the bank.**

The second page is the road. **A road that is not a road four months of the year is nine miles of made ground and two hundred yards of a bank that a cart will go up in a dry week and will not go up at all in a wet one, and the account has the two hundred yards in it four times and the four times are four different winters, and the second of the four is the one everybody in that place believes and the three others are in the account because an account that leaves out three is a story and not an account.**

The third page is the loads. **A load is what a cart can be trusted with, and the account gives the weight of what went over the bank in that winter and the number of times, and the number of times is nine, and there is a figure beside the ninth one and the figure is not the same as the other eight and the woman read it out at the same speed as the other eight and did not stop on it.**

---

**And two people in that lane have said since that the figure beside the ninth of the loads is the only one in the four pages that carries a mark, and that the mark is a small cross, and that a cross is not a figure and there is no column for it, and that a woman of about forty carried that cross four hundred miles on a line that is not a made road and did not explain it and was not asked.**

The fourth page is what they did. **It is written the way a thing is written when it was written at the time by somebody who did not know it was an account, and it has the day on it and the day is the day the water dropped and it is a day and not a night because they had been awake all night, and the fourth page is one paragraph and the woman read it at the same speed as the rest of it.**

**And at the fifth hour she stopped and put the four pages flat on the table with her hands on them, and she said the thing about the account that about two thousand people in that town have never had said to them by anybody, and she said it once and she did not say it a second way.**

“**I wrote it,**” the woman of about forty, who is called Sabra Rolf, said, “**and I will read it out, and nobody else will read it out for me.**”

And two people in that lane have worked out since that a woman of about forty said eighteen words in a room over a chandler's shop on a market Saturday, and that the eighteen words were about a reading and not about a road and not about a winter, and that she said them as though she had been saying them in a kitchen in front of people who had heard her say them before, and that nobody in that room understood for about half a minute that the last eight of the eighteen were the whole of her reason for being four hundred miles from her own table.

---

**And then she told them what she was, because a room that has been told a number by somebody with no stake in it asks what the somebody is, and what she was is that she was twenty-nine in that winter and she is about forty now, and eleven years of carrying four pages.**

“**I was twenty-nine,**” she said. “**And I have had eleven years to be angry at those four pages and I have not been able to make the figures say anything else, and I have not been able to make them say anything I would rather they said, and that is the whole of what an account is worth. It will not be better for you later and it will not be worse. It is what it was on the day.**”

A man of about fifty-four who burns lime at a kiln two miles off that town said that he had kept a day-book for about thirty years and that a day-book is not an account and an account is not a day-book, and that the difference is that a day-book is a thing a man does for himself at the end of a day and an account is a thing nine hundred people have agreed to be able to check.

“**Nobody has ever checked mine,**” she said. “**That is not the part I mind. The part I mind is that I am the only one who has looked at it.**”

---

**And at the sixth hour a man of about thirty-four with a sheet asked her the question that two people in that room had wanted to ask since the third hour, and he asked it in the ordinary way and without a form, and what he asked was what a winter does to a road on this side, and two thousand people in that town had wanted to hear it said out loud by somebody who had been in one.**

She said that their winter took the top off a road and theirs took the road, and then she said it in one more piece, and two people in that room worked out afterwards that what she had said was the first thing anybody had said to any of them about a winter that they would still be carrying in a month.

“**Your winter takes the top off a road,**” she said, “**and then it rains and the top comes back and the road is a road again by the middle of the season. Ours takes the road, and it takes the ground the road was on, and neither of them comes back inside the four months, and there is nobody on our side of that river who has ever been able to promise anybody that the ground will be back.**”

**And she did not say it a second time and she was not asked to, and a man of about thirty-two with a limp who had been in that lane for seven weeks wrote none of it down and had no place to write it down on, and about four people in that room have said since that a woman of about forty from four hundred miles off described this country's winter in nine seconds to four men who have all been standing in it, and not one of the four had ever asked a person about it.**

---

**And then a man of about thirty-three with a barrow asked what the winter had cost, and she gave a figure, and the figure was a number of loads and a number of weeks and not a number of people, and she said why in one sentence and did not soften it.**

“**I have not put a head against any of it,**” she said, “**because the nine of us who wrote the first three pages were not asked for one and did not think to ask, and we took what the bank told us and the bank told us about loads and not about who was in the houses, and I have gone back over it eleven years and I cannot tell you one thing about that winter that is not a thing about a river.**”

A man of about thirty-four with a sheet said that there are about four hundred and nine parish rolls in a building two hundred miles off that carry the names of about that many places and a figure against each of them, and that anybody in this kingdom can be found by what a person keeps, and that he had found a person that way in the last year and that the person had said yes.

“**Then you will be able to find us,**” the woman of about forty said, and looked at him for about four seconds, and what she said was a thing that about nine hundred people on a river four hundred miles off would have wanted said and would not have wanted heard, and it was not said to the man and it was not said about the man.

---

**And at the eighth hour she squared the four pages and put them back in order and put them in the bag, and the bag went back the way it had come, and about four pages that are the only account of the worst winter in eighteen years in about nine hundred people's lives went into a bag on a chair in a room over a shop in a lane, and about nine hundred people who live on that account have never seen a copy of it and there is no way in this kingdom to have a copy of it.**

A man of about fifty-four who burns lime asked her one thing that nobody else in the room had thought to ask, and what he asked was whether the four pages had been kept dry.

“**In a tin,**” she said. “**In a tin under a floor that is off the ground, and the tin is under the floor of a house that stands on the bank, and I have moved the tin four times in eleven years because the bank has come up four inches in eleven years, and every one of the four times was a year somebody in that place said the bank would not move again.**”

“**And it moved four times.**”

“**It moved four times,**” she said, “**and there is a number for how far it came up and not one of us has ever been asked to be asked, and that is the whole of my trade and I have been at it for twenty years and it has never once been anybody's job.**”

A man of about thirty-four with a sheet said that he could have it taken down by a man with a hand and a week, and the woman of about forty said that she had thought of that on the road and had gone over it every day for eleven days, and that a copy of it made by a man with a hand would be a copy of the figures and not of the four pages, and that the figures were the easy part.

“**They can have the figures,**” she said. “**They are nineteen nights and eleven inches and four feet and nine loads and one day, and anybody can write those down in a week, and none of those is why I am sitting here.**”

**And a woman of about forty came four hundred miles on a line that is not a made road for the first two hundred and thirty of it, to read four pages out loud in a room to nine people, and would not let one of them write a copy, and what the four pages are for is that about nine hundred people will be able to say that they were there, and the only person who can say so is the one who read them out.**
