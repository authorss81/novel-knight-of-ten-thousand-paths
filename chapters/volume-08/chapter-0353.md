# Chapter 0353

**The eighth day of Rainmonth, YR 317, a Sunday, and not a market day, and the fourth hour and the seventh hour and the eighth hour, at a crossing four miles south of a town of about two thousand people on the salt, where a bar of oak and iron goes down across a cut in a made road when a cart is coming and comes up when the cart has gone, and about nine people cross there on a day the crossing runs and nobody crosses there on a day the crossing does not, and a woman of about forty-one was on her own side of that bar on a Sunday because a bar has to be put down and lifted whether anybody comes or not.**

A Sunday on the salt is a Sunday. Two hundred of about two thousand people in that town were out of their houses and about four hundred were out on the flats, because a Sunday on the flats is the one day in the week that a person who works on a crossing is at a crossing, and the crossing is where they are and not where they would rather be. **Nobody in that county trades on a Sunday. The bar goes down and comes up on a Sunday in the fourth month of the year the crossing runs, and a woman who is paid for that fourth month is paid for that Sunday, and in the eight months that are not the four months she is not paid at all and she is at the bar anyway on about four days a month because a bar that is left down in the made road is a quarter of a mile of road that a cart has to go round, and there is no rate anywhere on the flats that pays a person for a day on which nothing crosses.**

**And a woman of about forty-one was on her own side of that bar at about the fourth hour, and about two thousand people in that town know her name and about four thousand people nine days off know nothing about it.**

The bar is oak with an iron strap and it is about nine feet long and it is set in two sockets in the piers and it weighs more than a man can lift alone out of its sockets, and it is lifted by a bar and a fulcrum and about four seconds of a good heave, and a bar that is left down in the made road is a bar a cart goes round, and a cart that goes round a bar is a cart that is a quarter of a mile longer on a made road.

---

**And a bar of oak and iron goes down across a cut in a made road in four seconds, and it takes four seconds because a lever of about four feet goes under the strap at the middle and a fulcrum is a stone in the cut with a flat top, and the fulcrum is a stone that a man put there, and about two people in that county have worked out a woman who takes a toll at a bar for eleven years has one fulcrum and about nine days of the year is the time of year when a person who can lift nine stone off a cradle by hand would want a second one.**

**The day-book in the hut by that bar is a page a month in a hand that goes back eleven years. It has no head on it. It has a ruled line across the top of each page and under that line two columns with no names over them and the left column is a day and the right column is a figure, and the figure is the day's take, and it is the only record in that county of what crosses that road, and it is not anybody's book and cannot be served with anything and is in no register and has no copy and the hut is about nine feet by seven and there is a stool in it and a lamp in it and nothing else anybody has ever put in it.**

**And a man came up the made road at about the fourth hour with two carts behind him and one of the two was empty, and the woman of about forty-one put the bar down, and the two carts came through, and she put the bar up, and none of the three of them said anything, because there is nothing to say at a bar four times a day and a Sunday is the day there is least to say.**

“**Four pence,**” she said, to the man in front.

“**For both.**”

“**Four a cart. I do not know what is in yours and neither do you and I have not asked you in about eleven years.**”

The man looked in the second cart for about four seconds, on the Sunday, in front of her, and about two people in that county have said since that a man looked in a cart on a Sunday because he had been looked in before, and that the whole of what that habit is worth is four seconds and a Sunday and nothing anybody can put in a column.

“**Oats,**” he said. “**Six bushel and I have got a note and I have never once been asked for it.**”

“**Then it is four pence and I have no column for a note,**” she said, and took it, and gave it back, and did not look at it.

**And about two people in that county have said since that a woman who takes a note in one hand and gives it back in the other and does not look at it, and that a man who has been asked for his note eleven years on a bar has never once had it read, and that the reason a note exists at all on that road is a buyer six miles up who does not trust a word, and that two people not trusting each other across six miles of salt is cheaper than one of them being wrong, and that neither of those is on a sheet either.**

“**You do not read them.**”

“**I do not read them,**” she said. “**There is one page a month and there is a figure on the top of it and the figure is a number of pence and I do not know whose, and a man who is careful enough to want a note is a man who is careful and not a man who is dishonest, and I have never had one of them try and I am not going to find out by reading one.**”

**Nobody writes down what crosses a bar on a Sunday. Eleven people went through that bar in the four months the crossing runs and about nine of them on an ordinary day and nobody on the eight months that are not the four months, and a day-book in a hut by that bar has about a page a month in it in a hand that goes back eleven years, and it is the only record in that county of what crosses that road and it is not anybody's book and cannot be served with anything and is not in any register there is.**

---

The two carts went on. **And eleven people came up that road on foot between the fourth hour and the eighth, going the other way, in ones and twos and once in a party of three, and every one of them paid a penny and every one of them said nothing, and two of them said nothing for a reason that had to do with eleven years.** And three horses came past led, at two pence each, and about the eighth hour two people came up from the wrong direction and did not want to cross because it was dark and the far side is four miles of flat with one cairn in it, and she told them about the cairn and they went back the way they had come.

**And that was the whole of a Sunday at a bar on the flats: eleven people, two carts and three horses, and two who did not cross.**

Eleven people at a penny is eleven pence. Two carts at four pence is eight pence. Three horses led at two pence is six pence. **Those three added on the page, because a toll-keeper who adds her day in her head is a toll-keeper who is wrong in a way nobody can check, are twenty-five pence.**

And about four people in that county have worked out since that a woman who takes a toll at a bar on the salt took twenty-five pence on a Sunday in the fourth month of the year the crossing runs, and that the eight months she is not paid for are the eight months the bar is up and a cart goes round it, and that a quarter of a mile of made road that a cart has to go round four times a day costs somebody something, and that in four months of the year the somebody is her and in the other eight months it is nobody anybody can name, and that there is no form on which a county can enter a quarter of a mile.

---

**And a man of thirty-two with a limp came up that made road on foot at about the eighth hour with his stick and about four seconds of trouble at the bar, and stood on his own side of it and did not put it down, and the woman of about forty-one looked at him for about two seconds and then said the thing that is the whole of it.**

“**You cannot get up that bank,**” she said.

“**No.**”

“**Then I will put it down for you and you will thank me for it, and in about a month there will be a price on this bar and you will be able to put it down yourself for a penny, and that is the whole of what a penny is for.**”

She put the bar down, he went through, she put it up. It takes about four seconds and the bar is heavier than a man can lift alone out of its sockets and she did it with a lever and a fulcrum and a man of thirty-two with a limp did not touch it.

“**What is the price,**” he said.

“**You have been on that bar about nine seconds and you want to know what I take.**”

“**I want to know what they are going to put on it.**”

“**They are going to put two shillings a cart on it,**” she said, “**or something like it, and it will be in about three weeks, and I have had three of these in about nine years and two of them were a price and one of them was a bar that came out at night and went in again, and I have never once known which of those three things was going to happen and I have not asked anybody and nobody has told me.**”

“**A penny a person and four pence a cart and two pence a horse led,**” she said, “**and I set that and I have set it for eleven years and before me my mother set it and nobody has ever written it down anywhere except in a page a month in a hut, and I do not expect that to last and I have not asked anybody whether it ought to.**”

He was quiet for long enough that a cart came up the road from the north and had to wait, and about two people in that county have worked out a man stood on the far side of a bar he had just been carried over and let a cart wait, and that he did not do it as a thing and that it is the only time in about two months he has let a cart wait.

“**They have put nine crossings and a price on a sheet,**” he said. “**Nine. And it is not your nine and I do not know whose nine it is, and a woman nine days off keeps a list of nine and has had it nineteen years and will not say whether it is the same nine.**”

“**I do not care whose nine it is,**” the woman of about forty-one said, and picked up her lever, and put it back against the pier. “**I have told you what I set. I am not going to tell you what I think of a sheet I have not seen, because I have nine days of a crossing in a year and eight months of a bar and a man who has not seen the sheet would say a great deal about it and I have seen eleven years of men who had not seen the sheet.**”

**And she said one more thing at about the eighth hour, and she had said it once before that year to a man who was complaining about the price of salt, and she did not say it again for another nine years, and nobody improved on it and nobody is going to.**

“**Four months I am paid and eight months the bar is up and a cart goes round,**” she said. “**You have come to tell me a price is coming. I have been at a bar for eleven years and every eleven years somebody has come to tell me a price is coming and twice somebody has come to tell me one has.**”

**And a woman of about forty-one said that to a man of thirty-two with a limp at a bar on a Sunday in the eighth month of Rainmonth at about the eighth hour, and about two thousand people in that town do not know it was said and about four thousand people nine days off do not know there is a bar, and the whole of what is written down anywhere about that Sunday is a page a month in a hut with eleven lines in it and a bar's weight in the taking column and nothing else at all.**
