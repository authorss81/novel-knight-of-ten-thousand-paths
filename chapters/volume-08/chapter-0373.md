# Chapter 0373

**The second day of Wolfmonth, YR 317, a Tuesday, and not a market day, and the second hour and the fourth hour and the seventh hour, on a made road four miles south of a town of about two thousand people on the salt, at a bar in a kerb in a made road, where a woman of about forty-one was on her own side of it and a man of thirty-two with a limp stood on the other one for about two hours and did not touch anything, and about two thousand people in that town do not know that anybody has been four miles to look at a bar.**

It is a quarter of a mile of good ground to go round and it takes a woman with a basket about nine minutes each way. **About two hundred people in four villages have been going round that quarter of a mile since the eighth of a month in Fallowmonth, which is a bar set on an anchor at the head of a towpath, and a bar set at the head of a towpath is one of about nine, and about nine hundred people in four villages have known that there is a bar there for about a month and not one of them has asked anybody which of the places it came off, and a bar at the head of a towpath is a thing that arrives before a conversation and stays after it.**

The man of thirty-two came out of that town at the second hour on his own and walked four miles, and it took him about an hour and ten minutes, and he went on the salt side of the kerb the whole way because the other side is soft. **He had a stick he cannot lean on and a left palm that was warm at the fourth hour and had been warm since the fourth hour, and two numbers in the pocket of that coat that he did not add together that morning and have never added together, and a page with nine crossings on it and no heads on it.**

At about the third hour a cart came up from the south with nine sacks of peat on it and went round. It took the cart eleven minutes to go round and about a quarter of that was the driver standing still deciding which way to go. **At about the fourth hour a bar went down. A woman of about forty-one put a lever under the strap at the middle and a stone in the cut and had it down in four seconds, and the bar lay across a made road with a chain on it and about two hundred people in four villages went round it, and she stood on the salt side of it and counted nothing.**

He watched it go down and he watched it come up and he watched it go down again at the fifth hour and come up at the sixth. **A bar is a bar. It is nine feet of oak with an iron strap and two brackets and a chain, and it does not ask a question and it does not carry a name and it is not a toll and it is not a rate and it is not anybody's business, and a person can get past it in about four minutes with a stick off a wall, and about two hundred people a week do, and a person who has decided to get past one is not stopped by it and is not doing anything anybody would write down.**

A bar and a toll are two different things and they have never once been in the same column. **A toll is a price, and the price on the Crown's own sheet for a cart at that crossing is two shillings, and a shilling is twelve pence, and a man gets nothing out of two shillings except the sum of it, and about two hundred people in four villages did not come that week and about nine people came the first week and about four came the second, and that is the whole of what a price does to a place that is not a market.**

The woman of about forty-one is paid for four months of the year and is at that bar on the other eight as well, and that is about eleven years of it. **She said the figure herself when he asked, and she said it the way a person says a figure they have said to themselves a great many times, and a woman who is paid for four months and is at the bar for eight is the only person within four miles of that road who is losing money on it, and nobody has ever found that out from her.**

---

**And then he did the ordinary arithmetic of a road, and the ordinary arithmetic of a road is the only arithmetic there is, and it took him about nine minutes at the bottom of that quarter of a mile with his back against a kerb.**

A road is only worth what it costs to be somewhere else. **The offer four hundred miles off is a road that holds a load in the four months when no other road on that side holds a load, and this side has a bar that is down for four months of the year and up for eight, and the four months and the four months are the same four months, and that was settled in a lane in this town a week ago by a man with a barrow and not by anybody since.**

**Which means the road four hundred miles off is only worth what can be put on it in the weeks when a quarter of a mile of good ground here is being walked round by women with baskets, and that a road four hundred miles off cannot be opened in the four months and cannot be worth anything in the eight, and that the whole of what has to happen on this side has to happen in the six weeks and a bit between now and the first of the season, and there are no people in this part of the world who are going to be told that by anybody in a form.**

---

**And about four people in that district have said since that a man of thirty-two with a limp stood four miles off a town of about two thousand people for two hours to find out that a road is only worth what it costs to be somewhere else, and that the finding out was nine minutes and the other hour and fifty minutes was standing at a bar watching a person get past it, and that he had been in that district about eight weeks and had not once done that.**

**And two people in that district have said since that about six weeks stand between that road and the first of the season on this side, and that in six weeks a yard of nine men can put a bar in and a man on it, and that a yard of nine men can also say no, and that the two of those are the same size and take the same time and that nobody knows which of the two is going to happen until it has happened.**

**And then he took the page out of his coat and looked at it for the first time since he had made it, and nobody was standing near him and nobody saw him do it, and the whole of its first use was nine minutes at the bottom of a quarter of a mile.**

The page has nine crossings on it and no heads and no columns and no ruling. **He made it at a bench-board in about nine minutes out of a Crown instrument of a kind that has six columns and five heads, and he put the nine places on it in the order they came, and he told nobody, and there is nowhere in this kingdom to enter it.**

**And a thing he has not said to anybody in about two years is the reason he can use it at all, and the thing is that the Crown's own series reads the same way in every room it is read in, and that a schedule with six columns and five heads is not a thing you take a figure out of, and a page with nine lines and no heads is a thing you can add up in your head on a wet road, and that the whole difference between the two of them is nine inches by six of paper.**

He used it, and it did not do what a page is for. **A page like that is for showing somebody where to go, and there was nobody to show it to, and what it did instead was let him count, and the count is that six of the nine are up for the same four months the road four hundred miles off runs, and that two of the nine are up for a fortnight on either side of that, and that the ninth is up the whole year and about four people a week go through it, and he has never been one of the four and does not know why he put it on a page in the first place.**

**And the crossing the offer is about is not one of the nine. It is four hundred miles off and it is not on his page and he cannot put it on his page, and two people in that district have worked out since that a man who made a schedule himself in about nine minutes found out inside nine minutes on a made road that the thing he needed to put on it was a person, and that a person is the one item a page cannot take.**

**And then he worked out what nine lines with no heads on them actually is, and he did not tell anybody and there was nobody within half a mile to tell, and a schedule is a schedule whether the head of it is a person's name or a person's trade or the name of a place, and he had made one in about nine minutes out of a sheet somebody else had ruled, and a sheet is a sheet whoever holds it.**

---

**And at about the sixth hour a man of about thirty with a mallet went by with nine nails and a plank and did not stop, and about two hundred people in four villages had been walking past a man with a limp standing on the wrong side of a bar for two hours by then, and not one of them had said anything to him, and he had not said anything to them either, and a man standing at a bar for two hours saying nothing is not a man doing anything that anybody could put in a column.**

**And about two hundred people in four villages use that quarter of a mile in a week and about nine of them use it with a load, and about four of the nine are at that bar on about four days in seven, and about two thousand people in that town do not know that the four of them are the ones a bar is costing.**

**A woman of about forty-one asked him what he was standing there for, and he said that he was watching a bar go down and come up, and she said that he could have watched it in a quarter of an hour instead of two hours and he said that he had about nine minutes of arithmetic to do in it and she said that a man who does arithmetic at a bar is a man who has come a long way to find out that a bar goes down in four seconds.**

“**That is a market street of about nine hundred stones,**” he said, “**four hundred miles up a county's own road, with nine men in a yard off the top of it and a bench-board, and I am going to it on Saturday.**”

“**Then somebody has told you a yard will do a thing,**” she said.

“**A yard of nine men has told me a yard of nine men will not,**” he said, “**about a season ago, and I have come back with a different thing, and I am not going to tell you what the different thing is, because you are on your own side of a bar and you have not asked me for it.**”

**And a man with a limp stood on the wrong side of a bar and told a woman who keeps one that somebody has told him a yard will do a thing, and what he actually said was that the yard had already said no, and a woman who has been on her own side of a bar for about eleven years is the last person in the world to be told a thing by a man who is about to ask nine men to do something four hundred miles away.**

---

**And at the seventh hour he put the page back in his coat, and the decision he had come out to that road to make is the one he had been not making for about eight weeks, and he made it in one sentence to a woman at a bar because she was the only person within four miles.**

**A yard of nine men and a bench-board will open a road on the flats, and the road will be a bar and a man on it, and neither of those is the offer, and the offer is still not answered and will not be answered in a lane, and both of those things have to be true at once and he had not found a way to hold them at once until the second hour of that morning.**

He was not going to answer it with a figure. **He was going to go and find nine men and give them an order, and the order was going to be the plainest one he had given anybody in about two years, and if nine men said no to that order then he was going to stand in their yard and not say the next thing, and about two people in that district have said since that a man of thirty-two with a limp worked out how he was going to be refused before he had asked, and that he had worked it out standing at a bar on made ground, and that the reason he stood there two hours is that there was nobody else within four miles he could have worked it out in front of.**

“**Saturday,**” he said. “**I will be four hundred miles off on Saturday.**”

“**Then I will have the bar up,**” the woman of about forty-one said, “**and you can come back down that kerb when you come back and I will not charge you for it, and that is the only thing I have ever said to anybody on that road and I am saying it to you because you have not asked me for anything.**”
