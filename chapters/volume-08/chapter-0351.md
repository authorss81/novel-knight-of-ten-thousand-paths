# Chapter 0351

**The twenty-sixth day of Longlight, YR 317, a Tuesday, and not a market day, and the second hour and the fourth hour and the fifth hour and the seventh hour, in a town of about four thousand people on a river, in a yard four hundred yards up that county's own road, where nine men were at a bench-board and a board about nine feet along it, and two men came up that road in one day with two figures off the flats that were the same figure to the penny, and about four thousand people in that town do not know that either of them came.**

Longlight is the month a boundary oath-stone is renewed in that county and the last renewal was on the first of it two years back and the next one is nineteen years off the last one, and a Tuesday in that month is a Tuesday. Two hundred of about four thousand people were out of their houses. The market four hundred yards long was shut. The road was open over four timber bays four hundred yards down a lane off the north end of it and about forty men were on it and a reach does not stop for anything.

**And a yard four hundred yards up the other way had nine men in it, and five of them were at seven pence a day and four of them were at eight, and the two figures were in that room before the fourth hour and the reason they were in that room is that a yard of nine men is the only thing within nine days of the flats that has ever put a span across anything.**

Five men at seven pence is thirty-five pence. Four men at eight pence is thirty-two pence. The two added on the page, because a yard that adds its day in its head is a yard that has lost a day, are **sixty-seven pence**, and about two people in that county have worked out sixty-seven pence is the whole cost of nine men for one day and that a day is about a ninth of a week and that the ninth of a week is what it costs to know that nine men exist.

---

**And a yard of nine men at about the first hour of that Tuesday was doing what a yard of nine men does, and the doing of it is the reason two men came up that road at all.**

Four of the nine were on the frame of a cart that had come in over the reach two days before with a lynch pin out and a side rail sprung, and the lynch pin had been cut out on the Thursday by a man of about nineteen with an empty hod and had been replaced on the Friday by a new one turned out of a bar of about an inch and a half, and the new one was a quarter of an inch too big and the cart had to be shimmed, and a quarter of an inch of shim in a lynch pin is a thing that gets found four months later. The man of about nineteen had held the empty hod for four months and had not been given a job with a hod in it, and had been given this one, and had done it in about nine minutes, and about two people in that county have said since that a yard keeps a boy of nineteen for four months before it lets him cut out a lynch pin, and that the four months are not patience and are not kindness and are that a man of twenty-five cannot tell a man of nineteen whether he is any good and will not find out in front of nine men.

The boy of fifteen was at the bench-board with a sheet in front of him and nothing to copy on it, and he was copying the space under the last number he had copied, which is a thing he has done every working day for about a year. **A figure and a space take the same hand. A hand that has learned a figure has not learned a space, because a space is a thing a person decides to put in, and about two people in that county have said since that a boy of fifteen has been practising a decision eleven times a week for a year and that nobody on that yard has worked out whether he knows there is nothing in it.**

The press in the room at the end of the yard stands open and has stood open since the twenty-fourth day of a month in Longlight two years back. It belongs to that yard and it cannot be served with anything, and in it on that morning was a bundle of four sheets with nothing written on the outside of it, and the four sheets are the only written refusal in that yard's book that has never left the county that made it, and it is on the yard's book, and it is never going to leave that county, and there is a man of about nineteen who put it in there four days after it arrived in a bag with a number of an office on it and nothing at all against a person, and nobody in that room has ever asked him what it was and he has never said.

---

**The first man came at about the second hour and put a folded sheet on the bench-board, and the boy of fifteen at that board copied the figure off it into the yard's book because a figure is a thing set and a sheet is not.**

The figure was six pence a man for a day, and eight pence for a man who could read a sill, and the flats wanted a crew at a crossing for the four months the crossing runs.

“**That is under the standing rate,**” the man of twenty-five said, and did not touch the sheet.

“**It is,**” the first man said. “**It is four pence under the standing rate and the standing rate has not moved in two years and I am not the office and I do not set it.**”

“**Then I will not take it at four pence under,**” the man of twenty-five said, “**because a yard that takes a road rate in Harvestmonth is a yard that has no Harvestmonth.**”

“**Harvestmonth is eight weeks off,**” the first man said. “**I am telling you what it is and not asking you what it will be.**”

And the boy of fifteen copied the figure into the yard's book, and the figure is a thing set, and a man who set a figure in that book can be called out of that yard on the strength of it for as long as the book is dry, and about four people in that county have said since that a folded sheet is the only piece of paper in that yard's history that anybody has ever been able to be sent away on, and that the boy of fifteen did it in about four seconds without asking anything.

---

**The second man came at about the fifth hour, and he came up that road on foot and not on a horse, and he had a sheet too, and the figure on it was the same figure.**

Six pence a man for a day. Eight pence for a man who could read a sill. And the second man said, before he said anything else, that it would be paid in salt.

“**Which salt,**” the man of twenty-five said.

“**The Marl salt. The coarse, out of the second boil, in a cart, at your gate, and it is a cart a fortnight and not a cart a month, and I have eleven carts and eight of them are on the road this week.**”

“**A bushel of imported salt is three shillings,**” the man of about sixty said, from the back of the yard, packing lime, and he did not stop packing it. “**The Marl salt is not imported and the Marl salt is not three shillings and I have bought it four times and I have never once paid three shillings a bushel for it, and a man who says a bushel of salt is three shillings on a yard in this county is quoting a book.**”

**And about two people in that county have worked out since that the only man in that yard not paid out of it said the whole of what the two offers were, in one sentence and without stopping, and that he said it with a hod in his hand, and that nobody on that yard took it up, and that a man of about nineteen with an empty hod was the only man standing in a position to ask him a question about it and did not.**

**And about two people in that county have worked out since that a man of about thirty-five came nine days up a made road with a sheet and stood at a bench-board for about an hour and a half and was quoted at by a man of about sixty who was packing lime against a heap with a lid on it, and that neither of them knew anything about the other and that the whole of the price of a season on the flats was settled in that exchange and neither of the two people who settled it was in a position to settle it.**

“**I have quoted a book,**” the second man said, and did not look up. “**I have quoted a book because a sheet has a figure on it and the figure was six pence and I have carried it nine days to say the six pence is six pence and not four, because salt at four pence over a bushel is eight shillings over a week for nine men and that is not nothing.**”

He put the sheet on the bench-board and the boy of fifteen did not copy it, because the second sheet was the same figure and a figure that has been in the book once is a thing set and putting it in twice is a different kind of entry.

“**You did not copy that one,**” the second man said.

“**It is the same figure,**” the boy of fifteen said, and went on copying.

“**It is the same figure and it is not the same figure,**” the second man said, “**and the difference between those two is a cart a fortnight and there is no column for it in that book and there never has been.**”

---

**And the man of twenty-five put both sheets on the bench-board side by side and neither of them went into the yard's book, and that is the yard's whole rule and it has never been explained to anybody outside it and it is not explained here.**

Nothing that belongs to a man goes in that yard's book. Only a thing set. **A folded sheet with a figure on it is a thing set, and a man who brings it is a man, and a rate offered to a yard is a claim and a claim is not a thing and a claim cannot be in that book because the moment a claim is in that book the yard has agreed to it, and a yard that has agreed to a rate cannot be a yard that says no to one.** The boy of fifteen copied the first figure and the second man watched him not copy the second one, and about four people in that county have worked out since that the rule was taught to a boy of fifteen by example and not by sentence and has therefore never once been argued about in that yard in about eleven years, and that the man of about nineteen with the empty hod was standing four feet off the board the whole time and understood about half of it.

“**You are not writing mine down,**” the second man said.

“**I am not writing either of them down,**” the man of twenty-five said, and put his hand flat on the sheet. “**The boy wrote the first one because a figure is a thing set. That was before you came. I am not going to take that back out of a boy's book in front of a stranger and I am not going to put the second one in.**”

“**Then I have nine days in a bag for nothing,**” the second man said.

“**You have nine days in a bag, and you have said a figure out loud in a yard in front of nine men, and in about four months there will be people on those flats who will know that figure because you said it here, and that is worth more than a book and it is not in a book.**”

**And the second man stood in that yard for about a minute and worked out that this was true, and about two people in that county have worked out since that a yard which keeps nothing that belongs to a man out of its book has accidentally built the only kind of record in the county that cannot be produced by anybody, and that the man of twenty-five did not do that on purpose and would not be able to say how it happened.**

---

**And a woman came to that yard at about the third hour with a child of about four, because there is nowhere in that county nine days off that a woman of twenty-six can leave a child for two hours, and she was on the step for an hour and a half of the four hours she had, and about two people in that county have worked out since that a yard of nine men has a child on its step four days a week in a market season and that nobody on that yard has ever paid anybody for holding one, and that a man of twenty-five has never once asked the yard's book to carry it, and that the yard's book carries a bench-board's figures and a lynch pin's size and a set of four sheets in a wrapper in a press and has never once carried a child.**

**And a man of thirty-two with a limp came into that yard at about the seventh hour and was not asked anything, and the two sheets were still on the board, and he read both of them in about four seconds, which is what he is for.**

He put his stick against the leg of the bench-board to get his weight off his right hip, which is the only reason that stick exists and the only thing it is good for, and he looked at the two figures and did not say anything for about a minute and a half.

“**Nine men at eight crossings,**” he said. “**That is a man at a crossing for the four months the crossing runs, and it is the whole of what the offer is, and it is a good offer, and it is not a position.**”

“**It is two offers,**” the man of twenty-five said.

“**It is one offer twice,**” the man of thirty-two said, “**and the one that came on foot and said it would be in salt is the one somebody wants you to take, and I do not know which of the nine crossings yet and I am not going to guess in front of a book.**”

“**You want me on them,**” the man of twenty-five said.

“**I want the crossings to still be crossings in Fallowmonth,**” the man of thirty-two said. “**Those are two different sentences and I have known since about the fourth hour that the second one costs nine men and the first one costs me, and I have not worked out yet which of the two I would rather pay.**”

And he went out of that yard at about the seventh hour with his stick and the two sheets stayed on the bench-board, and the man of about sixty went on packing lime against a heap with a lid on it and said nothing else, and the boy of fifteen copied the space under the first figure as well as the figure, and the space is empty and nobody in that yard has ever told him what goes in it and nobody is going to.

**And about four thousand people in that town do not know that a yard of nine men was offered the same figure twice in one day by two men who came nine days apart on the same road, and that one of them put it in a boy's book and one of them did not, and that the difference between those two is the whole of what happened in that yard on that Tuesday.**
