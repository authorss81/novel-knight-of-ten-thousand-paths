# Chapter 0408

**The twenty-eighth day of Greenmonth, YR 318, a Friday and not a market day, and the second hour and the fourth hour and the sixth hour, at a roadhouse nine miles on from the kilns on a made road between a market and about four hundred people, where about nine people a week are put a plate and a bed for about thirty years, and the woman who does it is about fifty, and about four hundred yards of that road is a ditch that takes a loaded barrow down about two inches, and a boy of about eleven with a pail is on its shoulder at every hour of the daylight and has been since the middle of Harvestmonth.**

## One

**She is called nothing at this roadhouse. She has been at it about thirty-one years and she bought it off a man of about sixty and she has kept it open every day of the thirty-one, and there is a stove in the kitchen and a fire in the front room and about nine feet between them, and the plates go into the front room and the beds are upstairs and about nine people a week come through a door that is not shut in the daytime.**

**The first person of the day comes in off the made road at about the eleventh hour and there have been about four people who came in at the eleventh hour and were still sitting there at the second hour of the afternoon,** and about nine people a week is not a season. **It is four in the week in Mudmonth, which nobody comes off a road in, and eleven or twelve in the week in Harvestmonth, and the difference between those two numbers is the whole of what a roadhouse on a made road is, and there is no figure for it anywhere except a woman of about fifty and a plate.**

The practice is the eighth of the eight and it is the shortest sentence in the song.

Feed the stranger first.

**And the whole of the argument about it, which has been going on for about a hundred years in about nine counties, is whether the stranger is fed before he is asked what he wants or before he is asked who he is, and about four places do it the first way and about five do it the second way, and this one does it both, in that order, and does not do it in a hurry.**

Aren Kest had been in the front room about nine minutes when he said the thing that made the rest of the day.

“**How many.**”

“**How many what.**”

“**How many people.**”

The woman of about fifty did not answer that for about a minute, and she did not answer it because she was deciding whether to lie, and the deciding took a minute, and a person who decides whether to lie for a minute in front of a stranger is a person who is about to tell the truth badly.

“**I have got a figure,**” she said.

## Two

**The figure is on the end of a board in the kitchen and it is a column of figures and there is no head on it. It is four feet of board and about three hundred and seventy figures on it, and that is nine to the inch, and nine to the inch is the most a person can write with a chalk and still be able to find next month.**

**That is the whole of it and it is about four feet of a board and it goes back about thirty years and the figures are in a hand that has changed three times, and there is no head, and there is no rule about how often a figure goes on, and a person who has kept it for thirty-one years cannot say what any of the about three hundred and seventy things in it means except that every one of them is a month's worth of plates, and a month of plates is about four hundred and thirty figures, and she has never once added a plate to the board in her life.**

**And she cannot read out any of it and will not, and she said why in ninety-three words, and the ninety-three words are the best thing anybody said to him in about nine days.**

“**If I read it out you would have the figure and I would have said it,**” she said, “**and I have said a figure out loud twice in thirty-one years and both times it was a mistake, and once of the two times a man of about forty came to the door afterwards with a sheet and wanted it in writing for a bench, and I did not give it to him, and he went away, and I do not know where it went and I have not asked and I am not going to.**”

“**What is it.**”

“**It is four shillings a week in bread and cheese,**” she said, “**which is about two hundred and eight shillings a year, and there are about nine plates a week, and a plate is not a plate, it is bread and cheese and a bit of whatever there was.**”

**A man of about forty-three who had come in with a load of glass and had been fed before anybody asked him anything did that in his head in about four seconds, and she said that he was the second person in thirty-one years to do it, and that the first one had not done it in his head.**

**A man of about forty-three said, in about forty-four words, that he had come about a load of glass and that the glass was for a man nine miles on and that he did not want the plate and had been given it anyway, and that this was the fourth week he had come and the fourth plate, and that nobody had ever once said the word stranger to him in his life and that he had not noticed that until about eleven minutes ago.**

**And that is the answer to the argument nobody in that room had started, and it is the answer that this practice has been giving for about nine hundred years, and it is that the word is not the point and the plate is the point, and a woman of about fifty with a kitchen about nine feet off her front room has been conducting the argument for thirty-one years with a pot and has never once said the word out loud and does not know that strangers are a category in her house.**

**And nobody in that room added it up for thirty years, and that is not modesty, because nobody in that room has the number of years in a column, and the first person who worked it out is twenty-four and was fed a plate before he was asked his name.**

## Three

**The boy of about seventeen came in at about the second hour of the afternoon with a hand-basket of glass and had been coming into that room for four years and had been fed about four hundred times and had never once been asked for anything at all, which is the best thing about the room and is not an accident.**

He came in, and he put the basket down, and the woman of about fifty put a plate in front of him, and then she said the thing that she says to about nine people a week, which is a question about where somebody has come from and not a question about who they are.
He answered it. He ate. And then he said, with his mouth not full, that he had a book.

“**You have got a book,**” she said.

“**I have got a book with about four hundred things in it,**” Ivo Serrat said, “**and I cannot read the words in it, and I have never shown it to anybody, and I have said that out loud twice now and both times to grown men who had not asked me for it.**”

“**Show me,**” she said.

**And that is the whole of what happened in that room in about four minutes, and nobody in it decided to do it, and the two people who did it had never heard of one another a fortnight before.**

She brought the board in off the kitchen wall and stood it on the table with the end with the figures on it facing him, and he put his book down and opened it, and neither of them said anything at all for about two minutes.

**Four feet of a board, no head, thirty years. About four hundred entries, no head, four years. A figure against a figure and a mark against a mark, and two collections of numbers with nobody's name on either of them, kept by two people who cannot read what the other one has written, about nine miles apart on a made road, and neither of them knowing about a fortnight ago that the other one existed.**

They found about nine marks in common. **Nine marks, and not one word between them, and a man of about thirty-two with a limp stood in a front room with a stick he could not lean on and watched two people find nine things in common in about four minutes with no speech at all, and the ninth mark was a mark neither of them could read, and there is no reason on the board and there is no reason in the book, and nobody in that room has the smallest idea what it is, and the person who could tell them is about forty miles off and does not know that a woman of about fifty has been keeping a column for thirty-one years.**

## Four

The man of about thirty-three with a barrow came in at about the fourth hour and put a load down in the yard and came round about a hundred yards of that yard on the grass because the ditch on the salt side takes a loaded barrow down about two inches, and he did not say why, and he came in and was fed, and the woman of about fifty asked him where he had come from, and he told her, and then she said that she had heard him in a market on Saturday and that it was a good song, and every person in that room stopped for about a second and a half.

“**I know,**” he said.

“**You should not have said it in there.**”

“**I know that as well,**” he said, and he did not say anything else about it, and he has not said anything else about it, and about forty people in a market town of about two thousand on the salt have heard him say nine lines and about nine of them have heard the ninth, and he has worked out what he did and has not worked out what to do about it, and he has not been asked to.

## Five

**The boy of about eleven with the pail is four hundred yards west of that door and has been on the shoulder of that ditch at every hour of the daylight since the middle of Harvestmonth, and nobody has ruled the ditch and nobody was going to, and about two hundred people in a town of about two thousand have gone round a quarter of a mile every day since and have not complained about it once.**

**He is not the roadhouse's and he does not come in. He is the reason the man of about thirty-three with a barrow stopped coming up that road in the winter, and he does not know that, and he is eleven, and a boy of eleven on a shoulder at every hour of the daylight is a thing about nine yards of soft ground and about two hundred carts a week and nothing else, and the two hundred people who go round it are not cruel and are not complaining and are simply not going to be the ones who start it.**

**The eight words were left on the front-room table of a roadhouse nine miles on from the kilns and were not taken by anybody.** She read seven of them out loud in the road, in the voice she uses for people at a door, and she did not read the eighth.

**And the eighth is the one in front of her in the order and it is the one she has been doing for thirty-one years without a song and without anybody's permission, and a practice you have been doing for thirty-one years does not need to be sung to you.**

And what she has is the closest thing in nine counties to the thing a man of thirty-two with a limp came here to build, and she has had it for thirty-one years, and it has no head on it, and it is four feet of a board, and it is not in a room anybody has ever been asked about, and about three hundred and seventy figures on it have never been read out loud to anybody, and the only two people who have ever looked at it are the woman who keeps it and a boy of seventeen who keeps another one and cannot read it.
