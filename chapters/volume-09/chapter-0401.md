# Chapter 0401

**The twenty-sixth day of Mudmonth, YR 318, a Monday and not a market day, and the second hour and the eleventh hour and the thirteenth hour, in a yard of four lime kilns two days west of Merefen on a made road, in a country of about four hundred people with one market and a road that goes nowhere the county trades, where a kiln is drawn in about nine hours and a draw takes four people and a song, and the fourth kiln in that yard is cold and has been cold since the middle of Harvestmonth, and about nine loads of peat are stacked against the second kiln's back wall and are stacked every year about this time whether the kiln is drawn or not.**

## One

The second kiln is the second one from the gate. **A lime kiln is a beehive of about nine feet across the bottom and about fourteen high, and it is not a kiln at all without a fire under it for nine days, and the nine days are the reason a yard of four has one cold kiln in it in Mudmonth and not two, because a yard that draws two at once in Mudmonth is a yard that has a buyer for both and a yard that has a buyer for both does not have a yard, it has a contract.**

**There are about forty hands in that yard and there are four families in it and the four families are not related to each other and have not been related for about sixty years, and they share a yard, a water butt, a cart, and a song, and the four of those things are shared on different terms and nobody has ever written any of the four terms down.**

**The fourth kiln is the only thing in that yard that is on somebody's list.** It has been cold since the middle of Harvestmonth and it is the first kiln at the gate and the first one anybody driving past sees, and a kiln standing cold at a gate in a dry country is an advertisement for about nine weeks and then it is only a thing in the way. **The three kilns behind it are in use and they are nine feet apart and a bar goes between two of them, and the reason a bar goes between two of them and not between all four is that a bar is about nine feet long and a kiln is about nine feet across and this is a thing about the width of a bar and not about anything else.**

**The buyer is about eleven miles off and he comes twice in a year and he looks at the face of the cold kiln from the road and he does not come in, and about forty people in that yard know what he thinks and not one of them has said it out loud, because a thing a buyer thinks is not a thing a yard has to answer to until he says it.**

The second kiln is run by a woman of about forty-four. **Her name is Brenna Tull and she is called that and not anything else, and she has run the second kiln for about nine years, and before her a man of about fifty-eight ran it who is her uncle by marriage and who is in a house two miles off and is not spoken of in the yard and does not come to the gate.** A boy of about seventeen wheels barrows between the second kiln and the third and has done for four years and comes in from four miles off with glass in a hand-basket twice a week.

“**You are the stone man,**” the woman said, and not as a question.

“**I buy stone,**” Aren said.

“**You buy stone and you have bought stone off this yard for two years and you have paid on the day twice and late once and you did not send anybody for it,**” she said. “**That is three things and I keep the three.**”

## Two

The draw began at about the second hour. **A kiln is not opened. A hole is made in the upper stone at about the second hour with a bar and a hammer, and the hole is made small, and the lime is let out of it by nine rounds over about nine hours, and each round takes the heat out of the stone a little and the man at the bar takes a little out and no more.** A man who takes too much at one round gets nine hours of work in one and a yard that has nine hours of work in one does not have a kiln, it has a hole with a fire in it and about forty people standing round a hole.

**The four who drew it were the woman of about forty-four, a man of about fifty-one who has been at that kiln for about twenty-two years, a boy of about nineteen who came with her out of a house two miles off, and the boy of about seventeen with the glass basket, who was not asked and was not sent away and whose hands knew where the bar went before anybody told them.**

They sang for the nine rounds. **The song is nine lines long and it is sung in parts, two parts, and the beat is the bar coming down on the rim of the hole and the words going on the way back up, and the bar comes down about seventy times a round and the words go on the way back up seventy times, and a man who has done it for twenty-two years can put a word on any one of the seventy and does not choose.**

**This is the song, and the woman of about forty-four gave it out loud in the second round and it is nine lines.**

> **Ask the far side.**
> **Two books.**
> **Never a person twice.**
> **Read it yourself.**
> **Say it in a room.**
> **Leave the name off.**
> **Anybody may come.**
> **Feed the stranger first.**
> **—**

**The ninth line was sung on one held note and there was nothing on it.** The man of about fifty-one held it the way a man holds a note he has held four thousand times, and the woman of about forty-four came down out of the note about a second before the bar came down and did not put a word in the gap, and the boy of about seventeen came down out of it a half second after her and did not put a word in the gap either, and the round went on.

Aren stood nine feet off with a stick he could not lean on and watched nine rounds go by.

“**Whose is it,**” he said, at about the fifth hour.

“**It is not mine,**” the woman said.

“**Whose is it.**”

“**It is not mine and I have not asked,**” she said, “**and I have been in this yard nineteen years and the man of fifty-eight was in it before me and the woman of about sixty-nine who is dead was in it before him, and I have asked one of them once and I did not ask it well, and I have not asked it again.**”

“**Then what is the ninth one.**”

“**That is not a word,**” she said. “**That is a road.**”

The man of about fifty-one said, without stopping, that somebody ought to write it down, and the woman of about forty-four said that he could write it.

“**I cannot write,**” she said, “**and I will not say it out loud twice in a room with a stranger in it so that a man with a pen can have it, and you have been in this yard nineteen years and you know I cannot write.**”

“**I have not asked you to say it twice.**”

“**You did not have to,**” she said. “**A room with a man in it who cannot write is a room where a thing gets said once and written down, and then there is a copy, and then there is a person you would have to ask about the copy. My house has no door on the back and I have not had one since I was thirty-one, and that is the whole of what I have got instead of a copy.**”

**And that is the whole of what she has got instead of a copy, and it is why the first of the eight is in her mouth and on a beat and not on a sheet, and there is no version of that in which it is written down.**

## Three

A kiln draw is a thing four people do and about nine people watch, and the nine watchers are not idle, and there is a hand on the wheelbarrow every eleven minutes and a hand on the fire every four minutes and a woman on the second kiln's rake. **What happened after the fifth hour is that the boy of about seventeen with the glass basket went to a stone bench on the far side of the yard and took a book out of inside his coat and wrote in it for about twenty minutes, and he wrote with a stick of chalk and not with a pen because the chalk was on the bench and the pen was in his coat and he could not do both.**

**He is called Ivo Serrat and he is seventeen and he has carried glass between kilns on that road for four years and he can read figures and not words, and the reason is that nobody taught him and not because he was refused.**

The book is a book of figures. **It has about four hundred entries in it and every entry is a number and a mark, and a mark is a small figure with a stroke through it, and the strokes are not words and are not a code and are his own.** He made it in the first year. **He has never shown it to anybody and he has never been asked to, and he said so himself before anybody could ask him, which is a thing a boy of seventeen does when he has decided in advance that a thing is not going to be taken off him.**

“**You have written the song down,**” Aren said.

“**I write down what they say,**” Ivo Serrat said. “**I have written it down four times. I do it every draw, and I have done it since the first year, and I do it because it is nine lines and there is nowhere else to keep nine lines.**”

He turned the book round on the bench without giving it away and put his finger on a line and did not read it.

“**I cannot read this,**” he said, “**and I have not said that to a grown man before, and I am saying it to you because you have not asked me for the book.**”

“**Can you read the first line.**”

“**I know the first line,**” the boy said. “**I have heard it four times and I know what it is because of what they do when they sing it. You cannot sing a number.**”

**That is the whole of what he could do and he did all of it in about nine sentences, and he had been carrying a book of things he could not read for four years and nobody in nine counties knew it, and the reason nobody knew it is that a book of figures looks exactly like a book of figures.**

## Four

The ninth line is ruled in the book and there is nothing on it. **The boy ruled it in the first year, with a straight edge and a nail, in the same hand and the same chalk, on the same afternoon, in the same place, and he has ruled it four times since because the page wears and he rules it again, and he does not know why he rules it, and he was asked why in the sixth round by the woman of about forty-four and he said because it is there, and she said that is not a reason, and he said that is the reason he has.**

**A line ruled for nothing is the cheapest kind of record there is, and there are about nine hundred of them in that book, and a man of about sixty who keeps two kinds of minute in a town two hundred miles off would tell you that a line ruled for nothing is a line somebody meant to fill in and did not, and he would be right, and about four in ten of the nine hundred are.**

What the woman of about forty-four said at about the eleventh hour, with the kiln about half down and the yard eating, was this.

“**You have been standing nine feet off for nine hours,**” she said. “**You are not here about stone. You are here about the ninth line, and the ninth line is a road, and I am not going to say the name of a road in a yard with a stranger in it, and I have not said it in nineteen years and I am not going to start because a man with a stick has stood still for nine hours.**”

“**I am not asking you to say it to me.**”

“**You are asking me to have said it,**” she said, “**and that is a different thing and it is worse, because a road that has been said once in a yard of four kilns is a road that nine people have heard, and I have spent nineteen years not saying it and you have undone the whole of it by standing still.**”

Aren said that he was sorry, and she said that he was not to be, because there was nothing to be sorry for, and that was the end of it, and then she went back to the fire.

The cost of the day is a column, and the column is what a draw costs and it is in peat and lime and not one of the two is a wage.

**Nine loads of peat at three shillings and tenpence a load, and a load is forty-six pence: forty-six, ninety-two, a hundred and thirty-eight, a hundred and eighty-four, two hundred and thirty, two hundred and seventy-six, three hundred and twenty-two, three hundred and sixty-eight, four hundred and fourteen — four hundred and fourteen pence. Eleven sacks of quicklime at two pence: twenty-two. Four hundred and fourteen and twenty-two is four hundred and thirty-six pence, and a mark is forty-eight pence, so the draw cost nine marks and four pence, and that is the whole of what it cost, and none of the four people who drew it was paid out of any part of it.**
