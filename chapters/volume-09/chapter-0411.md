# Chapter 0411

**The thirteenth day of Longlight, YR 318, a Saturday and a market day, and the second hour and the fifth hour and the eighth hour, in an upper room over a saddler's shop in a fen town of about four thousand people, taken for the day, where about nine hundred people are in a street four hundred yards long below, and the street is not the subject and the room is, and the room is nine paces by fourteen and there are about eleven people in it and nine of them are in it because it is a Saturday.**

## One

The test was a question, and he put it in the ordinary way, standing at the back of the room, one person at a time, and it took about eleven minutes to ask it eleven times and about an hour to hear what came back.

**The question was who has to be here for it to go on.** Not who does it. Not who is to blame for it, and not who would be missed. Who has to be here, so that on a morning in a month with nothing in it, when nobody in particular wants to, the thing is still done.

Nobody answered it the way he wanted and nobody refused to answer it, and both of those are the finding.

**A woman of about thirty-four who was in that room for a load of salt and nothing else said, before he had finished the question, that he was the third person to ask her that in about four years, and that the other two had been a man with a form and a man from a house two hundred miles off with a deed, and that she had answered both of the others the same way and would answer this one the same way, and she said it without any weight on it at all, and then she told him.**

**Then he stood at the back of the room and let about an hour go by, which is the only way a question like that gets asked twice in a morning without anybody noticing the second one, and about nine hundred people were in the street below and the floor of that room lifted about four times in the hour and every person in it looked at the door each time and nobody said anything.**

## Two

There were about eleven people in that room and four kinds of answer came out of it, and the room found the four by about the fifth hour without anybody deciding to look for them.

**A man of about thirty-eight who had come in off the salt road with a load of glass said that the person who has to be here is the man whose father's name is on the gate, and that if that man dies the thing stops until there is another one, and that there usually is, and he said it in the tone of a man describing a lock.**

**A man of about fifty-eight who keeps a pound in that township said that whoever is in the chair that night is the person, and that the chair goes round, and that a man sits in it about a month and then it goes to the next, and that the going round has been going round in this county for about a hundred and forty years, and that nobody in that township can tell you from one month to the next who is in it.**

**A woman of about thirty-one said that anybody in nine hundred people may put their name in the hat at the back of the room and anybody may come and look at the names before the names are read, and that about nine people do it every year and take a month at a time, and she said that this is the fairest arrangement she knows of anywhere and that she has never once heard of a thing that is done that way.**

**A man of about sixty-seven who had come in to be fed and had said almost nothing for two hours said that there is nobody, and that there has never been anybody, and that the thing is done and there is nobody in it to name, and he said it to the fire and not to anybody.**

The fifth of them was not one of the four kinds, and the room tried to make it one for about nine minutes and then gave up on it, and the reason the room gave up is that it neither agreed with the four nor disagreed with them, and the man of about twenty wrote five lines and could not tell which of the five was the odd one out and neither could anybody else, and the fifth line is still legible on that board to anybody who stands at the right angle.

**A man of about twenty-two who keeps a second-hand stall said that the person who has to be here is whoever has the next one coming, and that if the next one is nine years off then the person is nine years off, and that there are four people in this town who have a next one coming and about nine hundred who have not and the nine hundred are not behind anything, they are just here, and that he does not think the nine hundred are behind the four and that nobody in that room is either.**

## Three

The first thing anybody did with the four was number them, and the numbering was wrong inside about four minutes, and the man who did it was a man of about twenty hired for the day at four pence with a piece of chalk and a bench-board, and he had been hired for the day and not for the week and was to be paid at the eighth hour whether the sheet was finished or not.

He wrote one, two, three, four down the left of the first four lines, in the order the four had been said, and the order the four had been said was the order the eleven people had been standing in the room, and the fifth line had nothing against it at all.

**Then somebody asked which of the four was the first one, and there was no answer in the room for about half a minute, and the reason there was no answer is that a family and a nobody cannot be counted against each other, and a chair and a hat cannot either, and the four of them have nothing that is the same size as one another except that they are all a thing that gets done.**

A man of about forty-seven, who had not spoken for an hour and had not eaten anything, said the thing that the rest of the room had been agreeing with for an hour and that nobody was going to be able to write down, and he said it in nine words and it is worth having exactly.

**“One is first now, and there is no first.”**

**And nobody agreed with him and nobody disagreed with him, and the man of about twenty took the chalk and rubbed the four numbers out and did not put them back, and the five lines were still five lines.**

**And then the woman of about twenty-seven who had taken the room for the day was asked the question herself, in the ordinary way, and she gave a sixth answer and it was not a sixth kind either, and she gave it in thirteen words and then apologised for giving it in thirteen words and said it was because she had taken the room and not because it was the answer.**

“**Nobody has to be here. I let the room and I go home.**”

**And a man of about twenty-two with the second-hand stall said that is the only one of the six that is any use and that he had not thought of it, and the man of about thirty-eight with the glass said that the man whose father's name is on the gate cannot let a room and go home, which is true, and the man of about fifty-eight said that the man in the chair cannot either, and the man of about sixty-seven said nothing at all, and about nine people in that room worked out inside about four minutes that five of the six answers had a person in them who could be asked, and that the room was the one that could not be asked, and that she was standing in it.**

## Four

And then it turned out that not one of the four people he had come about was in that room.

**The woman of about forty-four who runs the second of four kilns is two days west and has not been in this town since Harvestmonth. The man of about forty-one who walks two miles of boundary is thirty miles south and was in his fold. The mason of about sixty-eight is nine miles off the kilns at an arch over a beck and has not been in a room in about eleven years. The woman of about fifty who puts a plate in front of about nine people a week is nine miles on from the kilns and does not come to markets and has not come to this one in about nine years.**

**And the four answers in that room came from four people who do a thing, and not one of them from a person who has a practice, and the difference between those two is the whole of what he came to find out and it took a room over a saddler's shop and about an hour to find it.**

The man of about twenty said, at about the seventh hour, that he had written five lines and had not been told what to do with them, and that he would be paid at the eighth hour and would take the lines away or leave them as they were.

**“Leave them,**” a woman of about twenty-seven said, who had taken that room for the day and was owed nothing for it. “**The next one in will see five lines with no head on them and that is a thing a room does about nine times in a year.**”

## Five

The cost of the day is a column and there is one line in it that is a wage and four that are not.

**A room over a saddler's at six pence for the day, a stove at two pence, a bundle of wood at three pence, a man of about twenty hired for the day at four pence, and a loaf at one penny: six, two, three, four, one is sixteen pence.** A mark is forty-eight pence and sixteen pence is a third of a mark, and it is the whole of what a room over a saddler's costs on a market morning, and about four of the eleven people in it came in because it was a Saturday and not because of anything else.

**And he did not number the four, and the four went back to the four people who had said them, and the fifth line with nothing numbered against it was left where it was, and a man of about thirty-two with a limp stood at the back of that room with a stick he could not lean on and worked out that he was going to have to go to four places to find out four things that four people in one room had already told him, and that the four places are two days west and thirty miles south and nine miles off the kilns and nine miles on from them, and that a chair that goes round and a family and a hat and nobody are four arrangements and not four of the eight, and that none of the four people who said them has ever heard of the other three, and that he is not going to be able to ask any of the four the same question twice.**
