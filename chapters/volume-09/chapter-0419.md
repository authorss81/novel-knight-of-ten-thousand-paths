# Chapter 0419

**The eighteenth day of Rainmonth, YR 318, a Saturday and a market day, and the second hour and the fifth hour and the ninth hour, in the room over a cooper's shop, taken by the day, where a market runs on two days in seven and this is the second of them, and about nine hundred people are in a street four hundred yards long below, and the bench-board in that room has had three sheets on it since the first of a month in Greenmonth, and this is the fourth, and the fourth is four lines in chalk and a ninth line ruled and nothing on it.**

## One

The four were laid out side by side on the bench-board at the second hour and they were in chalk, and the chalk was on the bench-board and there was about a millimetre of dust on the board and the chalk went into the dust and would not come off for about four years.

**Seven, anybody may come, at a yard of four lime kilns two days west. Four, read it yourself, at an arch over a beck nine miles off that yard. Three, never a person twice, in a fold of ground thirty miles south, about two hundred people and a boundary of nine stones walked by one man. Eight, feed the stranger first, at a roadhouse nine miles on from the kilns, kept for thirty-one years by a woman of about fifty with no head on anything.**

**And under the four, in the same chalk and the same hand, a ninth line ruled and nothing on it, because a man of about twenty was told to rule nine lines in a back room in Greenmonth and there were eight words, and nobody in this kingdom has ever put a word on the ninth one, and about forty people heard it in a market in Greenmonth on a Saturday with a load of dressed stone standing in the street, and about nine of them heard it properly and about thirty-one were inside about sixty feet of a nine-hundredweight load with a corner chalked on it, and a woman of about nineteen said it to a man of about thirty who wrote it on the back of his own hand and put a bar of soap over it in the evening and nothing over it in the morning.**

A mason of about sixty-eight was in the room. He had come because he was told there were nine men in a yard anywhere near here who wanted a top course done, and there were not nine men, and he had been in the room about two hours and had not been asked to do anything and had not offered and had not gone. **And it was the first room he had stood inside in about eleven years, and he had not said so, and nobody asked, and he came anyway, and a man of about thirty-two with a limp noticed it and did not say anything about it then or afterwards.**

## Two

The column was written at about the fifth hour and it took him about four minutes and it has four lines and four heads and he wrote the four heads and then he stopped for about a minute and a half and then he wrote them.

**What the thing is. Where it is. How often it happens. And a fourth head, which is who holds it.**

**And the fourth head is the only one of the four that is a question, and he wrote it last, and everybody in that room can see that he wrote it last, and nobody said anything about it.**

He filled the first head against the four lines. **Anybody may come, at a yard of four lime kilns, at a draw, about nine times a year.** **Read it yourself, at an arch over a beck, whenever there is a stone to be read.** **Never a person twice, in a fold, all year round, and once a year on a boundary.** **Feed the stranger first, at a roadhouse, about nine times a week.**

Then he came to the fourth head and the room got about as quiet as a room over a cooper's shop gets on a market Saturday, which is not quiet at all and is a different thing.

**And he wrote four names in it. He wrote the woman of about forty-four who runs the second kiln, and he wrote the mason of about sixty-eight, and he wrote the man of about forty-one in the fold, and he wrote the woman of about fifty at the roadhouse, and there was a man of about twenty-seven in that room who watched him do it and did not stop him and did not say anything and has not said anything about it since.**

A woman of about thirty-nine in that room, who keeps a stall of hinges and who has a daughter of about eleven, read the two columns off the board from about nine feet off, in the ordinary way, and read them as two lists, and said that the first list was a list of things and the second list was a list of people, and that she would not know what to do with either.

**And a man of about sixty-eight who had been in that room for two hours and who had said nothing until then said the whole of what he had to say about it in nine words, and the nine words are the reason nobody in that room improved on it and the reason nothing about it is in a book or in a room two hundred miles off.**

“**It stops being a thing and becomes a man.**”

## Three

**And nobody agreed with him and nobody improved on it and the room went on, and that is the whole of what happened about it, and a man of about thirty-two with a limp stood nine feet off a bench-board with a stick he could not lean on and did not say one word in about nine minutes.**

He had been wrong about this twice in about forty-one days and both times he had been wrong in the same direction, and the first time was a sheet of eight words into eight places and the second was nine words said in a market where a load was being settled, and both of the times the thing he had got in his hand had turned into a person about four minutes after he picked it up, and about forty people had heard a road's name and about nine of them had heard it properly and one man of about thirty had it on the back of his own hand under a bar of soap and nothing over it in the morning.

**Nobody improved on that either and the woman of about thirty-nine who had a daughter of about eleven said, at about the seventh hour, that a list of things is worth having and that she would like one in her own house, and that she had asked for one twice and had been given a form, and that a form has a head on it that says the name of the person who is to do it, and that this column has four heads and one of the heads is the name of the person, and that this is the first one anybody has handed her that has got somebody's name in it in the wrong place.**

And she said that in the ordinary way, and she was not talking about the column, and nobody in that room knew she was talking about the column, and he did not answer her, and nobody asked her twice.

## Four

**And then he did the only thing he did all day, and it is printed here because it is the decision and not because it is a good one.**

He wiped the four names out with the flat of his hand and then wiped the dust off the board after them, which took longer. **He did not wipe the four heads out. He left four lines with about four feet of chalk on them and four heads, and under the fourth head there was about nine inches of clean board, and he said that he was not going to be the person who decides which of four things is the fourth, and that a column with a head on it and nothing under it is a column that anybody can put a name in later, and that this is the worst of the four possibilities and he chose it in about four seconds in a room over a cooper's shop because the other three were worse.**

Then the four went back to the four people who hold them, in the order they were sung, and the ninth line stayed ruled and empty and about nine feet long, and the mason of about sixty-eight went out at about the ninth hour without a day's work and without a sheet of paper, and about nine hundred people went out of a street four hundred yards long, and the room was let by the day and the door is not locked and the board is not a form and has no head and a person who takes that room next will see a list of four things in chalk on a bench-board and will not know what it is and will not have been told.

## Five

The cost of the day is a column and the biggest single item in it is a cart that went about thirty miles and came about thirty miles to bring a man who did not work, and that is the whole of what the day cost.

**A cart at four shillings a day, which is forty-eight pence, a room at six pence, a sheet of paper at one penny, and a loaf at one penny: forty-eight, six, one, one is fifty-six pence.** A mark is forty-eight pence and fifty-six pence is one mark and eight pence, and the cart was out of a yard about nine miles off at about the second hour and back at about the ninth hour, and a mason of about sixty-eight was in it both ways, and about four shillings and sixpence a day is the rate he named in a field beside that beck, on the fifth of a month in Greenmonth, about seventy-three days ago, and he is a man who is about forty miles off that arch, and he did not do a day's work and the cart cost four times what his day would have cost and the day is not the point.

**And four words and nine figures and a clean space where a fourth head had been are on a board in a room that is let by the day, and about nine of them are about a road, and a man of about thirty with a bar of soap has nine of them on the back of his own hand in a market town of about two thousand people on the salt and nothing over it, and about nine people in nine counties have a name they can be asked about, and a road is not a place and has nobody in it and cannot be entered in a column at all, and that is the only reason the ninth line is still empty, and it is still empty on a Saturday in Rainmonth fifty-six days after about forty people heard it in a market about two hundred miles from a room with a door on a lane.**
