# Chapter 0403

**The fifth day of Greenmonth, YR 318, a Wednesday and not a market day, and the fourth hour and the seventh hour and the ninth hour, at an arch over a beck nine miles off the kilns on a road that is not on any sheet and is not named on any map, about forty years old and standing, where the beck is about eleven inches over the sill and the arch ring is about nine feet above the water and a man can get under it if he does it the way a man who has done it before does it.**

## One

A single arch, one span, no parapet on the arch itself, and about nine feet of approach on either side with a top course of dressed stone on the approach and nothing on the arch, which is right, because a stone on the top of a curve is a stone that is there to be looked at and a man looks at it.

**The road it carries is nine miles long and it is called Coldharbour, and the harbour is nine miles further on and is a field, and the field has been a field for about forty years, and the road has been the road to a harbour for about four hundred.**

The beck is eleven inches over the sill at that state of the water and the sill is mossy on the upstream edge and the arch ring is nine feet above the water, which means a man standing on the sill has about seven feet and four inches of head and a man of about thirty-two with a limp has to come down onto both knees first, which takes a man about four seconds longer than a man who can stand.

**He had got up off a bank on the seventh day of a month in Longlight and had not got up off one since, and getting up is about four seconds longer than sitting down and he knows the number to a point where the number has stopped being about a bank.**

A mason of about sixty-eight was under there already with a lamp hooked to his own belt. **His name is Saber Vahl and there is nobody else in nine counties who could tell you whether it is the same man, because he has not been in a room in about eleven years.**

“**Do not put a hand on the ring,**” the mason said. “**Not because it will come down. Because it has been dry forty years and you will take the skin off it and in nine years there will be a mark on that arch where you put your hand and it will not be the mason’s mark.**”

## Two

**The mark is on the underside of the ninth voussoir from the left.** The ninth is not special. A man laying up an arch ring counts, and the ninth from the left on this arch is the one at about the third of the rise, and the reason there is a mark on it at all is that a mason whose business it was cut his mark into the bed of every stone he dressed, on the face that goes into the mortar, because that is the face nobody sees and that is exactly why it is the face a mason uses.

**A man counts the stones of an arch from the ground, in the dark, off a lamp, and he does not count them by eye. He counts them off a hand.** A voussoir at the third of the rise is about two feet wide and a hand is about nine inches and the joint above it is about a finger thick, so two hands and a finger take a man from the springing to the crown in about eleven movements and about thirty seconds, and a mason of about twenty-eight would do it in about nine seconds, and a mason of about sixty-eight with a bad knee and a lamp hooked to his belt took about four minutes and was right.

**And a mason does not count the same way twice, because the ring is not a wall and the stones do not line up, and the mason who laid it counted off his own hand in his own order and the mason who reads it forty years later is counting somebody else's hand, and there are about four ways of counting a ring and only one of them gives the ninth stone, and the way you know which one is the way the ring is wider at the haunch than at the crown, which is the one thing about an arch that everybody who has ever built one knows and that nobody who has only looked at one does.**

**A mason's mark is a small figure with a stroke through it. It is not a letter and it is not a number and it cannot be read by anybody who was not taught it, and a mason who is dead is a mark that is a scratch, and a mason who is alive is a man who looks at a scratch and knows.**

The mason of about sixty-eight had a nail in his hand and not a chisel and he worked the point of it in the mortar of the bed joint and scraped out about an inch of it, which took him the better part of a minute, and then he put the lamp close.

“**That is a stag,**” he said. “**Two strokes and a cross, that is a stag, and I have not seen a stag on a dressed stone in about thirty years.**”

“**Whose.**”

“**Mine,**” he said. “**The year it went up. Forty years this next Longlight.**”

“**You are sixty-eight.**”

“**I am sixty-eight and I cut that when I was twenty-eight and there were four masons on it and there is one mark left that anybody alive can hold to and I have never once seen a man in this county who knew it and I have never once said so out loud.**”

He said that last part to the arch and not to anybody, and then he said the other thing.

“**There is a clerk in a town about forty miles off,**” he said, “**and he came out here twice. Once in the year I laid a floor at his mother's house and once the year after for no reason that he gave. He asked me whether I had put anything on the stones. And I said no.**”

“**Why did you say no.**”

“**Because he wrote it down,**” the mason said. “**And then I have not said no, and he has not come back, and a man who says no twice is a man who has made a decision and a man who has made a decision about a stone is a man somebody will ask about again.**”

## Three

There was work at that arch and the work was not his. **About nine feet of the top course on the northern approach is out of level, and out of level in a dressed top course on an arch approach is not a thing that gets worse and it is not a thing that gets better, and a mason who leaves it does so because he cannot be got, and the reason he cannot be got is eleven years.** The mason of about sixty-eight was the nearest working mason and he was about forty miles off, and the reason he came was not the top course.

“**I came to look at my mark,**” he said, when he was asked. “**It gets a whole lot of people in about ten years, in a wet one, and I have not been under it since the year it went up and I am sixty-eight and there is not a second time.**”

The work wanted doing and he would not do it. **He said the top course wanted two men and four days and a new stone and a barrow, and that the stone in it is a good stone and a man who has not seen it in nine years does not put his mark on it, and that the man who should do it is about forty miles off and will do it for the rate and the rate is four shillings and sixpence a day for a man who can read a dressed face, and it is not my trade, my trade is arches, and a mason who will not say that a top course is his trade is a man who has been out of work eleven years.**

## Four

**What he wanted was said once, standing in a field with a beck in it, to a stranger, and he did not improve on it.**

“**There was a job in Longlight about eleven years ago,**” he said. “**A new arch over a drain at a house in Cask, and I was asked, and I was not taken, and I did not argue and I did not go, and I have not had a day’s work since, and there is a difference between not going and not having a day, and it is the whole of what these eleven years have been.**”

“**Why were you not taken.**”

“**I was drunk,**” he said, “**and the man who put me on that job is dead and the man who did not take me is dead, and the county knows it, and nobody has ever said it to me, and I am not asking you to say it to me.**”

He looked at the lamp for a while.

“**So I am after a day’s work,**” he said, “**and I am not after anything else, and if you have nine men in a yard anywhere near here who want a top course done I will do it at four shillings and sixpence a day and I will not want the stone.**”

Then he said the thing the whole of the next six days turned on, and he said it flatly, and he had clearly been carrying it since the second hour.

“**I have been saying that name to anybody who stops at that arch for forty years,**” he said. “**Not the mark. The road. Somebody always asks what the road is called, because it is called nothing on the sign, and I say it, because it is a road and not a person.**”

“**Is it a problem to say a road’s name.**”

“**It is nothing,**” the mason said, “**and I will tell you why it is nothing, and then I will tell you the one thing about it that is not nothing, and the one thing is worse.**”

“**A road has got nobody in it,**” he said. “**You cannot ask after a road. You cannot send a form to a road. I have been saying that name to about nine men in forty years and I do not know one of their names and not one of them knows what I am, and there is not one piece of paper in this kingdom with Coldharbour in it except a man’s mouth.**”

## Five

He said the name in the field. **He said it twice, once to be sure of it, and about nine feet of a field and a beck and a lamp, and it is the only time in about forty years that that name has been said with nobody in earshot to be asked about having said it.**

Then he got himself out from under the arch, which took a man of about sixty-eight about eleven seconds and took the man of thirty-two with a limp considerably longer, and stood on the sill in the mud, and did not ask for help and was not offered it.

The cost of the two days is a column and there are two things in it and neither of them is a wage.

**A cart at four shillings a day for two days is eight shillings, which is ninety-six pence. The mason at four shillings and sixpence a day for two days is nine shillings, which is a hundred and eight pence. Ninety-six and a hundred and eight is two hundred and four pence, and a mark is forty-eight pence, so two days of a cart and a mason came to two hundred and four pence, and the mason had the hundred and eight of it in his own hand on the second afternoon at the arch, and the man of thirty-two with a limp had the ninety-six of it in his own hand at the cart on the second morning, and neither of the two of them said anything to the other about a word.**

**And about nine men have heard the name of a road said out loud in a field in about forty years, and the mason does not know one of them, and there is no sheet, and there is no column, and the man who wrote eight words on the back of a sheet in a back room four days ago does not know that any of this has happened.**
