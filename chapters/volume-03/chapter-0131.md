# Chapter 0131

The water in the ditch above the seed room had been four inches deep for nine days, and on the fourth morning of the fourth week of the fifth rolling it was still four inches deep, and Coll Ferrow called a bench-day.

The guild held its bench on the fourth week of a rolling and the council held its on the second, and the two bodies had never once sat on the same day in nineteen years, and both of them had arranged that by accident nineteen years ago and neither of them had ever seen fit to change it. So the guild's bench-day fell in the week the oath-stone was sworn in the field, and the whole county went to the field in the morning, and then eleven of them came down the hill to the guild's long room over the tool store, because there was a matter.

The room had elm on both walls hung on pegs in the order it was wanted and not the order it was bought, a bench of nine along the north side, and a slate on the table that had been in the county thirty years and had never once been asked to leave the room it was in. Ferrow had the book open on the table before anybody came in, and it was not nine months old.

“That,” he said, before anybody had sat down, “is the twelfth rolling. Written before I met any of you. It has been in that press since the spring and I have read it twice this week and I have not altered one line of it, and the reason I have not altered a line of it is that I wrote it in the twelfth rolling and it was wrong in the twelfth rolling, and a man who corrects his own plan before anybody has read it is a man who wants to be agreed with.”

Nineteen men, or most of them. It was the fourth week and four of the guild’s nineteen were away at a landlord’s ditch two days off and the room was fifteen full and the room knew what it was being told.

Aren stood at the end, where a stranger stands, and he had asked for no place and got none, and the slate woman was at the wall with her back to the elm.

Ferrow turned the book round.

“Four hundred and forty yards of ditch from the top sluice at the weir to the trough above the seed room. The whole length lies eleven yards off the trough the oath was sworn at. Eleven yards, measured by me and by the bound and by two men with a rope, and the rope was on the ninth and the water was not on the ninth and I do not care what the water was on the ninth. The water is the only witness in this county that has never once come to a field and given its word, and a thing that has no witness is a thing I do not put in a book with my name at the foot of.”

He put his finger on a line and did not read from it, because he had read it twice this week.

“Take the ditch up and put it in the trough. Three days, sixteen men, the guild’s rate, and we are not doing a fortnight of talk about it first. A north crew would want a survey and a season and a man from the county town to agree with the survey. And the reason is not stupidity. They want a drawing because a drawing is a claim, and a claim can be looked at again, and a boundary in this county is water, and there is no honest drawing of water — a drawing of water is a picture of something that has already gone, and it is going to go again before the ink is dry. And then in nine years a man comes along with the drawing, and the drawing is right and the man is wrong, and the drawing wins, because it is paper and he is a person. That is what your drawing is for, all of it.”

“Then how do you keep a record of a day’s work on a boundary,” said the bound, who was fifty-one and had held the same two miles since he was nineteen.

“By asking the man who held it, who is standing here, and by his telling the next one, and by the stone,” said Ferrow. “That is the whole method. It is a bad method. It is the only one in this county that has ever worked and I am not going to be the man who improves on it.”

Nobody laughed. It is not a thing anybody in that room found funny.

Then he came to the four lines he had written on the first morning of the rolling and had not spoken to anybody.

“And here is the thing I have not said out loud. The whole length is off the trough because the boundary is a promise and the promise has been kept a hundred and forty years and the keeping has moved the ditch eleven yards. That is not the stranger’s finding. I put that on the stone page myself nineteen years ago, and the council will not have the page lifted, and I am not asking them to lift it, and I am going to say the rest of it out loud in a room because the rest of it is the work.

“The promise is the boundary. The boundary is a channel, the channel is a ditch, a ditch has a head, and the head of that ditch has not been at the trough since before my father was born. Here is all of it and it is a short thing.

“The trough is set. A man sets a trough in the first week of a rolling and digs it to a level, and a hole does not walk, so it has been at that level for a hundred years. And the oath is sworn at the trough, and it has been sworn at the trough for a hundred and forty years, because a trough is the one place on this boundary where a man can stand and look at the water and know where the line is. And the ditch, which is the boundary, has gone downstream a hand’s width a year for a hundred and forty years, because the top of a bank cut the same depth in the same line since before the stone was set comes down a hand’s width every winter, and the man who renews fills it in on the side it came down on, and then swears to the place it has got to. So the boundary has gone and the trough has not. They are eleven yards apart. They have been for longer than any living man, and the oath has been sworn eleven yards from the truth every year since the first one, and nobody has ever once looked down.

“And here is what that costs. A ditch whose head is not at the trough does not take the trough’s water, and it has not taken it for a hundred and forty years. So the water comes over the boundary at the trough and has nowhere to go along the boundary at all, and it stands against the low side of the bank, and a pond against a bank on a mile of boundary is a water table a mile wide, and a water table a mile wide under four villages is the salt coming up out of ground that always had it in it. That is the white. There is no curse in it and no poison in it and no man in it. It is eleven yards.”

He put his hand flat on the book, and the room had seen the stranger do that eleven days ago over a sheet of paper, and Ferrow had looked at him then and not spoken.

“So the ditch is eleven yards off the trough because the stone says so, and the water is coming up out of four villages because the stone says so, and both of those are true at once and they have been true at once for a hundred and forty years, and there is no meeting in this county that is going to find a third thing to blame, because there is not one.” He looked round the room. “I am going to put the ditch in the trough. That is my work. What the trough does to the stone afterwards is not my work, and it is not the council’s, and it is not a thing I am going to pretend I can answer for, and I will not be the man who comes back in nine years and says I did not think of that, because I have thought of it and I have thought of it for nineteen years.”

He stopped there, which for Ferrow was a long speech, and the room let a good deal of the room’s own time go past before anybody spoke.

Munn spoke, from the far end, and she had come down from the field and still had the dust of it on the hem of her coat.

“Then say the rest of it, Coll. If the ditch goes into the trough, then the boundary is at the trough, and it has not been at the trough in a hundred and forty years, and the stone is not a page in a book. It is a field with nine men standing in it in four weeks, and every one of those men has sworn at that trough before, and every one of them will swear again, and not one of them will know that this is the first time in a hundred and forty years the words they are saying have been true. What is that field going to be.”

“They will find the water where they swore to it,” said Ferrow, “because the water is at the trough. That is what a trough is, and the water has been at the trough for a hundred and forty years, and it is the only fixed point on that boundary, and it is the one place your stone has always been right about. I am not taking your stone anywhere. I am putting eleven yards of water back under it.

“And the field in four weeks will be a field in which nine men say true words for the first time in a hundred and forty years, and they will not know that, and you will know that, and you will have to stand in that field and not say it. That is my work. I have had nineteen years to think about the rest of it and it is not a thing a first hand of a guild is entitled to do to a bench, and I am not going to do it, and I would thank you for not asking me to.”

Nobody answered, because there was not an answer, and the room sat with it the way a room sits with weather.

Then the bound said, “It is going to need a witness at the trough. When the water is at the trough, a man has to be able to say he saw the water at the trough, and not that he was told the water was at the trough.”

“He will have the water in front of him,” said Ferrow. “That is the witness. That is the only witness that has ever been worth anything in this county and it costs nothing and it cannot be forged and it cannot be copied, and it is the reason this county has never once had a forgery in a boundary, and the reason you have never once heard of a boundary in this county that came out of a room.”

Aren had been standing at the end through all of it with his hands behind his back and his mouth shut, and he had a thing in him that was hard to hold, and he held it. Thessa’s nine lines were on the council’s table in the market town and her leaf with the bank was under them, and eleven yards was a fact he had known for five rollings, and the man at the end of the table had said it in the first ten minutes of a council sitting in a field before a person with eleven years of numbers had said it, and the man at the end of the table was right about that, and had been right about it longer.

Ferrow got the slate turned round and put the sum where the room could see it, and made the bound read it out, because the bound had the best hand of the nine and the guild did not trust a stranger with figures or a stranger with his own.

“Sixteen men,” the bound read. “Eight pence the day, that is the guild’s rate and it is the rate the council has. That is a hundred and twenty-eight pence the day. Three days: a hundred and twenty-eight, and a hundred and twenty-eight again, and a hundred and twenty-eight again, which is three hundred and eighty-four pence. Iron for the new bottoms and elm off our own rack, at what it would fetch if we sold it, which is ninety-six pence. Three hundred and eighty-four and ninety-six is four hundred and eighty pence.”

“Ten marks,” said the bound.

“Ten marks,” said Ferrow. “And the county has not got ten marks, and the council has never got ten marks, and I have never once in nineteen years got ten marks out of that council and I have stopped expecting to. The guild will take it in salt, at the county’s own figure of nine pence the bushel. Four hundred and eighty at nine the bushel is fifty-three bushels and three pence, and I want the three pence written down, because the three pence is ours and it will be the three pence that is argued about in four years, and a man who has had his three pence argued about once does not enjoy it twice.”

The bound wrote it. The slate woman had the whole thing off the slate in her own book in under a minute, which she did not do for him but did for the book, and he let her, and she let him see that she was letting him.

Then the man at the drawknife said the thing he had come for, and he said it to the room and not to Aren, and it was four words.

“Ask him the question.”

Ferrow said, “He does not get a question. He does not get a name on the book, he does not get a vote, and he does not get asked what he swore, and that is the same as the last three rollings and there is no reason to change it in the fourth week. He holds a line at seven and he is paid the guild’s rate, and all his standing in this county is that nobody has ever asked him anything, and he has been here five rollings and not one of them has asked him anything, and you are not going to start at a bench-day with the oath sworn two days ago.”

“He has not been asked what he swore because nobody thought of it,” said the man at the drawknife. “I have not been asked because I have never told anybody, and I have been in this county five rollings, and I will tell you now that I swore nothing. Nobody asked me. I am not on your roll and I am not on your oath, and I found that out about four weeks after I got here, and I have not been sick about it once. And I am saying it in this room because that man has not been asked either, and I have watched him work out every day this week what it costs to be a stranger in a county that keeps everything it is given, and he has worked it out, and there is no use in it to anybody. You could all be right. I would rather it was asked than not asked. That is all of it.”

He sat down. The room did not take it up. Ferrow looked at Aren and Aren looked at the elm pegs and neither of them said anything at all.

Then the minute-man, who had come down from the field with the council’s book under his arm because he was a man who carries the book to anything, said what he had come down for, and he said it in the voice he used for facts.

“Four hundred and eighty pence, of which four hundred and seventy-seven is salt at nine pence the bushel, and fifty-three bushels and three pence. The guild’s rate and the county’s salt. That is in the minute as offered and as the guild’s terms. I want one thing entered beside it and I want the room to hear me ask it.”

“Ask it.”

“The county has not got ten marks. The county has got salt, and the salt comes off the ground the four white villages are standing on, and the four white villages are the four villages the guild is going to keep standing for another four years. If the council takes the guild’s plan, is the council asking the sixty for salt.”

Nobody answered him for long enough that the room had plainly decided it was not going to, which is how he knew it was the right question, and then Aren answered it, because he was the one person in that room who could.

Aren looked at the slate and said, “The plan does not fall on the four villages. I have read all of it and there is nothing in it that goes into the lowest ground. The salt is the council’s scrape, out of the ten villages, and it comes off the roll, and it is not off the four villages more than any other village, and the amount is small, and it is not asked for this year and it will not be asked for this year.”

“I have the book here,” said the minute-man, “and I will enter that. And I will enter the sum, because a sum that is not entered is a sum that gets said to be a different sum in four years. And I will enter that the sixty are not on the roll for salt and have not been asked for salt and are not being asked for salt, with the hour, and I will read it back to the room before I shut it.”

He did read it back, and the room listened, and the minute-man shut the book, and Ferrow put his hand on the book with the plan in it and left it flat on the table where anybody could come and read it for as long as they liked, which was a thing he had never done with a book in nineteen years.

The stranger from four hundred miles off was the last man out of the guild room, and the man at the drawknife was waiting for him in the lane with a whetstone in his hand, and the rest of the afternoon went by with a stone going round an edge and nobody saying anything at all.

“A guild day,” the man said at last. “Bench-day. Nineteen years and I have never been inside that room, and I would not have understood two words of it. And I will ask you now, because you are the only man in this county who has not been asked, and it is a poor thing to be let off. What did you swear.”

Aren looked down the lane at the market town and the white fields and the ditch with four inches in it, and he had five rollings of the answer ready and every one of the five was wrong in a different direction.

“I swore to a bridge guild in a fen county,” he said, “in a hall, with a bench of nine, and I have not voted at it in four years and I hold the crossing’s crossing and not the crossing, and what I swore is one line long and it is not a promise to anybody’s water, and I would rather you did not write it down and I would rather you did not tell them I gave it to you.”

“That is not an answer about an oath,” said the man at the drawknife. “That is an answer about a guild, and it is the truth, and I have not written it down, and I will not, and I have told the room I swore nothing and I will go on telling rooms I swore nothing until somebody asks me a different question.”

He put his thumb on the whetstone and looked at the ditch and then at Aren, and then he said the thing he had come out of the lane to say, and it was four words long and it was the distance.

That night Aren walked down to the market town and up to the chamber over the tithe barn, and the council was not sitting, and the minute-man let him in the way he let everybody in, which was without ceremony, and the room under the chamber was cold and the roll chest was against the wall with the string through its eyes.

He asked the minute-man one question and got a straight answer inside a minute, which was the fastest the county had ever given him anything.

“If the council takes the guild’s plan, does the plan carry the county’s seal, or the guild’s?”

“The guild’s,” said the minute-man. “The plan is the guild’s. A council can accept a guild’s plan and the work is the guild’s work and the guild’s men are in it and the guild’s rate is the rate, and the minute will say the council took the plan and the guild did the work at the guild’s terms. It will not say the council ordered the work, because the council did not order it and cannot, and if the minute said the council ordered it then in nine years there would be a book in this county with a council in it that had ordered four hundred and forty yards of ditch, and that book would be cited by somebody, and I have kept this book twenty-two years and I am not going to be the man who hands a council an order it did not give.”

“That is the right answer and I did not expect it in under a minute.”

“I have had twenty-two years,” said the minute-man, “and nothing has ever come up those stairs that wanted an answer in under a minute, and I do not intend to start handing them over now that somebody has begun bringing them.”

He shut the room and went up the outside stair, and Aren sat under it with the roll chest against the wall and the plan four hundred yards away in the guild's book, and took out the notebook and did the two things he had come to do with it.

The first was a figure and nothing else. Four hundred and eighty pence, ten marks, of which four hundred and seventy-seven is fifty-three bushels of salt at nine pence and three pence is a remainder that belongs to the guild. The hour. The reason in one line, because a figure in a book without a reason is a claim and he had learned that in a room over a weigh-house four hundred miles off: *and the salt this year is off the ten villages and not off the four, and the year it is off the four will be a year this county cannot argue about, and there will be nothing on paper then to show anybody why it was not, and I am not going to be the man who says he did not think of that either.*

The second line was not a figure at all. He shut the notebook on it without writing it, because it was a thing about people and his book had a column for water and none for that, and he had noticed that about himself in the fourth week of the fifth rolling and did not improve on it.

The standing water in that ditch would want a man in it with a pole, at the guild's rate, on the guild's account, and a northern man with a pole was the cheapest thing in the county, and it would last until the water was gone.

And the plan was in a book in the guild room with the wrong line in it, and nineteen men in the county knew exactly how it was wrong, and not one of them had said so to the council and not one of them had said so to him, and the reason was not manners. The reason was that in this county a thing known out loud is a thing somebody is asking you to do something about, and a man asked to do something about the water in this county has been made an office, and there is no office in the Marches and there is not going to be one made out of him in the ninth week of a rolling by nineteen men with a plan and a book.
