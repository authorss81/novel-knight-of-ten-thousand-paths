# Chapter 0144

The guild's book has four lines in it about four men and none of them is the name of anybody, and that has been true since the fourth week of the seventh rolling, and Ferrow looked at the page for a long time in the last week of the twelfth rolling and then went and found the man of fifty-two in the gateway behind the tithe barn, where he was cutting a peg out of a scrap of a fence rail with the point of a chisel, because a man in this county does not carry a knife out of his own yard.

“Come and look at this,” he said, and they went back into the guild room together, and Ferrow put the book on the bench and turned it round so the page faced the other man, which is the way a first hand does a thing and not the way a keeper does a thing.

It reads:

*Four men of a yard, from the fourth week of the seventh rolling, on the council's account, at the guild's rate, with the asking beside it. Entered by me and not asked for twice. Not on our oath and not on the council's roll, and not to be asked what they swore. T. Kest.*

That is all of it. Nineteen words and a name at the end, and the name is the only thing in that book in nineteen years that belongs to somebody outside this county.

“The county has had your distance since the seventh rolling,” said Ferrow. “Four hundred miles, in the council's minute, on a cost board, in my hand, because a cost board wants a county the way a letter wants a town. Your county has never had your name. I have wanted to put it in nine times and I have not, and the reason I have not is that you said in the guild room in the eighth rolling that you were not going to be a thing in this county that somebody found out from a book, and I agreed to it, and I have kept to it for four rollings by leaving the most useful half of it out.”

“That is all of it,” said the man of fifty-two, “and I want to say the other half of it before you put it in.”

“Say it.”

“The county wrote four hundred miles on a board because a cost board wants a county,” he said. “I have never written it and I have never said it, and I have been asked twice, once by a man with a slate and once by a woman at a sluice who wanted to know whether we were kin, and I gave them the same answer both times, which was that we are a yard and a yard is where we are, and both of them took it for manners.”

He put his hand on the page.

“Put it in,” he said. “The yard. The street. The name of the man whose it is. I have been in this county since the fourth week of your seventh rolling and there are nineteen men in your guild and not one of them knows where we are from, and none of them has asked, and that is not a courtesy. They have not asked because we did not say, and we have not said because we did not know what it would be for.”

“It would be for this,” said Ferrow, “and I am going to do it in this book and not in any other, and a guild's book is a trade and a trade cannot be made to answer for anybody, and a bench can, and that is the only reason I am doing it and it took me four rollings to work out that the safer book is the one I have the most control of.”

It took him about a quarter of an hour to get it right, and he got it wrong twice, and it went into the book exactly as he gave it.

“The yard is the yard of Tovan Kest. Weir Street. In a fen town four hundred miles off, on the coast road where the road is a causeway, and the town is called Merefen and nobody here has ever been to it and I am not going to describe the town because I have not got time and because you would want it more than I would give it.”

“The name of the man whose it is, then.”

“Spelled with a v.”

Ferrow wrote it. He spelled it wrong the first time and the man of fifty-two stopped him in about a second and a half, and did not say that it was wrong, only said the letter again, and Ferrow took it out and put it in, which is the exchange, entire, and neither of them made anything of it.

“Nineteen years I have kept this book,” said Ferrow. “I have had it open in rooms in front of more people than there are men in this county. And there is now a name in it that is not a man of this county's, and it is the only one, and I have wanted it since the fourth week of the seventh rolling, and I have not written it because I did not know what it was for, and I have just found out what it was for.”

“What is it for.”

“It is for the day you go,” said Ferrow. “On the day you go there will be a moment, and it will not be a speech, and it will be that there are four men in this county who have been paid nothing and a book that says so, and one of you will be asked by somebody in nine years where those four men went, and the answer will be in a trade book in a guild room in a county that is not on any map, and nobody will be able to make that book answer, and that is the best thing this county has to give anybody.”

The man of fifty-two looked at the page for a while.

“Then put the street in the book as well,” he said, “and not just the name, because a name is a man and a street is a place, and a place is a thing a person can be shown and a man is not, and if anybody ever comes looking they will find the street and not the man.”

Ferrow put the street in.

Then they went out into the gateway, and the man of fifty-two said the other thing, and he said it to about nine of the guild's men who were all within earshot and to nobody in particular, in the flat voice of a man saying a price, and the drawknife man was at the rail and heard it and the man of sixty who keeps eels heard it and nobody else in that county heard it.

“We are from a fen county four hundred miles off, on a coast road, and we are the best crew in that county, and there is nobody in this county who can do what we do, and I have said it in a gateway, and I would like it noticed that I have said it in a gateway and not in a room.”

Nobody wrote it down. The minute-man was not there. The slate woman was not there. There is no line about it in the guild's book and no line about it in the council's minute and the only person in the Hearth Marches who could have said that it was true is the man at the drawknife, and he was asked about it once, in a yard, in the fourth week of the twelfth rolling, and said that a crew is a crew and that anybody who says otherwise has not been in a fen in February.

The rest of it is salt.

There is ninety-six bushels of the market town's salt in the guild store in the last week of the twelfth rolling and it came out of the council's account at nine pence the bushel, and the four men of the yard were owed for the last twenty-seven days of a stay that began in the fourth week of the seventh rolling, and the county could not pay them all of it and did not pretend to be able to, and the part it could pay it paid in the only currency it has ever been paid in, which is salt and seed out of a market town that is nine miles from a river it cannot use.

Ferrow put it on the board in the guild room in front of nineteen men and the four of them, and he did it the way he does everything, which is to read the addition out and then say the total, and not the other way round.

“Four men at the guild's rate of eight pence. Eight pence apiece is thirty-two pence between them, and thirty-two pence is a day.

“Nine days is nine times thirty-two. Thirty-two, and thirty-two, and thirty-two, and thirty-two, and thirty-two, and thirty-two, and thirty-two, and thirty-two, and thirty-two. That is two hundred and eighty-eight pence.

“Nine more days is two hundred and eighty-eight, and two hundred and eighty-eight again. Six hundred and fifty-six.

“Nine more days. Six hundred and fifty-six, and two hundred and eighty-eight, and that is eight hundred and sixty-four pence.”

The bound said, “That is eighteen marks.”

“That is eighteen marks exactly,” said Ferrow, “and it lands clean because twenty-seven divides into a hundred and sixty-four without a remainder and that is the only arithmetic luck this county has had in a year, and I am not going to pretend it was arranged.”

“And the county has no eighteen marks,” said the man of fifty-two, “and I have known that since the seventh rolling and so have all four of us, so say the rest of it.”

“The rest of it is salt,” said Ferrow. “Ninety-six bushels. Nine pence the bushel, and nine pence ninety-six times over. Nine, and nine, and nine, and nine, and nine, and nine, and nine, and nine, and nine, and nine, and nine, and nine, and nine, and nine, and nine, and nine, and nine, and nine, and nine, and nine, and nine, and nine, and nine, and nine, and nine, and nine, and nine, and nine, and nine, and nine, and nine, and nine, and nine, and nine, and nine, and nine, and nine, and nine, and nine, and nine, and nine, and nine, and nine, and nine, and nine, and nine, and nine, and nine, and nine, and nine, and nine, and nine, and nine, and nine, and nine, and nine, and nine, and nine, and nine, and nine, and nine, and nine, and nine, and nine, and nine, and nine, and nine, and nine, and nine, and nine, and nine, and nine, and nine, and nine, and nine, and nine, and nine, and nine, and nine, and nine, and nine, and nine, and nine, and nine, and nine, and nine, and nine, and nine, and nine, and nine, and nine, and nine, and nine, and nine, and nine, and nine, and nine, and nine, and nine, and nine, and nine, and nine, and nine. That is eight hundred and sixty-four pence, and it is the same figure, and it is not a coincidence, because the county's rate is the county's rate and the county's salt is the county's salt and this is the only sum in this account that has come out the same way twice.”

He put the chalk down.

“Now the other half, and it goes in this book and in the council's book and in neither of them under anybody's name. The last twenty-seven days is eight hundred and sixty-four pence and it is paid, in salt, in full, and the salt is in the store and you will take it and I will not count it out in front of these nineteen because a man who is handed his money in front of a room is a man who has been given a figure, and you have had four rollings of a figure and I am not going to be the one that gives you another.

“The rest of you is at none. The rest of you is from the fourth week of the seventh rolling to the fifth day of the ninth of the twelfth, and it is four men and none, and none is in two books in this county and it is the only line in either of them with nothing in a money column, and I have said for four rollings that in nine years somebody is going to take those two books and put them side by side and say this county owes four men it cannot name.

“It can name you now,” said Ferrow. “It has a name and a street and a yard and a rate and a date and a count, and it has not got a figure for the rest of you, and I want the room to notice that the name arrived first and the figure did not arrive at all, and that this is the way every debt in this county has gone for nineteen years and the only difference this time is that we have written the name down.”

The drawknife man said, from the rail, in the voice he uses for a landing that will not take, “Then the yard's book will have it too.”

“The yard's book will have what it was given and not the paper,” said the man of fifty-two, before Ferrow could answer, “and that is the yard's rule and it is not a trick and it is not a thing I have said to be let off. Nothing that belongs to a man goes in that book and only a thing set. A contract is a thing set. The salt is a thing set. The name is a thing set. The rest of it is not a thing set, because the rest of it is a figure that a county in a market room agreed it could not pay, and a yard that puts that in a book has admitted a debt it cannot call in, and there are four of us and there is not one of us who is going to call it in, and the book does not need to know that we have decided that, because a book that knows what a man has decided not to do is an office.”

Ferrow looked at him for about four seconds and then wrote one line in the guild's book and it is the shortest line in it.

*And the yard's book holds the fact and not the paper, and the fact is salt, and the paper is in a fen county four hundred miles off, and neither of those two books can be made to answer to the other, and that is the arrangement and not an accident.*

They left in the first week of the thirteenth rolling, four men and a handcart and ninety-six bushels of salt, on a day the council named for them, which is a thing the council does and has never done before and entered as a fact against no person, because naming a day for a departure is the same instrument as naming a day for a breaking and the county has one of those now and it is going to use it.

The last hour of the last day of the twelfth rolling, about a hundred yards below the trough, the man at the drawknife and the man of fifty-two put the last of the elm revetment in and pegged it and did not hammer it, because a peg that is not hammered goes in again in the spring and a peg that is hammered is a peg a man has to cut out with a bar in nine years.

The man of fifty-two looked at the bank for a while with the peg maul in his hand.

“Your guild has been calling this dangerous,” he said, to nobody, in the way a man says a thing at a bank, “and I have been here for five rollings and I have dug more boundary in this county than I have dug anywhere in my life, and I am going to say one thing about that and then I am going to go and pack a cart.”

Nobody asked him for it and he said it anyway, and the only person who heard all of it was the drawknife man, who was three feet away, and the drawknife man has never repeated it to anybody.

“A boundary in this county is a channel,” he said. “I have never in twenty years dug one that was not. And I have dug in four counties and a boundary is a thing a man puts between two pieces of ground, and here it is a thing a man puts between two pieces of water, and that means that when the two sides of it argue, they are not arguing about land, they are arguing about where the ditch goes, and the ditch has been going the same wrong way for a hundred and forty years because the man who fills it in fills it in on the side it came down on, and that is not wickedness, that is a man with a barrow standing on the side he is standing on.

“You have stopped the walking. That is a real thing and nobody in this county could have done it and it took me five rollings to believe that four men from a fen county were in on it, and I believed it on the fourth day and not before. And I am going to take my crew four hundred miles home and they are going to dig somewhere else, and I am not going to be one of the men who tells a story about a bank in a county that is not on any map, and if a man asks me where I was I am going to say I was on a job and I was paid in salt for the last four weeks of it, and both of those are true, and the second one is the half that matters.”

He put the peg maul down in the cart and did not say anything else about it, and the four of them were gone by the second hour of the first day of the thirteenth rolling, and the county's year went on without them, and the ninth men in the guild's book are nineteen again, and there is a name in it that is not a man of this county's, and a street, and a line that says a thing was set and not a man.
