# Chapter 0127

The seed room was behind the chapel at the edge of the market town, stone-walled, low-doored, cool even at noon. The woman who kept it was sixty-six, spare, grey-haired, with soil scored into her knuckles too deep to wash out. She had kept the room forty years and had never asked the council for anything, and the council had never offered her anything, and both sides preferred it that way.

Aren came with Ferrow’s leave and without an escort, because the keeper had sent word through the boy that the stranger might look if he touched nothing. The room smelled of dry grain and old lime. Nine sealed jars stood in a row along one wall, waxed and tied, each marked with a year in chalk. Along the other rose a wall where two blocked doorways showed as seams in the plaster, built up with stone and mortar in two different years.

She watched him take in the jars first, as visitors always did.

“Wheat not grown here in forty years,” she said, before he asked. “Nine jars. Sealed against damp and mice. The last sowing failed in the white ground, and the one before barely headed. I keep them because throwing seed away is a thing a county does once and regrets for a generation.”

He nodded and did not reach out. “And the wall?”

“Two earlier answers,” she said. “The first room was smaller and flooded in the low weeks, so they built it out. The second door faced the wrong wind and let the damp in, so they built it up. Every answer in this county is a wall until it is a doorway again. I have laid both courses myself with my own hands.”

She showed him the ledgers next, three thin books with sowing dates and yields in a plain hand. The yields fell year by year in the four white villages and held elsewhere. She did not interpret them. She laid them down and left him to read what they said.

Ferrow had told him she did not want the cooperative’s thanks, and she told him so herself within ten minutes, in her own voice.

“If your friends in the north mean to thank me in a minute or a letter, tell them not to,” she said. “Thanks in a book becomes a debt in a book, and a debt becomes a claim, and I have kept seed forty years by keeping it out of books that travel. What I have here is for this county when this county needs it. If a cooperative forms, it will form round water and seed and not round me.”

He said he carried no thanks and had no cooperative to promise. She studied him a moment and accepted it.

“What I want,” she said, “is that the seed be in nine jars and not in a wall. Jars can be opened. Walls have to be broken. I have built up two answers and I do not mean to build a third.”

They stayed a while among the jars. He asked how long the nine would keep. She said five years, perhaps six, if the wax held, and after that they were meal. She said it without self-pity, as a person states the measure of a room.

Then she took the two ledgers off the shelf and put them on the floor at his feet, and he understood that she had been waiting since the ledgers came off the shelf to ask him a question she had no right to ask a stranger.

“The second door,” she said. “The one that let the damp in. I laid that course myself and I was thirty-one and I have never been sure of the year. The books say the year. The books say a sowing and a failure and nothing about a door. I want to know whether the stone in that course is the stone of a bad year or a good one, because a man builds a door out of what is to hand, and a man with a bad harvest in his hands builds a different door from a man with a good one, and I have been thirty-five years waiting to be told which man I was.”

Aren looked at the wall. The two blocked doorways showed as seams in the plaster, and the seams were the only honest thing in the room, and behind them were two floors of a seed room that had been wrong twice and had been made right by being stopped up.

He said he could read a great deal off a course of stone and some of it well, and that he could not read a man’s harvest out of it, and that what he could read was the year the stone was quarried and whether the masons had had a good season or a hungry one. She said that was two thirds of her question and that two thirds was what a person got in this county from anybody.

“What do you want read?” he said, and he asked it because he had learned in four hundred miles that a question with no record behind it is a cheap thing to ask and a ruinous one to have answered.

“The floors,” she said. “Not the seed. The floors. There are three feet of wall between the outside of this room and the inside of the older room, and there is stone in it, and in nine years or in a flood there will be a man in the dark with a lantern and no idea where he is standing. A man who knows where the wall is can find the floor. A man who cannot find the floor will take the seed, because the seed is the only thing anybody has ever heard of in here, and a man in the dark who has been told there is wheat under this room will dig for wheat.”

He said that was the first sentence anybody had said to him in this county that he had been told to write down, and that it was not for a book.

“It is for the wall,” she said.

He asked her for leave to mark it, and told her what marking it would cost, which was that the face of the outside course would come away in three places to get at the beds, and there would be new mortar, and a wall that was forty years old would have three scars in it that nobody had asked for.

She said a scar is a thing a wall can have and an answer is a thing a wall cannot have, and that she had been keeping the wall for forty years and had never once been asked to keep it from anybody, and that if the marks were in the county’s own language and put there by a man who could read them then they were not a stranger’s marks on her keeping. They were one mason’s marks on another mason’s wall, and a wall has never once asked whose hand it was. She allowed them on the outside only, and above the line the water had come to last year, and she would keep the measure of it in her own head, because a measure in a book becomes an office and a measure in a head is a woman of sixty-six.

He marked them in the afternoon with a cold chisel off Ferrow’s rack and three bits of tin, and it took until the light went. A bridgewright marks the way a mason marks and for the same reason, which is that a mark has to be findable in bad light by a man who has never seen it and does not know it is there. He lettered the height of the older floor on the outside, and the line the water had come to, and beside it a third mark, low, at the height of the floor inside the third course.

“Why that one?” she said, from the door.

He told her the truth about it, which was that the lowest mark is the one that saves a man, because a man with a lantern in a flooded room is not looking for a floor to stand on. He is looking for the floor above his own head, and there is one, and it is three feet off the ground, and if nobody has told him that in advance he will put his lantern through it.

The keeper held the doorway a long moment with her hand on the frame and the last of the light behind her. Then she said that she had built two answers into that wall with her own hands to keep nine jars dry, and that there were now three marks on it in a mason’s language, put there by a stranger, and that she had not decided whether she had been made a better keeper or a worse one, and that she would decide in about a year, in the same room, with the water down.

He said he was sorry for the wall.

“You be sorry in the morning, when you are holding a line instead of a chisel,” she said, “and do not be sorry in this room, because a person who is sorry in a seed room has come to be forgiven and there is nobody here to forgive anybody.”

On the way out she stopped him at the door. “You stood in the field and said nothing,” she said. “Thessa told me. That was well done. A man who speaks in a field becomes a document. A man who listens remains a person.” She looked at the marks on the outside of her wall, which were above the old flood line and below the new one, and she said the last thing to him in the voice of a person setting a hand on a rail. “Now there is a mark on this room and there is no keeper of it but the two of us, and the water does not read marks and cannot, and the wall is the only thing in this county that does not need a book to be read. I have been wrong about that for forty years and I am not going to argue with you about it tonight.”

Ferrow met him at the staging and asked what he made of the vault. Aren said nine jars of wheat ungrown forty years was a county keeping faith with its own future at the cost of its present hunger, and the keeper’s refusal of thanks was the only honest position in the rolling. Ferrow said she had refused his guild’s offer of labour to enlarge the room, twice, on the ground that a larger room would need a larger keeping, and larger keeping would need an office, and she kept seed precisely to need none. He said that he had put three marks on the outside of her wall that afternoon, with her leave and against his better judgement, and that a person who has put a mark on somebody else’s keeping of forty years does not sleep entirely well, and Ferrow said that was the correct amount of sleep a man would get.

The Water slid past, high and brown, and up under the near corner of the chapel there was a line on the stone that nobody had cut. Ferrow spoke of his nineteen men and the wage refused thrice, and how the vault keeper’s stand resembled his own: to want the same work all year rather than more work once. Aren said the county was full of people keeping narrow ground against wide weather. Ferrow said narrow ground was the only kind anybody ever truly kept, and then he said the marks, and that a mark on a wall was a thing a bridgewright could do and a county could not, and that the county would be doing the reading for four hundred years and would never once think to check the mark was true.
