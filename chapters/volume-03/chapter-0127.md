# Chapter 0127

The seed room stood behind the chapel at the edge of the market town, stone-walled, low-doored, cool even at noon. The woman who kept it was sixty-six, spare, grey-haired, with soil scored into her knuckles too deep to wash out. She had kept the room forty years and had never asked the council for anything, and the council had never offered her anything, and both sides preferred it that way.

Aren came with Ferrow’s leave and without an escort, because the keeper had sent word through the boy that the stranger might look if he touched nothing. The room smelled of dry grain and old lime. Along one wall stood nine sealed jars in a row, waxed and tied, each marked with a year in chalk. Along the other rose a wall where two blocked doorways showed as seams in the plaster, built up with stone and mortar in two different years.

She watched him take in the jars first, as visitors always did.

“Wheat not grown here in forty years,” she said, before he asked. “Nine jars. Sealed against damp and mice. The last sowing failed in the white ground, and the one before barely headed. I keep them because throwing seed away is a thing a county does once and regrets for a generation.”

He nodded and did not reach out. “And the wall?”

“Two earlier answers,” she said. “The first room was smaller and flooded in the low weeks, so they built it out. The second door faced the wrong wind and let the damp in, so they built it up. Every answer in this county is a wall until it is a doorway again. I have laid both courses myself with my own hands.”

She showed him the ledgers next, three thin books with sowing dates and yields in a plain hand. The yields fell year by year in the four white villages and held elsewhere. She did not interpret them. She laid them down and left him to read what they said.

Ferrow had told him she did not want the cooperative’s thanks, and she told him so herself within ten minutes, in her own voice.

“If your friends in the north mean to thank me in a minute or a letter, tell them not to,” she said. “Thanks in a book becomes a debt in a book, and a debt becomes a claim, and I have kept seed forty years by keeping it out of books that travel. What I have here is for this county when this county needs it. If a cooperative forms, it will form round water and seed and not round me.”

He said he carried no thanks and had no cooperative to promise. She studied him a moment and accepted it.

“What I want,” she said, “is that the seed be in nine jars and not in a wall. Jars can be opened. Walls have to be broken. I have built up two answers and I do not mean to build a third.”

They stood a while among the jars. He asked how long the nine would keep. She said five years, perhaps six, if the wax held, and after that they were meal. She said it without self-pity, as a person states the measure of a room.

On the way out she stopped him at the door. “You stood in the field and said nothing,” she said. “Thessa told me. That was well done. A man who speaks in a field becomes a document. A man who listens remains a person.”

He went down the lane thinking of nine jars against thirteen rollings, and of two answers sealed into a wall by the same hands that kept the seed, and of a woman of sixty-six who refused gratitude in advance because gratitude entered in a book would outlive the grain.

Ferrow met him at the staging and asked what he made of the vault. Aren said nine jars of wheat ungrown forty years was a county keeping faith with its own future at the cost of its present hunger, and the keeper’s refusal of thanks was the only honest position in the rolling. Ferrow said she had refused his guild’s offer of labour to enlarge the room, twice, on the ground that a larger room would need a larger keeping, and larger keeping would need an office, and she kept seed precisely to need none.

They stood watching the Water slide past, high and brown. Ferrow spoke of his nineteen men and the wage refused thrice, and how the vault keeper’s stand resembled his own: to want the same work all year rather than more work once. Aren said the county was full of people holding narrow ground against wide weather. Ferrow said narrow ground was the only kind anybody ever truly held.
