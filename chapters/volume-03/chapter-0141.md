# Chapter 0141

The first week of the tenth rolling came in dry, which after the seventh rolling felt like an insult, and the room under the tithe barn had the door open and the boards swept and about forty men in it, and they were not there for the council.

They were there because a thing that has happened to everybody in a county at the same time gathers everybody in one room within a day and a half, and what had happened to everybody in this county in the last three weeks was a river four feet below where it had been, going down at a rate a boy could watch, and a bank above a pond that had come down on the fourth day of the ninth rolling and buried about forty yards of old bank and a barrow and nothing else, and four villages with eleven places under water and nine gone.

The minute-man had the book open on the table and the roll chest against the wall where it has been since the first rolling, and he had not come in yet, and Ferrow was in the room early, which he does not do, because a first hand in a room before the men are in it is a first hand who has something to say and wants the room full before he says it.

He waited until the room was full. Then he said it in the middle of the floor, to Gace, in front of about forty men and eleven on the bench.

“Ordmar Gace. I have called your proposal dangerous for four rollings and I said so on the stair and in a gateway and twice to people who did not repeat it, and I am going to take it back in a room, because you are owed that in a room and not in a passage, and because a man who has been frightened of a thing for four rollings and then says so in a passage has only been frightened in public.”

Gace had a hat in his hands and put it down on the table.

“You have not been frightened of it,” Gace said. “You have been frightened of what it does to the stone.”

“Yes,” said Ferrow. “And I have been right to be. And I said in a doorway in the last week that the proposal of the third rolling is the least dangerous thing in this county, and I said I had not told you, and I have not told you since, and I have thought about telling you every day and I did not, because a man who is told he is not dangerous by the person who has been frightened of him for four rollings will believe it for about a day, and this county cannot afford a day of that.”

“And now?”

“Now the tenth rolling has come in dry and the pond has gone down two inches and the salt has not moved,” said Ferrow, “and I have had four rollings of watching a thing I called dangerous sit still while I was wrong about what I was frightened of. So I am taking it back, in this room, with your name and the rolling against it, and I am going to say the rest of it and then I am going to cost it, and the costing is the part I would have started with if I had not been sitting on it for four rollings.”

The plan is Gace's plan and it is not a bridgewright's, and the difference shows in the first thing Ferrow said about it.

“An oath that can be broken,” he said. “Not lifted. Not absolved. Broken, on purpose, in public, by the men who swore it, on a day named in advance, with a release in it, and a consequence, and no office behind it. He said that in this room in the third rolling and I have been calling it dangerous ever since and I would like the room to hear the exact shape of it, because a frightened man repeating a thing wrong is worse than a frightened man saying nothing.

“So here is the shape of it in my words, and then Coll Ferrow will put the first hand's words after mine, and he has held the water in this county for nineteen years and I have held it for none.

“Three things. First: the day. Nine men who renew the stone will name a day, a rolling ahead of today, out loud, in this room, and the day goes into the council's book with the rolling and the hour on it, and every man in this county will know it before it happens, which is the design entire and the reason it is a door and not a wall. A thing that can be broken on a day nobody knows is a thing that can be broken at night by a man with a spade.

“Second: the release. On that day, in the field before the trough, the nine men will speak the renewal and then they will speak the release, and the release is that any household in this county may declare itself loosed from the boundary as it has been sworn, and may work its own ground to spare the soil, and may say what it will not plant without forfeit of lease, and I am putting that in because this county has never in nineteen years had a rule about what a man will not put in his ground, and a thing you are not allowed to say out loud is a thing you say with your boots, and everybody in four villages has been saying it with its boots since before I was a first hand.

“Third: the consequence. A list. Households to be helped, with seed and with labour, entered by this council's own hand, read out at each sitting. Not a punishment. Not a reward. The list is what happens to you instead of an office you can appeal to, and that is everything a man who holds no office in this county can be given, and it is worth more than a fine and less than a pardon, and I have never in nineteen years had a better instrument put in front of me in a room.”

Nobody in the room said anything for a while, and outside a cart went over the ruts by the tithe barn and did not stop.

Delya Munn asked the question she asks, which is the one about the stone.

“The stone stands.”

“The stone stands,” said Ferrow. “The stone is the only evidence this county has ever made that anybody ever agreed to anything with anybody, and it is standing in a field in front of nine men who can be asked what they swore. A bench that lifts a stone is a bench that has been supplied with a date. A bench that breaks an oath in public on a day it named in advance has been supplied with a practice, and a practice is a thing that can happen again, and this county has never had one of those either.”

“And the four villages,” said the bound, from the end of the bench, in the voice he uses for a length of water.

“The four villages go on the list,” said Ferrow. “All four. Not the ones that broke it and not the ones that did not. The consequence of breaking an oath in a county that cannot refuse anything cannot be aimed at the people who broke it, because the moment it can be aimed it is a list of persons, and this county has been telling the truth about a list in a gateway to a child of eleven for eight rollings and I would like to keep being able to say it.”

Then he put the chalk on the board and costed it, and the room went quiet in the way a room does when a first hand is about to say a number out loud in front of men who are going to work.

“Nineteen of mine at eight pence the day,” he said. “A hundred and fifty-two pence. The four of the fen yard at our rate, which is eight pence each, is thirty-two pence between them. A day is a hundred and eighty-four pence.

“Nine days. A hundred and eighty-four, a hundred and eighty-four, a hundred and eighty-four, a hundred and eighty-four, a hundred and eighty-four, a hundred and eighty-four, a hundred and eighty-four, a hundred and eighty-four, a hundred and eighty-four. That is one thousand six hundred and fifty-six pence.

“Elm revetment off our own stacks at what the stacks would fetch, ninety-six pence, and I have put ninety-six in three columns now this year and it has not changed, and a figure that does not change is the only kind I trust.

“One thousand six hundred and fifty-six, and ninety-six, is one thousand seven hundred and fifty-two pence.”

“Thirty-six marks and two shillings,” said the bound.

“Thirty-six marks and two shillings, and the county has not got it, and will have it by the last day of the twelfth rolling or will not have it at all, and I have said that out loud before this room so that nobody in nine years can say I did not.”

He put the chalk down and picked it up again, which is not a thing a man does with a piece of chalk.

“Now the other half of it, and this is the half I would have started with. We opened four hundred and forty yards of ditch in three days in the fifth rolling and that was four hundred and eighty pence, and it is in the book with the addition on it, and I have not altered a figure in that book since the fifth rolling and I am not going to.

“This is one thousand seven hundred and fifty-two. The difference is one thousand two hundred and seventy-two pence.”

He waited for somebody to work it out and nobody did, because it is not a hard sum, it is an unpleasant one.

“Twenty-six marks and two shillings,” he said, “and that difference is what it costs to do a thing twice. The first time we opened a ditch in a field that had not been under water. The second time we are opening a mile of boundary in ground that has been under water since the seventh rolling and has not been dry under the top of it, by the two sides of it, in nine days, and there are about forty men in this room who hold a length of water for wages out of a rent roll that has been going down for nine years and there are four men in this room from a county four hundred miles off who are not being paid at all and are still here.

“So the plan costs one thousand seven hundred and fifty-two pence and the doing of it a second time costs one thousand two hundred and seventy-two pence, and the second figure is the one I would put in front of anybody who came up here with money, and the first figure is the one that looks like a bridge.”

The minute-man came in at about the second hour, which was late for him, and came in with his coat half on, and Aren Kest understood why and said nothing about it.

The minute-man had been at the weir.

It is not in the minute and it is not in any book in that county that the keeper of the minute spends the first hour of the tenth rolling at a hut on the water, and he did not say where he had been, and Ferrow looked at him and did not ask, and the two of them have known each other for nineteen years and one of them is the only man in that county who has ever been asked a question by a keeper and answered it.

The minute-man read the plan back. He read the three parts, and he read the day, and then he stopped and asked for the day in numbers instead of in words, because a day in numbers is a thing that can be entered and a day in words is a thing that can only be read out.

“The fourth week of the twelfth rolling,” Ferrow said. “The day the renewal has been on for a hundred and forty years is the fourth week and it is in the fourth week this year too, and we will not move it, because Gace's instrument consists of a man knowing the day. If we move the day into a week where nobody expects it, we have gone back to a thing that can be done at night by a man with a spade.”

The minute-man entered it, and read it back, and the entry in the council's book is the longest single entry in that book since the finding, and it is the first one in twenty-two years that names a day in the future.

*Entered, in the first week of the tenth rolling, that the council takes the proposal of the third rolling as spoken and adds to it, and that a day is named in advance for the breaking of the oath and the release, and that the day is the fourth week of the twelfth rolling, and that the day is not moved, and that the reason the day is not moved is that a man knowing the day is the instrument.*

*And entered: that the consequence is a list of households to be helped with seed and with labour, entered by the council's own hand, read at each sitting, and that the four villages of the white ground go on it whole and not by household and not by fault, and that the reason is that a consequence that can be aimed is a list of persons, and that this council said in a gateway in the second rolling that there was no list, and that the council has said it four times to four voices and it is in no book, and that the council is not going to be the reason a boy of eleven was lied to.*

The room made a small noise, and Munn looked up, and Ferrow said, before anybody else did, “Enter it as spoken and do not improve it,” and the minute-man entered it and read it back.

Aren Kest had been sitting at the end of the bench with a board and a stub he had not written on since the sixth rolling. He had come up the outside stair that morning at the fourth hour with the intention of saying something, and he had not said it, and the reason he had not said it was the sentence he entered in his own book in the fourth rolling of the year before, which was that a man who is not asked is not to ask, and which Ferrow had named out loud in a room in the eighth rolling in front of eleven people, and which was therefore no longer a thing he could be using in private without it being a lie about the same thing twice.

So he sat at the end of the bench and let a county take a plan he had not made, which is the first time in nine rollings that the thing he came four hundred miles to do has been done by somebody else and been better, and the word for what that costs him is in a document and not in a scene and there is a fair copy of it on a board under this bench that any person coming up two flights may read.

The last hour of the tenth rolling is when the year happened.

The minute-man said it, and he said it flat, and he said it to the room and not to the council, and he had been not-saying things to rooms for twenty-two years and this was the other kind.

“Then there are three rollings after this one,” he said, “and a year is thirteen rollings from the first sowing and a rolling is four weeks, so the year is a year and a rolling, and the year ends in the fourth week of the thirteenth and the first sowing of the next one is within a week of that, and it is the first sowing in thirteen rollings that this county has made without a stone being in the ground for it.

“I have been in this room twenty-two years and I have entered every year in this book, and the way I enter a year is by its stone, because that is the only thing a year in this county does, and this year there is going to be a sowing and a boundary and they are not going to be the same event, and in nine years somebody will come to this book to find out what was sown and there will be nothing in it, because the record of a sowing is a stone and the stone is a boundary and a boundary is not a return.”

The slate woman asked him what he proposed to do about it, and he said he did not propose anything, because a man who keeps a book is the last man in a county who gets to decide what goes in it, and the only thing he had decided was that the year would be entered as a year and that the sowing would be entered as a sowing, in the same run of the page, in the same hand, and that if a man comes in nine years and wants to know what this county sowed he will have to read two entries and not one and he will not be able to make one line out of the other.

The four white villages will sow in the twelfth rolling, because the ground has to be turned and the water has to be off it, and the crop comes off in the thirteenth, and this county has had eleven places under water in the lowest field at Sallow Marsh and nine gone, and nobody in this room knows what that crop will do and it is the only thing in nine rollings that none of us can put in a book.

Nobody said anything for a while. Then the man of fifty-two, from the floor, said the one sentence he said in the last year he said one, and it was not about the crop.

“You said the number of the days on the board,” he said, “and you said the difference between the two sums is what it costs to do a thing twice, and you did not say whose the second time was.”

“It was ours,” said Ferrow.

“It was ours,” the man of fifty-two agreed, “and I have been in this county since the fourth week of your seventh rolling and I have watched a great many things be done once by you and twice by us, and I would like the book to know that I know the difference, and I am not asking for anything.”

The minute-man looked at him for a moment and then wrote, and the entry is nine words long with a date and no hour, in the guild's book and not the council's, because a bench can be asked to answer and a trade cannot, and the trade cannot be made to answer for four men from a county four hundred miles off who have never once been asked what they swore.

Outside, in the lane, about forty men were getting ready to go and dig a mile of boundary in ground that had been under water for three weeks, and the third hour of the tenth rolling was still an hour away, and the eleventh rolling would begin on ground that had already been dug once and filled in twice, and the water table under four villages would come down over four years if the digging went on, and nothing anybody in this room did in the tenth rolling would make it come down this year.
