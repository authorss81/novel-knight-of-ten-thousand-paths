# Chapter 0133

The Water came back down over the sixth rolling in a way the county had no word for, and the county had a word for everything that came out of the ground in eleven villages, so the word it had for a river that was sitting in the wrong bed above the town was the word it used for a man who has said something he cannot take back, and nobody in the market town said it, because nobody came down to look.

The sixth rolling was quiet in the way a rolling is quiet when the work is done and everybody can see what it did and there is nothing else to do that week. The water came down the new trench at the head and it went away at the trough and the pond against the low side of the bank had gone down two inches, which Thessa's board said, and the salt in the lowest field at Sallow Marsh had not moved, and the first barley of the rolling went in to four villages and came up in patches, and the year was going to be a bad year, and everybody knew it, and there is a difference between a bad year that comes on you and a bad year that you have been given four months' notice of.

Then Ferrow had the causeway built.

It went across the old bend four hundred yards above the town, from a stub of the boundary bank on the town's side to a rise of gravel on the far side, one hundred and sixteen feet of it, of elm deck on four bays with a handrail on the upstream side only, because the upstream side is the side the bank gives way on and a rail on that side is a rail a man can get hold of when the deck is going, and a rail on the other side is a rail for looking at.

He had four days' notice. Not four days' warning of anything: four days before the ground stopped taking footings, which was a fact about the ground and not about the weather, and he took them, and the guild worked all four of them, three and a half on the deck and the rest on the approaches, and nineteen men and four of a fen yard and a stranger with a bar were on it, and the money was not what anybody expected and was the argument the whole county argued about for the rest of the rolling.

The bound read it out in the guild room on the fourth evening and read it twice because the first time he had a figure in the wrong place and started again.

“Nineteen men at eight pence the day. Eight and nineteen is a hundred and fifty-two pence the day. Four days is a hundred and fifty-two, a hundred and fifty-two, a hundred and fifty-two and a hundred and fifty-two, which is six hundred and eight pence. Elm for the deck and oak for the rails, off our own stacks, at what the stacks would fetch, ninety-six pence. Six hundred and eight and ninety-six is seven hundred and four pence.”

“Fourteen marks and two shillings and eightpence,” said the bound, and then read it again, because the second time was for the room.

“It is on the council's account and not the guild's,” said Ferrow, “because it is not the guild's ditch. It is a deck across a channel that the Water has not been in for longer than any of us, and the council has a road to the north villages and a road is a causeway and the people who keep it are this guild, and that is the arrangement in this county and I have not been asked to change it. I am telling the room that we are four hundred and forty yards into a job we were not paid for and seven hundred and four pence into a second one, and that the council has no money and the guild has less, and that in about nine years somebody will find both of those figures in two books and put them side by side and call one of them a favour.”

The woman of sixty on three hearths was in the room for it, which she had never been in the room for before and did not explain, and she said one thing and went out again before the argument started.

“My lowest hearth is the one you are standing on,” she said. “Not the ground, the hearth, the house. It is eleven yards off that channel and it has been four years since I have had anybody in the house, and my grandmother’s hearth is the other one and the third is up the lane. The guild is building a deck across a place where the Water has not been since before anybody’s grandmother and nobody has ever said the name of the thing it is going to be in.”

Nobody said the name of the thing, and she went, and Ferrow let her go without one word, and afterwards he told the room that the reason he had not said it either was that a guild which says out loud what a deck is for is a guild which has decided the deck is a flood deck, and a flood deck is a thing the county has never wanted, because a flood deck means the council is expecting a flood, and a council that expects a flood is a council with a plan, and this county has never had a plan for anything except a boundary.

The third day of the four, in the last of the light, a man with a lantern went into the seed room behind the chapel.

Nobody sent him. He was one of the guild's men, forty-one years old, a digger, and he had been in the room twice that day carrying a bench out of the way of a wet patch, and the second time the water in the room had been over his bootlaces, and when he came to shift the bench the third time there was a foot of it and he had a lantern in his hand because the light had gone and there was no other way to get at the shelf.

He went in. The room is nine feet by fourteen with nine sealed jars on a shelf along the north wall at the height of a man's chest, and a wall on the other side with two blocked doorways in it, and the water coming up through a gap in the floor at the far end, and a woman of sixty-six in it up to above the knee with a lamp held over her head and nine jars being moved one at a time from a shelf to a higher shelf, and she did not put the lamp down.

He had a lantern in his left hand and his right hand out, and he put the right hand on the wall to find the way, because you always put your hand on a wall, and the wall under his hand was the wall, and it is the outside of the wall that he would have been touching if he had been outside, and the inside of the wall is plaster and the outside of it is stone and there are three feet of it between the two, and he put his hand on plaster.

The lamp was above the nine jars and the lantern was below his own hand, and he had come in off the far end where the floor was under, and he did not know about the floor inside the third course, and he took one step and put the lantern through it.

It went in about a foot and stopped, and the glass did not break because the lantern is a lantern and it is a foot inside a stone room, and his hand was on the wall and he had nine inches of the thing in the water and he was a man of forty-one in a flooded stone room in the dark and he did the only thing anybody does in that position, which is to go down on his hands and knees and stop.

The floor came up under his knees. Not the near floor, not the one he had come in on. The other one, three feet off the ground, inside the third course, three feet of dry stone in a room with a foot of water in it, and dry, because the water had come through the floor at the far end and had gone round the wall base and had not come up through the middle of a raised floor built in a year when the men building it had a good season and had laid it on a bed of gravel.

He got his lantern out and set it on the stone, and his knee was on a floor that is in a wall in nine bits of tin in a mason's language, and it did not matter, and nobody ever knew it, and the floor held him, and he stood up on it, and the nine jars were over his head.

The woman of sixty-six looked at the marks on the wall in the lamplight for a long time and said nothing, and the man of forty-one stood on the inside of the third course and said, “I did not know there was a floor.”

“There is a floor,” she said. “It is a different floor. That is all it is. I have not written it down anywhere and I am not going to, and you are not going to tell anybody that you found it, and if you tell anybody that you found it then in nine years there will be men in here who want to know how high it is, and how high it is is a number in a head, and a number in a head that gets out of a head becomes a number in a book, and then there is an office, and I have kept this room forty years with no office in it.”

“I am not going to tell anybody,” the man said, and he did not, and the only person he told was Ferrow at the end of the week because Ferrow asked him about the floor of the room in a yard and a man who has put a lantern through a floor and been saved by a floor answers a question about floors.

Ferrow did not go and look. He asked the digger one question, which was whether the mark was low, and the digger said it was low, and Ferrow said, “Then the man who put it there did not want a man to find a floor to stand on,” and went on shingling, and the guild's book has no line about it, and the council's minute has no line about it, and the only account of it in the world is a digger's memory of a wet week and a sentence a woman of sixty-six said with a lamp over her head.

The deck was finished on the third and a half day and the fourth day went on the approaches, and the approaches are what nobody in the county has ever thought about since. A causeway is not a bridge. A bridge ends at a pier. A causeway is four hundred yards of road that happens to be over water in one place, and its cost is in the two ends, and a man from a fen county knows that and a county that has never had a bridge in it does not. Ferrow spent the fourth day on the two ends with eight men, made them eleven feet wide at the heads, put a sill of oak across each and set the deck eleven inches above the top of the sill, and the eleven inches is the guild’s entire argument with the water in this structure and there is not one line of it in any book.

Thirty-one days is how long the standing water was in the new trench above the town after the work was done, and the count of them is in the guild's book in one line with the hour, and the line says thirty-one and not thirty and not thirty-two, because a man who is going to be wrong about a number in a book four hundred years from now writes the number he counted and not the number he expected.

The barrowloads that came out of the trench went into the bank, because there was nowhere else for them to go and the bank is the boundary, and for thirty-one days there was a hundred and forty feet of new bank four hundred yards above the town, higher than the old bank and looser than the old bank and made in a hurry by sixteen men and four of a fen yard in three days, and a hundred and forty feet of it sat downstream of everything else on a mile of boundary, and Ferrow looked at it every morning for thirty-one days without saying one word about it to anybody.

On the thirty-first day the water in the trench went down in an afternoon, all at once, the way standing water goes when the ground under it finally takes, and the boy with the pole read the board at the weir and then read it again and said the number out loud twice, and it was four inches under the winter mark, and it was the first time in six rollings that anybody had said a number at that board that was not higher than the last one.

Thessa Wend wrote it in her book in her own hand and put the two readings side by side and the two of them were hers, and she took the page to the council on the following morning and the slate woman read it out and the minute-man entered it as observed and not as decided, which was the phrase the council had used for the hand's width in the first rolling and had not improved on since, and Munn asked whether the council was going to say anything about it and the minute-man said that the council was going to do what it had done in the first rolling, which was to enter it and let the year decide, and Munn said, “Then the year will decide it and the year is the same year that the barley came up in patches,” and nobody had an answer to that either, and the sitting went on.

Aren was on the staging at seven that morning, as he had been every morning since the fifth rolling, and the four men of the fen yard were on it with him, and the seventh rolling was two days off and the sixth rolling had gone past in a way that felt, to a man who had come here to be asked for something, like being let alone on purpose.

In the last of the sixth rolling Ferrow came out to the staging where the sill for the new approach was laid out and the four men were planing it and the stranger was holding a line they were not using, and he did not say anything about water or banks or the thirty-first day, and he said one thing.

“I have not told the council why I built that deck and I am not going to, and I want you to have heard me say that I have not, and I want you to have heard the date, which is the first week of this rolling, so that if a man asks you in nine years you can say the first week and be exact without knowing why. You can keep a date. You cannot keep a reason and be right, and I would rather a county had the date.”

Aren put the line down.

“Why,” he said.

Ferrow looked at him for a while with the shingle in his hand, and then he said the thing that the second third of this county's year is made of.

“Because a ditch is work and a deck is a promise,” he said, “and this council will take the work and it has never in nineteen years taken a promise, and I am not going to spend nineteen years being right about water in a room that has a book in it. When the water comes, it will come, and it will come on the last week of the sixth rolling or the first week of the seventh or the second week of the seventh, and it will be in the wrong part of the year, because this year's water has been in the wrong part of the year since I was thirty and there is nobody in this county who can tell me why and I have stopped asking. And the guild will be asleep. And the deck will be there. That is all of it. I have built a thing in four days for a day I cannot name, and the naming of the day is not a thing a first hand of a guild gives to a council, and if I gave it to them this year then in four years there would be a book with a date in it and a bench that had decided to expect it, and a bench that has decided to expect a flood is a bench that will not hear a guild say the water is falling.”

He put the shingle down and went back to the deck, and the four men of the fen yard had heard every word of it and not one of them wrote it down, and Aren went up to the town alone and did not write it down either, and put the date in his own book with the hour, because a man may keep a date.

He went up past the seed room and looked at the outside of the wall in the last of the light without stopping, because a man does not stop at a wall, and the three marks were above the line the water had come to the year before and the line the water came to that week was a foot and a half above them, and it had been a foot and a half above them for eleven days and had not gone down, and there was gravel in the chapel corner again and a woman of sixty-six sweeping it out at dusk with a besom and no lamp, because it was not dark yet and she did not need one.
