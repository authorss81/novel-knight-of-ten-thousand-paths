# Chapter 0128

Ordmar Gace held no office and farmed twelve acres on a short lease at the edge of Sallow Marsh. He was forty-nine, weathered, slow-spoken, with a tenant’s habit of answering a question with the field it belonged to. He led what the county called the tenant league, though it kept no roll and collected no dues, being only men and women on short ground who met in barns and spoke of what they dared not plant.

Aren met him in a barn with perhaps twenty others, at Ferrow’s suggestion and with Munn’s knowledge. The yard’s four men came too, because Ferrow wanted northern labour to hear what northern labour would be asked to work beside.

Gace stated his want without preamble, in his own voice. A man should be able to say what he would not plant and not lose the field for it. The white ground took wheat and returned straw. Barley did better one year in three. Beans held the soil but paid nothing at market. A tenant who sowed beans to spare the ground was told he wasted the landlord’s acres, and a tenant who sowed wheat to please the landlord watched it pale. To say openly what the ground would bear, and to be heard without forfeit, was the league in one sentence.

He was right about the wages, and proved it with the slate. The guild’s rate for ditch labour was eight pence a day for a man. A tenant hiring himself out at that rate for nine days lost nine days of his own field at the very part of the season when beans needed hoeing. He chalked the figure and footed it before them, because no figure went unfooted in a room he stood in.

Eight pence for the first three days was twenty-four pence. Eight for the next three was twenty-four more, which made forty-eight. Eight for the last three was twenty-four more, which made seventy-two pence. He said the total aloud. Seventy-two pence for nine days, against a field going to weeds.

“Paid in salt at nine pence the bushel,” he said, “that is eight bushels. Eight bushels of salt for nine days off my own ground. I do not refuse the wage. I refuse the season it falls in.”

The room murmured. He was on the council’s side in every argument about money, for the council had no money and the guild had no money and the nine who renewed the stone had what money there was, and he said so plainly. He was against the council in one thing only: the council would not write down that a tenant might decline a sowing and keep his lease, because writing it would make the council answerable to landlords, and the council answered to nobody by design.

Ferrow, present as a guest, said the guild wanted the same nineteen men all year at a rate they could refuse, and Gace’s season was the guild’s season stood on its head. Munn, also present, said the council could not give what Gace asked because the council had no standing with landlords and would not take one. Both were honest, and Gace thanked them for the honesty without conceding the point.

The yard’s man at the drawknife spoke from the back, saying he and his mates were strangers on the county’s own calendar, not on the bench and not on the oath and never asked what they swore, and that a bargain that let a man say no and keep his place was the only bargain worth travelling four hundred miles for. Nobody wrote that down, and Gace nodded to him as one working man to another.

Aren said little. He watched Gace hold a room without office or oath, by stating a cost and refusing to flatter it, and thought of benches he had known that could not do as much with seal and writ. The barn smelled of hay and lamp oil, and the twenty tenants listened the way people listen when the talk is about their own fields, and the meeting ended with no resolution, because the league did not resolve but remembered.

Walking back, Ferrow said Gace was the most dangerous man in the county, because he asked for nothing the council could give and everything it should want to give. Aren said dangerous was the wrong word for a man asking to name what his ground would bear. Ferrow said that was why it was the right word.

At the staging the yard’s men were banking the fire for the night. The drawknife man asked Gace plainly whether tenants would stand the nine days’ ditch labour if the wage came in salt. Gace said they would stand it if the season allowed and the lease held, and not otherwise, and a wage that cost a field was no wage. The drawknife man nodded slowly, recognising a reckoning he had made himself in other counties, and offered Gace his hand on it, man to man, with no book between.

Aren wrote that evening that the league wanted no more than a sentence: to say what ground would bear without forfeit. A small want, and the rolling turned on small wants more than on great terms. The seventy-two pence stood footed on the slate in his memory, eight bushels of salt against nine days off the land, and the arithmetic explained the county better than any oath.
