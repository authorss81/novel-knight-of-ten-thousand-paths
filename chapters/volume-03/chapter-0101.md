# Chapter 0101

The thirtieth of Greenmonth, YR 313, was a Tuesday, it was not a market day, and the county's market days were on Saturday and Thursday, and the river was a finger up on the lowest water of the year, which had been the day before, which meant the Sedge Reach deck was dry under the third bay and had been dry under the third bay for two days running and nobody in this county had ever written that down anywhere.

Aren Kest was on the deck of the third bay at the second hour with a tool bag, and there were nine men on it, and the work was pointing.

The stone had gone down on the twenty-fourth and the twenty-fifth and the twenty-sixth and had been pointed on the twenty-seventh and the twenty-eighth and was being pointed again on the thirtieth because pointing is the one job on a bridge that is never finished on the day it is begun, and the fourth of the eleven tons was in the toe of the north pier and the rest of it was spread along the cutwater in courses that the man at the drawknife had laid himself and would not have anybody else's.

"You have done the third joint twice," said the man at the drawknife, without stopping.

"I have done the third joint twice," Aren Kest agreed, "and I am going to do it a third time, and the reason is that the first time I was tired and the second time the boy was watching me, and those are two different reasons and only one of them is in the trade."

The lime came out of a barrow that had been mixed on the Sunday and had gone stiff in the middle, and they cut the middle out and used the ends, and the weather was a quarter inch of it in a wind that came off the fen and did nothing with it, and the whole of that morning was a bridge holding.

It is worth saying what that means, because this county has spent eleven years being a place where a bridge is a thing people argue about. A bridge holding is a bridge that is carrying what it was built to carry and is going on carrying it, and there is nothing to be done to it in the morning, and about forty men find that out every time and nine of them find it out badly and go home and think about the winter. The man at the drawknife had said the whole of it out loud once, in the yard, in a fortnight when the yard was a fortnight, and had said it in these words: *a road that is holding is a road nobody has a reason to be clever about, and clever men in a county like this one are the most expensive thing on the deck.* Nobody improved it and it has been in the yard a hundred and ten days.

The toll-taker came out at about the third hour with a complaint. There had been a wagon on the reach that morning with a load of reed four inches wider than the gap, and the wagon had gone over on the gravel and had cost the town nothing at all, and the toll-taker wanted it entered as a loss. The man at the drawknife said a wagon that goes over on the gravel and costs nobody nothing is a toll-taker's best morning. The toll-taker said the town had a rule about widths and the rule was the rule. The bench-clerk, who was in the gateway for some reason of his own, said the width rule had a figure in it and the figure was the width of a cart and not the width of a load, and that a load four inches over a cart is not a breach of anything and is a fact about a morning.

"And write it in the roll," said the man at the drawknife.

"I will not write it in the roll," said the bench-clerk. "**I write in the roll what the bench decides, and the bench has decided nothing, and the best thing that has happened on that deck since the sixth of Mudmonth is that nothing happened on it, and if I put that in the roll somebody will come along in nine years and read it and think this county is a place where things are entered. It is a place where things are kept out.**"

He counted load points when he came out onto the deck at about the fourth hour, because that is what he does, and it is a habit and not a working and there is no book in this county that it goes in. Four in the toe of the north pier, four in the toe of the third, two under the cutwater. He did it twice to see whether he had got the same four, and he had, and the two he got the second time were the two the lime had been standing on all morning and not the two the stone had been set on, which is a thing about a new deck that nobody teaches.

---

**The bench was asked a fourth time at the fourth hour, in the guild hall over the wharf, and the whole of the asking took about five minutes, and the page in the guild's roll is now the second shortest thing in it.**

The ask was for the Old Toll.

The Old Toll crossing is in municipal trust and the trust has been in abeyance since before this bench existed, and the tolls on it are suspended by an instrument a court has found lawful and has said it has nothing further to say about at any time, and so the crossing has no owner and no money and no seat on this bench and no glass in the sill and no third seat, and it is the oldest working crossing in the county, and it is four hundred yards up the road from where the bench sits.

There is a dressed block in its abutment and forty yards of tarred hemp, put there on the fourth of Hearthmonth of the year before by four men and a boy on a road warden who had said in a hall that he would come when he was wanted.

And there is a man in the room who has come in on the strength of the book.

He is the same man twice. He entered his name in the subscription book in the market in the fourth of Frostmonth and entered it again in the ninth of Hearthmonth because the first time he had put it in a column headed *In the hand of* and the second time he put it in a column headed *Name*, and he has come in on the thirtieth of Greenmonth to ask whether a name entered twice in one book is one name or two, and the bench-clerk has told him, without looking up, that it is one name, and that the bench-clerk has not entered the second one, and that the bench-clerk entered the first one and has been sorry for sixteen weeks and does not know how to take it out.

"Then leave it in," said the man, who was about sixty and had a cart and one horse and no other money that anybody knew of.

"I cannot leave it in," said the bench-clerk. "It is in a book that anybody may read in a corridor in this hall and a name entered twice in a subscription book is a thing two people can be shown, and the second person is the person it is about, and he has not consented to be in it twice."

"I consented to be in it once."

"Then I have made a mess of the once," said the bench-clerk, "and I would rather have the mess in the open where I can see it, and I have entered it in the margin in a fair copy and the margin says that the second entry was made by a copyist and not by the man, and the copyist is me, and my name is at the head of the book and anybody who wants to be angry at somebody can be angry at me on a Thursday."

About nine people in the room watched this happen and two of them understood that the whole of the county's money was in it, and the reeve said nothing, because a reeve who says nothing while a bench-clerk is caught being honest is a reeve who has understood something about a bench that is not a bench.

"Fourth time," said the bench-clerk, who was a careful man of nineteen years' standing in a copyist's trade and had not been a member of the bench and never would be. "Asked. Declined, with a chest and a book. I am obliged to put to the bench that this is the fourth asking, and that the sum wanted is the sum wanted in the third, and that the chest has two marks in it and the book is in a market and has had four names in it since the fourth of Frostmonth and two of them are the same man twice."

"Yes," said Reeve Aldis Fenn.

"Will the bench ask a fifth time."

"Yes," said the reeve, "and I would like the minute to carry three things and I would like them carried in my words and not improved on."

The room waited, because the reeve has nine years in that room and has learned that a woman who says three things has counted them.

"First," she said, "that Tovan Kest said in this hall in the fourth of Frostmonth that a bench asked three times and saying yes three times is not a bench, it is a subscription, and the bench voted for the thing in spite of it, and the minute of the ninth month carries that, and the bench has asked a fourth time and voted a fourth time in spite of it again, and I want that on this page as a fact and not as a complaint, because it is a fact and because the only thing worse than a bench that does not know what it is is a bench that knows and does not write it down.

"Second. The Old Toll is a crossing and it is in abeyance and this bench has no power over it and cannot have any power over it while the trust is in abeyance, and I am asking for money for a thing I have no power over, and I would like the minute to say that I said that out loud in a room, because in about nine years somebody is going to read that this bench asked for the Old Toll and is going to assume that the bench could have done something about the Old Toll."

Nobody improved on either of them, because the bench-clerk read them back exactly.

"Third," said the reeve, "and it is short, and it is about a man with a board and sixpence, and I would like it before the bench votes."

---

**And the third thing is the reason this page is in the roll, and it took about eleven words and the room did not go quiet because the room had eleven men in it and eleven men can make a noise on purpose.**

The guild's man with the levels had been paid six pence on the twenty-fourth, out of a surveyor's box that is not a fund, for a written report on the scour at the third pier, and he had put it in his own book with the hour on it, and the county's surveyor had told nobody, and the thing had been on a deck and not in a room and therefore had gone nowhere at all.

He had been paid six pence again on the thirtieth, out of the same box, by the same hand, for a second written report, and this one was on the Old Toll.

"I have been paid six pence again," he said, "out of the same box, for a second report, and nobody in this room has been told, and I am telling it myself, and it is about eleven words: **the county has paid a man twice and has told nobody.**"

Somebody laughed, and one man laughed long enough that it turned into something else, and the bench-clerk put his pen down and picked it up again, which he had not done in three years.

"Enter it," said the reeve.

"Enter what, precisely," said the bench-clerk, "because I am a copyist and I would like to know whether I am entering a payment."

"Enter that a man of this guild has been paid six pence out of a box twice, on the twenty-fourth and the thirtieth of Greenmonth, for written reports on the Sedge Reach and the Old Toll, and that the payments came out of the county surveyor's box, and that the bench has not been told of either of them by anybody until this minute." The reeve put both hands flat on the table, which she does not do. "And enter that the bench does not know whose box it is, and that I want to know, and that I am asking the question in a room and not in a gateway, and that if the answer is that it is a fund then the fund is a box and the box is in a surveyor's house and this bench has voted four times for money out of a chest in this hall and has never once voted for money out of that box, and I would like the difference on the page."

"Entered," said the bench-clerk.

The county's surveyor was in the room. He had come about a boundary stone and had been sitting at the end of the long table for the whole of it with his hat on his knee and had not expected to be asked anything, and he said:

"It is my box. It has been my box for nine years and it is a box for small survey work and it has never had a name and it is not a fund because a fund is voted and a box is not voted, and I did not tell the bench because I did not think the bench would want to hear that I had spent sixpence of my own money on a hole, and I have now been told twice on a market day in front of eleven people and I would like the minute to say that I paid it out of my own money and not out of the county's, because that is the only part of it that is mine."

"Enter that too," said the reeve, "and enter that the box has no name, and that a box with no name in a minute is the fourth thing this bench has been told about this year that is a document and not a person."

The bench voted. It asked a fourth time and was given two marks and a book for the fourth time, and it voted a fourth time, and the whole of the vote took under a minute, and the chest still has two marks in it.

---

**What changed was not the money. It is that a bench which cannot pay for a crossing has now minuted a defect in it, and a man who owns a box has been obliged to put the box in a minute in his own county.**

The guild's man with the levels read the Old Toll himself on the afternoon of the thirtieth and put it in the same book with the hour on it, and it is a page in about four books in this county and it is the second entry in one, and what it says is this, in his own hand, and it was copied into the guild's roll that evening at the reeve's instruction, which nobody in this county has ever done with a page out of that man's own book:

*Old Toll, abutment, north. The dressed block has been in sixteen weeks and the hemp has held and the sill has not moved. The water is under the sill at the low and out of it at anything else, and the sill is sound, and there is no glass in it and there never was, and there is no third seat, and I would like it entered that both of those are absences and not defects and that I have looked for a third seat twice and it is not a thing anybody has taken away from this bridge.*

*And the abutment. There is a wet place on the north cheek of the abutment about a foot above the hemp line where the stone is dark and has been dark since the fourth of Hearthmonth when it was laid, and it is not running, and it is not salt, and it is not the river, and I have had my thumb on it and my eye on it for sixteen weeks and I cannot tell you what it is.*

*And I am going to say the hard thing, because I have been paid sixpence to say a hard thing and I have been paid sixpence twice and both times it was for this: **a crossing in trust in abeyance is a crossing that nobody is paid for, and a crossing nobody is paid for is read by whoever is passing, and being read by whoever is passing is not the same as being read by anybody whose name is on it.** That is the whole of my report and the sixpence is a year a day for me, so do not think I am being generous.*

That page went into the guild's roll, and the copy is in the corridor press in the hall where anybody may come and read it, and eleven people read it that week, and two of them were angry with him, and one of them said it was the only honest page in the roll.

---

**And at about the eighth hour on the thirtieth of Greenmonth, YR 313, a road warden of the Merefen bridge guild stood in a gateway on Weir Street with a wet sleeve and asked a yard whether it had anything from a county it had never been to.**

Nothing had come. There was no such thing on any road. He had asked because a man who asks a question he has no reason to ask is doing one of two things and he wanted to know which, and it turned out to be the second.

He went back up to the room over the cooper and wrote nothing down that night, which is the second time in four years he has written nothing down on a night when he had something, and the first time was the night a woman in a clay county asked him a question he could not answer.
