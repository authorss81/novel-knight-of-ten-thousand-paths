# Chapter 0123

The hut at the weir held one room and a stove and a plank on two bricks, and a book under a board, because a book left on a shelf in a house in this county is a thing a neighbour can read while the keeper is at the water.

Thessa had been up since the second hour and had not lit the stove until she noticed it. A woman who has read a post twice a day for eleven years and has never been asked anything has a way of being alone that is not lonely and not waiting. It is the posture of a person expecting no knock.

There had been no knock, and that was the trouble. She had spoken nine sentences in a wet field before eleven people and sixty more, every word worked out alone in this room across eleven years, given away for nothing to a woman with a slate standing in a furrow. The price was that she was now a person who had spoken, and a person who has spoken can be asked what she meant.

She sat with the book on her knees and did not open it. The book held eight thousand and thirty-one lines in her small quick left-sloping hand, two a day since the first sowing, with one line for the day she started. Nine lines in the middle were in another hand, an older paper, the only lines in eleven years that were not readings, left for a week she had not been present, with space kept round them for eleven years. She had shown them once to the stranger and told him a gauge read twice a day by the man whose land lay under it was no gauge but a thing agreed with, and he had not pressed her, and the not pressing had been the hardest courtesy in the week.

The boy came down with bread and cheese and windfalls wrapped in oiled cloth, left the bundle, and went off without a word. Somebody who could not read had left food most weeks for eleven years, never with a name. She ate the bread, pocketed the apples, and left the cheese for the evening.

Aren came to the sill in the middle of the morning with his boots in his hand, because the ground below the hut was soft. She told him she would tell him where the nine sentences came from, and would not tell the council, and wanted to know if he would keep it. He said he had carried a notebook twelve days and told nobody anything in it, and would not start. She said that was a habit and not a promise, and she would take a habit.

Her brother Perrin had been twenty-two and had drowned in the low week in a ditch below the Sallow foot, in a season when the ditch had been opened in a week by hired hands. The man who read the gauge that week was her father, who held the water above the ditch and had signed for the work because the boundary had to stand at the trough by the renewal. The reading was taken by the man whose signature stood on the labour, and written in a hut by a woman with no power to refuse anything. She had never said that in a room, in this county or any other, and she had said its substance in the field because somebody had finally asked her a question, and a question was a door and a person walked out of it whatever lay beyond.

She told him she had wanted a reading by somebody who did not own the weir since the first year, and the reason was the drowning, and she had never said the reason aloud and this was the saying of it. He heard it through to the end and did not write it down in front of her and did not offer comfort, because comfort from a stranger in a hut would have made the telling smaller.

Then he asked the one question, badly, saying he had been thirteen days in the county and there was a thing in the field nobody had named, and he could not mend it and would not pretend he could, and she might call it foolish and he would go down to the water and come back and they would both have had a morning.

“How do you keep a promise that has been kept for a hundred and forty years and is killing everybody who keeps it?” he said.

She did not answer for a long while, while water came over the second lower post and fell back. At last she said there were two ways. Keep it and let it end you, or break it and learn that morning whether you were a person who could be asked. She had looked eleven years with pole and book for a third way and there was none, because a promise kept out of doors before the people it concerned was the only kind anybody here had ever learned to make, and it was a good kind, and she would not give it up.

He said yes, and she said he had not chosen either way, and he said no.

Before she went down to the water she took a leaf ruled off straight, and in her own hand wrote the bank: a hand’s width a year on the same line at the trough above the Sallow foot, four stretches she could put a year to, learned from nobody, never asked for. She dated it by the county’s own count and set it on the bench facing the door, the way a thing is laid in a hut for the next person coming in.

He did not ask her for a line of the book. She did not ask him for the bank. Both refusals happened in the same breath and neither named it, and the not asking was not courtesy and not a rule from a form, and it was the one act either of them had done in this county that neither would ever be able to write down.

She gathered the nine readings for the council in her own hand on a sheet torn straight, weir post and Sallow foot in two columns, with no name signed because the council’s book had no column for a name either. She went down the bank with both leaves, and he followed with his boots in his hand, and neither expected gratitude and neither wanted it.

The climb to the barn took them past the yard’s staging, where the four northern men were setting feet in soft ground with the patience of men who knew soft ground. The drawknife man looked up and nodded to them, and Thessa nodded back, and neither spoke, because a morning’s work asks no questions of another’s.

In the chamber the slate woman received the nine lines as if receiving a relic, and read them aloud, and the minute-man entered them as a page in another hand kept by the council. Thessa stood while her own figures were read, hands clasped, eyes on the slot of light. When it was done she said the leaf with the bank went with the readings or not at all, and the council, uncertain, accepted both, and entered the hand’s width as observed and not as decided.

Munn thanked her with care, choosing words that promised nothing. Thessa accepted the care and not the thanks, saying the figures were for the county to use or leave, and her want remained what it had been since the first year: a reading by someone who did not own the weir. The slate woman said the council had no such person and would have to become one, and the remark hung there, too large for the room, and no one took it up.

Aren watched from the slot and said nothing in the room, and no one thanked him for the silence, because silence was not a service to be thanked but a debt to be carried. On the stair afterward Thessa said to him alone that giving the page had frightened her more than speaking in the field, because a spoken sentence lived in memory and a written page lived in keeping, and keeping was longer. He said keeping was also narrower, and she said that was why she had given only nine lines and not the book, and he understood that the boundary of her gift was its meaning.
