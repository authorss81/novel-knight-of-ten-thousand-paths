# Chapter 0119

The country is eleven villages and a market town and a river the people here call the Water, and the Water is in spate, and it is in spate in the wrong part of the year, and a man who has been out of his own country four hundred miles can tell that in about an hour without being told anything, because a river in spate in the wrong part of the year is not a river being fed by rain, it is a river being fed by a ditch system that has stopped doing the one job a ditch system has, and every ditch in that grid is straight, and every straight ditch is a ditch somebody built on purpose a long time ago and has not touched since.

**On the second day he walked the low side with Coll Ferrow and a boy of fourteen who carried a pole, and it took until the light went, and he counted every ditch on that side and could have told you the year of about nine of them by the way the bank has gone over on the downstream face, and Ferrow watched him do it and said nothing at all for two hours, and then said: "You are the first man from anywhere who has looked at the bank."**

They stopped at about the twentieth. Ferrow put the pole in himself and made the boy read the water and then read it after him, and the boy said it a finger different from what Ferrow had said, and Ferrow did not correct him and made him say it again, and the third time the boy said what Ferrow had said the first time.

"That is a gauge and that is a boy learning a gauge," said Ferrow, "and the day he says what I said the first time is the day I have to be careful. **I have forty men and about nine of them are under thirty and one of those nine is that boy, and the wage of a man who holds a length of water comes out of a rent roll that has been shrinking for nine years, and in about four years there will not be a boy, and I have asked for the wage three times and the bench said no in a full room and was right to.**"

"Are they short now."

"They are paid," said Ferrow. "That is the thing about it. They are not short. They are paid out of a roll that is going down and a man who is paid out of a roll that is going down stops being able to refuse anything, and by the time he notices he has stopped being able to refuse anything, and that is not a thing anybody has to be wicked to arrange."

**And the boy of fourteen walked a mile ahead of them for the rest of the afternoon and did not turn round once, and the reason he did not turn round is that a man who holds a length of water learns to be looked at, and there was a stranger in the county looking at him.**

On the third day a woman took him down into the lowest of the four villages where the ground is white, and the white is not a frost and not a salt crust on the surface. It is a field that has gone the colour of a bone and it is wet under your boot, and the boot comes up with a white smear that is gritty, and the grit is not sand.

And on the fourth and the fifth days he sat in a hut at a weir and read a book, and the book is the reason the rest of this week is nine days long and the other things are not.

---

Nine gauges on a river of that size. Nine posts, cut and painted, set along nine miles of it, and the last one is above the place where the water is standing in the fields and it was put there nine years ago by a man who was laughed at, and there is a second line of posts below the weir and those are older and those are the ones that matter.

Nine keepers. Nine books. Because there are nine gauges and a reading is a reading of a post, and a woman of thirty-eight can walk from one to the next in a morning and a man with a cart cannot, and that is why there are nine.

And the book in the hut at the weir is hers, and she has read the weir post and the Sallow foot twice a day since the first sowing eleven years ago, and she has read nothing else in this book, and the other seven are seven other books in seven other huts in seven other villages, and nobody has ever copied any of the nine into the same book, and nobody in this county has ever asked to see any of them.

He said the number out loud when he had it, and made her say it again.

"Eight thousand and thirty-one lines."

"Eight thousand and thirty-one lines," said Thessa Wend. "Two lines a day, twice a day, since the first sowing, and one for the day I started, which was a day I did not know was the first one. **That is all it is. It is not a register and it is not a roll and there is no office in this county that has ever seen it, and I have never once been asked for a page of it, and I have wanted that since the first year.**"

"By whom," said Aren Kest.

"That is not a question anybody has ever asked me," she said, "and you are the first person who has asked me anything in eleven years, and I am not going to answer it here, and I have told you the number and the place and that is a bigger gift than this county has given anybody in a long time and you will do with it what you came to do."

Then she put the book on the bench and turned it so he could see it, which nobody had ever done, and it is not a clerk's hand. It is a small, quick, slightly left-sloping hand that gets worse in the wet, and the first four hundred lines are neat and the last thousand are not, and there is a place about a third of the way in where a whole season is written smaller than the rest because she had been ill.

And there is a place in the middle of it, about nine lines, that is in a different hand and is a different kind of entry entirely, and he found it and she let him find it, which is a decision and not an accident.

Nine lines, a different hand, an older paper, and they are the only nine lines in eleven years in that book that are not a reading.

*Of the low week: the ditch below the Sallow foot was cut in the week of the low water by two men and a boy and the water came up in the night and the field at the corner was under it to the third row and I was told in the morning that the ditch had not been cut and that it had been a bank that gave. I did not see it cut and I did not see it not cut and I was not in the county and I was nobody's man and nobody told me anything and I wrote nothing. **Whoever reads this: there is a week in this book that is not in the book. It is the one I am not there for. I have left the space and I have left the space for eleven years.***

"You have kept a gap," said Aren Kest.

"I have kept a gap for eleven years and I have never once been asked about it and nobody in this county knows there is one, and if you tell anybody in that room over the tithe barn that the gauge at the weir has a gap in it, they will move it, and they will move it to a post nearer the water, and it will be a better post and it will be eleven years of somebody else's back, and **a gauge that is read twice a day by the man whose land it is under is not a gauge, it is a thing he has agreed with.**"

**That took two minutes and it was eleven years, and she said it in the tone of a woman reading out a figure she has read out before and never had anybody ask her for, and he did not ask her the question, and the not asking was the hardest thing he did that day, and he did not do it for a reason, he did it because a man who has read a condition may not turn it into a reason, and that is a thing he learned in a room over a weigh-house and not on a bank.**

And the council has never looked at it. Not once, in eleven years. The council meets in a room over a tithe barn and argues about a boundary and the boundary is a ditch, and the ditch has a keeper, and the keeper has a book, and the council has never asked for the book, and the reason is not that the council thinks the keeper is careless: the reason is that in a county where a boundary is made of water, the man who holds the water and the man who holds the oath have been the same person for a hundred and forty years, and asking a ditchman for his book would be asking a man to show his own account of a thing he is also the judge of, and no council in a county like this has ever been willing to do that, and this one has not either, and it is not cowardice. It is a hundred and forty years of habit, and the habit is the only thing anybody in that room has.

And the fifth day was the day he was given the thing that changed what he came for, and it was given to him by accident, by a woman of about sixty on three hearths, in a doorway, in about four minutes, while somebody else was going past.

The lowest village is called Sallow Marsh and it has been in the same four villages as the white ground since before the oldest man there was born, and about sixty households of it are on the roll, and the roll is the council's roll and the roll is a list of households and a roll of a county is the only roll in this case that anybody in the world is likely to hold in their head without a book.

She is not on the oath.

Not because anybody struck her. Because the oath is renewed in a field in the fourth week of the rolling, in public, and it is renewed by the men who hold the water, because a boundary here is a channel and a boundary you cannot dig is not a boundary, and the men who hold the water are the ditchmen, and the ditchmen are forty men, and she is on three hearths and she does not hold water and she has never held water in her life, and there is no form anywhere in this county that says a woman may hold a ditch.

And when he said the word oath she said: "Which of them asked me?"

"Asked you what?"

"**Nothing,"** said the woman of about sixty. "That is what I am telling you. Nobody asked me anything. I have lived on this ground forty-one years and I have three hearths and four of them were in the water last year, and I have never been asked what I swore, because I never swore anything, and I would tell you if you asked, and nobody has."

She looked at the white field through the doorway and then at him.

"You are the man off the road," she said. "The one they sent for. **You will go home and you will write it down and you will say a woman of about sixty was on three hearths and was not on the oath. And that is all that will be said, and it will be said in a room where there is nobody from here, and in nine years a man will read it and think he knows something.**"

"I have not written anything down," said Aren Kest.

"You will," she said, "and I am not asking you not to. I am telling you what it will be. **Nobody here has ever been asked anything, and the only one of us who has ever had a stranger in a doorway did not get a question either. She got a number.**"

And that night he wrote down the two things he had come four hundred miles for, and he wrote them in the fair part of a book that is not a clerk's hand and is not his either, on a bench in a hut at a weir, with a lamp, and the woman who keeps it was not in the room and would not have let him use her book if she had been, and he copied the two lines out on to his own page and put the lamp out.

*Nine gauges. Nine books. Eight thousand and thirty-one lines in one of them, twice a day, eleven years, in a hand that is not a clerk's. Nobody has ever asked for a page. The council has never looked at it. It is the only account of this county's water that exists and it is a claim, because a claim is a thing nobody can be put in front of, and a record is a thing a person can be contradicted out of, and there is no person in it.*

*And about sixty households of the four white villages are on the roll and not on the oath, and the oath is renewed by the men who hold the water, and the woman on three hearths has never been asked what she swore because she never swore anything, and nobody has ever asked her.*

And the honest third line, which is the one a notebook is for, and which he wrote and did not improve:

*Eleven years. Twice a day. Eight thousand and thirty-one lines. And a bridgewright can see the mechanism in a day and has, and it took him a day and a half, and the woman who has the eleven years has not been asked for them once, and if you were to ask her for them she would have to be asked, and being asked is the whole of it, and being asked has not happened, and it is going to have to happen from a person, and a person can be asked, and she has been waiting eleven years to be.*

He put the lamp out and the water was up over the second of the lower posts at the weir, and the gauge was read twice a day and had been read twice a day since the first sowing, and nobody had ever asked to see it, and he had three more days in that county and one question to ask and it was not the one he had come for.

**And on the sixth day, before the light, the woman of thirty-eight came down the bank to the fourth step again, and he had not asked for her, and she had come, and neither of them mentioned that.**

"I have thought about one thing since the weir," she said, "and I am going to say it to you and then I am not going to say it again, and you may do what you like with it and I have not asked you for anything and I am not going to.

"**That book is mine. It has my hand in it and my mother's before me and I keep it in a hut because a hut is where a person keeps a thing that is theirs. If it goes into a book in your county it stops being a thing that can be contradicted and becomes a thing that is true, and a thing that is true does not need a person to be standing behind it, and the day it is that, the first man in your county that wants a different number will move a post.**"

"Nobody in that room wants a different number," said Aren Kest. "I have been in this county five days and there is not one person in it that has wanted a number out of anybody."

"Then you are a stranger and I am telling you what a stranger is," said Thessa Wend, "and a stranger is a man who will be right in about four years and by then it will not matter that he was right. **I am not refusing you. I am telling you what it is you are asking me for, and you are asking me for a thing that cannot be unasked for, and I have watched men in this county sign a thing in a field because a man read it out and it sounded like the same words twice.**"

Then she went up the bank, and the last four of the nine days were the terms, and the terms refused him, and the refusal was hers, and it took four seconds, and it was asked by a woman who had never been asked anything.
