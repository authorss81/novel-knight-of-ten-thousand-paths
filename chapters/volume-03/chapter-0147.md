# Chapter 0147

Nobody died.

There are four hundred people in this county and about thirty of them were on a bank in the dark with a river coming over a thing five yards long, and a boy of fifteen was up the bank at the second hour with a lamp in each hand and no instruction of any kind, and eleven men came down the north approach in the fourth hour with a cart of elm off the guild's own stack that they had no business cutting in a flood, and none of them came back on it. The number is thirty and the reason it is thirty is a ditch, a bank, nine days of work, and a man on a lid.

That has to be said first and it has to be said plainly, because the rest of that night is about one man and everything else in this account is about a place that has never had a flood in it in living memory and had one in the seventh rolling and has now had water over a boundary it made itself nine days before.

At the fourth hour Thessa Wend came up the bank.

She had been at the weir until the second hour and then she had come up because the boy had told her that a man was out on the lid and had said the word *sill* twice, and she came up with her boots in her hand and a lamp and a piece of paper and she did not go out onto the span, and nobody asked her to.

She came as far as the third line of lines on the far side and she put the lamp on a stone and she watched him for about a quarter of an hour and she did not speak to him once in that quarter of an hour.

He changed his weight at the fourth hour. That is in her line and it is in the guild's book in different words and neither of those two places is the place it matters. He had been standing on the near bay for about four hours with his weight through the heel of his right foot and his hands on the rail, and at the fourth hour he took the weight off the right and put it into the left and stayed there, and he did not change it back, and the reason he did not change it back is that the right hip had gone somewhere it had not been and he had about nine hours' habit and one leg and the rail.

She watched him do it and she saw that he did not change back and she understood what it was, because she is a woman who reads a fixed thing on a river for eleven years and a load point is a fixed thing, and what she saw was a load point move.

She went back down to the hut, which is a quarter of a mile and which she did in about nine minutes in the dark with a lamp, and she did not speak to anybody on the way and she has never told anybody what she did in those nine minutes and it is in her own book with a book-mark in it and she showed it to nobody, and it is the second line in eight thousand and thirty-one lines that is not a reading of a post.

*Fourth hour. A man on the lid at the trough moved his weight off the near side and did not move it back and the water was over the deck both sides and his hand did not go to the rail, which it should have. I do not know what that means. I have read this book for eleven years twice a day and this is the second line in it that is about a man and not about a post, and the first one is about a boy in the water and the ninth, and the ninth is the bell, and I will not have the number of times I rang that bell in a book.*

She bookmarked it at the ninth line of the page so that she would find it, and a book-mark in a private book is a thing that cannot be served and cannot be produced and cannot be copied, and she has wanted a reading by somebody who does not own the weir since the first year, and she has had two, and neither of them lasted longer than a night, and one of them is on a lid four hundred yards from her hut and will be gone by the light.

At the fifth hour the near sill moved.

It moved about two inches toward the ditch and it did it in under a minute and it made no sound at all, which is the thing about a sill that goes, and thirty men on a bank heard a thing they have all heard once and none of them could have named.

The near bay went with it, four inches, and came back, and the entire of it was in about a second and a half.

He was on the near bay. He had a bar through the far rail and a line round the far sill and he had been using them in the way a man uses a bar and a line on a lid, which is to keep the deck in one piece so that the water has one way to go, and when the sill went he had about a second and a half to decide whether to be on the far bay or the near one, and the far bay was where the bar and the line were, and the far sill is the one that was sound.

He went to the far bay and he took the bar and the line with him, and in the second and a half it took to do it the near bay dropped the other two inches and caught on the mortise of the far bay, and the drop went through the heel of the near bay and into the right leg, and the right leg went sideways.

What held him was the rail. He had both hands on the far rail and he kept them there, and a man who lets go of a rail in a night flood is a man in the water, and he did not let go of it, and the whole of the load went through his hands and his right hip and his right leg, and about half of it went through the leg.

After that the span was a lid with one bay hanging off the far one. It was a bad structure. It was going to be a bad structure for about three more hours, and it took the whole night river the whole three hours, and the far sill held, and the water went across the deck and off the end and never once found the four inches where the near bay hung, and that is not luck, that is what a lid does.

Nobody on the bank came out. Ferrow called the far sill at the fifth hour and the sixth hour and the seventh hour, and every time he called it, the thirty men on that bank put gravel on the far lip, and the far lip held, and the man at the sluice said afterward that a man can be a bridgewright and be the best in a county and still be half a foot short on a dark morning, and that the reason the span did not go is that a man who has decided what he is throwing off in the night has already thrown it off.

He stayed on it until the seventh hour. That is nine hours, from the tenth hour of the last day of the last week of the last rolling of the year until the seventh hour of the morning, and the eleven calls are in the guild's book and every one of them was about four seconds early.

The water went down at about the sixth hour and a half and it went down the way it came up, which is at once, and by the seventh hour the trough was a trough and the ditch at the head was running and the span was a span with a bay hanging off it and a foot of gravel on the north lip and a man standing on the far end of it with his hands on a rail.

Then he had to get off it, and getting off it took about four minutes and four minutes is a long time.

The right leg would not take him. That is what a leg does when it has been the outside of a lever for a second and a half in the dark: the hip is the joint and the hip is what goes, and what a man gets afterwards is a leg that will do about a third of what it did and a hip that will do about a half and a great deal of both that cannot be done at all until it has been done four or five times in a day.

He got himself back along the far bay, which is the end that was sound, and down the far abutment onto the gravel, and across four yards of wet ground on two legs and one rail, and the man at the sluice met him at the ditch side with a bar because he had been told to stand there with a bar and not to use it unless he was told to use it, and he was not told to use it, and he did not use it, and he put his shoulder under the man's arm at the ditch and walked him up four feet of bank without one word.

The man at the drawknife went home with the rest of them at the start of the rolling and is somewhere between here and a fen town by now. There was nobody in that county whose job it was and there was nobody whose name it would have been.

The minute-man came up the bank at the seventh hour with the book under his arm, which he has never done in nineteen years and which he did not do by accident, and he read three entries into it on a wet bank in front of thirty men who were too tired to go home.

“The eleventh call, the seventh hour, against no person: a man stood on a lid at the trough from the tenth hour of the last day to the seventh hour of the morning, and the near bay is hanging four inches and the far bay is sound and the water has gone down and nobody is in the water. And that is all of it, and there is no name in it, and there is no hour on the man, and I am not going to look for one, and if anybody in ninety years wants to know what happened on that lid they will find a book that cannot be served and eleven calls that were four seconds early at the outside and they will not find a man, and that is the best that this county can do and it is more than the county four hundred miles off can do with everything.”

Then the last thing, and he said it to the bank and not to a room, and the room was not there, and that is why it worked.

“Entered: that the road is given, that the council has not asked him for it and did not know it was being given, and that a fair copy of it is on a board under the stair in this room, and that the two names on that fair copy are the only two things in this county that a man can be shown and be found by, and that a county which has spent nineteen years being careful about the roll has in nine rollings put two names on a board in the one book in this kingdom where nobody can get at them.

“Entered: and that I have been asked no question about it by the man concerned, and that in the second week of the eighth rolling I asked him a question in this room and he answered it, and that this time I did not ask, and that the not asking is not entered against him and is not entered at all, and there is a woman in this county who has taught me what the difference is between a rule and a use.”

He shut the book and went down the bank, and the rain started again about an hour later, and it was an ordinary rain, and a man in this county who had scraped ground for forty years said that a rain in the morning after a night like that is a good sign and a rain in the night after a day like that is a bad one, and that this one was in the morning.

The last hour of the last day of the last week of the year is the hour a man with a limp spends sitting down.

He sat on an upturned barrow in the gateway behind the tithe barn with his leg out and the bar he had put through a rail at the fifth hour across his knees, and about nine people came and stood about four feet away and did not say anything, which is a thing a county does at the end of a year.

Munn came last and she said one sentence, and it is the only one she has said in nine rollings that she has not said in a room, and she said it to him and not to the council and not to the minute-man.

“Nine men said true words in a field in the fourth week of the twelfth rolling for the first time in a hundred and forty years and I stood in that field and did not tell them,” she said. “And a stranger stood on a lid for nine hours last night so that a boundary I have kept for nineteen years would still be a boundary in the morning. And you are going to be a hundred miles off before the light is properly up, and there is nobody in this county who can tell you that you did the second thing for the first one, and I am not going to be the person who tells you, because I have been the person who tells this room things for fifty-five years and I am tired of it and my grandmother's name is on a stone and it will still be on it in nine years whether I say anything or not.”

Then she went up the outside stair and did not look back, and he did not ask her what her grandmother's name was, and nobody in that county knows, and the name is on a stone, and the stone is standing in a field with nine pegs in a new ditch beside it.

At about the third hour he wrote in his own book, and this is the entry that was written on the far side of a line on faith and is checked on the near side in the morning, and he wrote it in the register he uses in this county, which is a month this county does not use and a day nobody here keeps.

*Went out on the fifth of a month this county does not use, at the fourth hour, on a line made up in nine minutes with the hour against each of the nine. The office's printed figure is three weeks. The days are a hundred and seventeen. The error is ninety-six. The error is about four fifths of the days and not of the warning, and the warning is not what the error is over, and the path is nowhere in it. The printed figure has not changed and the office cannot say whose figure it is and I cannot either, and nobody in this county has ever seen it. I am writing this on faith on the far side of a line and I will check it on the near side and if the two figures are not the same number then I have put a month in it twice and I will have earned that.*

*And the error is in me. It was eleven days the other way when I came in, with the warning longer than the absence, and it is ninety-six days the other way now, and what changed between them is the work and not the road, and I wrote that down two volumes ago in a book in this county and I have now proved it twice and I would like it entered that I proved it the second time on a lid with one leg.*

He shut the book on it and went down to the lane.

The line was made up in about nine minutes, the way a line is made up, and the hour went against each of the nine.

The hand at this end was the guild, and the hand at that end was a yard of a fen county four hundred miles off that went out of this county at the start of the rolling with ninety-six bushels of salt in a handcart and four men in it, and the witness at that end was the same yard and nobody in this county knows whether it has entered anything yet and there is no instrument in this kingdom that could tell them, and he did not ask, and asking is not a thing you may do about a road you have just given away.

The witness at this end was chosen by this end, as the fifth of the county's own terms requires, and the minute-man asked who and the room said nothing for a count of nine, and then the woman of about sixty on three hearths said, from the sill, in the voice she uses when she is not going to be asked twice:

“I'll hold it,” she said, “and I will do it properly and I will do it in front of people, and you will all hear it and you will all remember it, and there is not one book in this county that can produce me afterwards and that is why it is worth doing.”

Nobody improved on that and the minute-man wrote it and read it back and the entry in that book for the thirteenth rolling is the last one of the year and the shortest of the nine rollings, and it is eight words long, and it carries the hour.

He went down the line at the fourth hour on a day this county keeps, and the two hands were a guild and a yard and the two witnesses were a man who scraped ground for forty years and a woman on three hearths who has never once been asked what she swore, and one line in a book four hundred miles off in a county that is not on any map may never be opened, and that is the arrangement and not a failure, and the last two volumes of this case have been about one thing, which is that you do not call a road and you stand at the end of it.

The thirteenth rolling of the Hearth Marches is a year and a rolling, counted from the first sowing, four weeks to a rolling, and it is over, and the first sowing of the next one is within a week, and the salt under four villages is still a foot and a half down and will be for four years, and nothing anybody did in that county in a year and a rolling will make it come down this year.
