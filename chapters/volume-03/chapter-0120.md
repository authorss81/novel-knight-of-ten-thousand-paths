# Chapter 0120

The sixth day he was taken up two flights of a stair into a room over a tithe barn, and there were eleven people in it, and all eleven of them were that council and all eleven had been in a guild hall over a wharf in a fen county twenty-five days before, and a bench that argues about a boundary was sitting on a bench and was not a court and had no writ to answer and no bell to ring and no Crown's schedule anywhere in this kingdom that carried it.

**He put his own terms on the table at about the sixth hour, and there were four of them, and he read them out, and three of them were the Marches' own six lines with the price and the door left in, and the fourth was his.**

*One. The line is made on the six lines and on nothing else.*
*Two. A hand at each end and a witness at each end, and the witness at that end is chosen by that end.*
*Three. A day, when that end names one, and the line is opened on it and on no other day in that year, and if that end does not name a day the line does not open, and I will not attempt it and I will not call it anything else.*
*Four. **And the water is read.** The nine gauges on the Water are read twice a day by nine keepers in nine villages, and one of those books has eight thousand and thirty-one lines in it and has never been asked for by anybody, and a true copy of the nine readings of a month is to sit in the line's book at this end, and anybody in this county that comes up two flights of a stair may read it, and a keeper of records in this county is a person and not an office and I have told her nothing and she does not know.*

And the fourth one broke the second of the six, and everybody in the room knew it inside about a minute, and nobody said so for a while.

And in the middle of that while a woman of about thirty-four with a slate on her knees, who is the clerk of that council and is not one of the nine and is the only other person in the room who writes things down, asked the room a question nobody had asked, and the room had asked each other questions for a hundred and forty years.

"Which of you is paying for the rider," she said.

Nobody in the room knew. That was the answer, and it took about eleven seconds to establish and it is the shortest true thing said in that room in nine years. The council has no money. The guild has no money. The men who renew the stone are the nine men who have money, and they are nine men out of about forty who hold water, and every time a thing has to be paid for in that county it arrives at that table as a question with nowhere to go, and it has been arriving at that table that way since before anybody alive was born, and the guild has asked the bench for a wage once and been refused in a full room, and the man who asked for it is the first hand of the guild and is in the room, and he told the delegation about it in this county a month ago and he has not mentioned it since.

"A stranger's rider," said the woman with the slate, "to his county and back, and a man has to go or a boy goes because a bag does not travel alone on that road. And it will not be paid twice. **And if it is paid once it will be paid every year, and in nine years there will be a line in that minute about a rider, and nobody will remember what it was for.**"

"Nobody will remember what it was for," said Aren Kest, "because a minute is a book and a book keeps the what and throws away the why unless somebody stands in a room and says the why, and in this county the men who say the why are the men who renew the stone, and they are nine, and they have a hundred and forty years of practice at saying a why in a field where everybody can be asked about it afterwards."

A column with a woman's name at the head of it is a name. The second line is: *no name is to be read out on the far side of the line by anybody from the north, and no name is to be written in the line's book at the north end.* He was not offering to read a name out to anybody. He was offering to put a name in a book four hundred miles off in a county that had a room with a door on a lane in it and anybody in the county could come up and read, and he had made no promise about names because he had not thought about names at all since the ninth of the month before because he had a bag of salt and a sheet of paper and a tool bag and a bell he did not ring.

---

And the man of sixty with a spliced line across the back of his right hand said one thing and it was about the third term and it was the only thing in the room that was about the road.

"The third one is ours," said Coll Ferrow, "and it is the best thing anybody has put in this room, and I want it minuted that I said so, because in four rollings somebody will tell my nineteen men that the stranger wanted a thing off us and I will not be here."

And the woman of fifty-five said the other thing, and she is Delya Munn, and she renews the stone, and she is the person in that county who will not have it lifted.

"No," she said. "Not the fourth, and not the water. **The book is not to be read by anybody from the north and it is not to be copied and it is not to be sent, and I will give you the reason and it is a hundred and forty years long and it is not mine.**"

"Go on," said Aren Kest.

"A boundary in this county is a ditch. That is not a figure of speech, it is what the word means. A man who holds a ditch is the bound and a man who holds the water is the ditchman and they have been one word for a hundred and forty years, because a boundary here is a channel and you cannot argue about a channel with a stick. **And the only thing anybody in that countryside has ever had that does not need an office to be true is a promise kept out of doors in front of the people it is about, by men who are still alive and can be asked what they swore.** You have offered to take the water out of my hand and put it in a book. And in nine years the book is in a press in a county four hundred miles off, and a man in that county that has a filing system has never seen it and does not know of it, and I am told you have not told her. **You have offered to give the only durable thing we have to a keeper. And you are not a keeper. Nobody in this room is.**"

Nobody said anything, and the man who keeps the minute of that council put his pen down, and Ferrow looked at the floor, and nobody contradicted her, because she was right and everybody in the room could see the shape of the rightness of it and nobody could see the bottom of it.

And then the woman of thirty-eight of Sallow Marsh, who is not on that council and has never been on it and is in that room because she reads the water, asked one question, and it took about four seconds, and it is the refusal, and there was no vote.

"How many?" she said.

"How many what."

"How many people in your county are going to read it."

And he had the honest answer ready and it took him about a second and a half to find it, and the finding of it is the reason the room turns on it.

"I do not know," said Aren Kest. "There is a woman in a room over a weigh-house who makes copies for a living and she is the best I have ever worked with, and she does not know this county exists, and I have not told her, and I have not written to her since the second of a month that I have a name for, and if you are asking me to promise you a number I cannot promise you a number and I would rather say that in a room than write it on a sheet and have it read out in a hall in a fen county as though a man from here had said it."

**And the woman of thirty-eight said: "Then that is the answer, and you have said it, and I did not have to argue."**

And Coll Ferrow said: "The road is refused."

**"The road is refused on your terms and accepted on ours," said the man who keeps the minute, writing, "and the road was never agreed and is not agreed and the line is not made, and enter it as a fact and not as a defeat, and enter the hour."**

And then a thing happened in that room that a man from the north had not been in a room for in four years and a half, and it happened because nobody in that county had the English for it and had had to go and find some.

**They had no word for refusing.**

The word they had was the word for a thing that got lost. The man who keeps the minute said so in about nine seconds, in his own dialect, and a woman at the back of the room gave him the other half of it, which is that a thing that got lost can be looked for, and a thing that has been refused cannot, and the whole of their law is that you may ask for a thing back.

"So that is what has happened," said Thessa Wend. "**The road got lost. And in nine years, in a county four hundred miles off, there will be a man with a book who says that a county refused a road, and he will be telling the truth, and nobody there will be able to look for it.**"

And on the eighth day the stone was sworn, and he was not in the field, and that was the first time the fourth of the six had been kept in the four weeks that anybody in that county could remember, and it was kept because a stranger was in the county, and a woman of fifty-five came and found him in a gateway in the market town and told him the hour of it and did not ask him to come.

He went on the ninth day. A long field, a boundary, a trough of stone in the ground with water in it, and a stone on the low side of the trough with a cut on it that men renew in public, and the cut had been renewed so many times in a hundred and forty years that the face of the stone is not a face any more, it is a palimpsest a foot across.

And he was taken there by the woman who will not have it lifted, and she walked ahead of him the whole way and did not speak for about a mile, and at the trough she stopped and waited for him to catch up, which is a thing a person does who is going to tell a man something and wants him standing.

"It is not lifted," she said, "and it is not going to be, and I have been asked to have it lifted twice and I have said no twice, and the second time I said no out loud in this room in front of eleven people and I put the reason in the minute, and the reason is that a thing a hundred and forty years old that nobody can lift is the only piece of evidence this county has ever had that anybody agreed to anything, and if it goes into a cart it goes into a cart with a hole in it, and I do not know what a judge would be able to do with a stone in a cart and neither does the judge."

"You are the only person I have met in four years and a half who has refused a thing in a form that would survive being written down," said Aren Kest.

**"I have refused it in a form that will survive being written down," said Delya Munn, "and I have had the same two men bring me a different reason each time, once that it was in the way of a drain and once that the ground was soft, and neither of those was the reason and both of them were better than my reason, and I have said no twice and I will say no a third time in the same words, and the fourth man will come with a reason that is better than mine and I will find that I have none."**

And the ditch was eleven yards off the trough and had been eleven yards off the trough for a long time, and the trough is the boundary, and he stood in the ditch with a pole and put it against the downstream bank and the bank went over.

A hand's width. Not a collapse. A bank that has been cut back on the same line, by the same men, in the same month, at the same stage of the water, for so long that the topsoil has gone and what is left is the clay, and the clay is what a bank of a hand's width a year goes to, and there is a hundred and forty years of it on both sides of that trough.

He said nothing.

He said nothing because of the third of the six — *the north is not to send anybody who can be asked to come and look* — and because a man who has been in a county five days and has a tool bag is exactly the man the third line is about, and because he is a bridgewright and not a ditchman, and because the woman who has eleven years of numbers has never been asked for them once, and if he said the thing about the bank in a gateway in this county then the one thing that county has which the north does not have would be gone inside a day, and it would be his, and it would be in a book, and it would be a thing a keeper could not contradict because the man who said it would be four hundred miles away and would not be able to be asked.

He wrote it in the book with the hour against it and the honest second half, and the second half is this:

*Seen, not told. Not said in this county. Not to be said in this county by me, and if it is said in this county it is to be said by the woman who keeps the book at the weir, or by one of the forty men who hold the water, and not by a man with a bag.*

**And on the ninth night he wrote the figure, and it is the first one he has written since the last crossing, and he wrote it before he knew whether it was true, and he knew that while he was writing it, and he wrote that down as well.**

*Crossing A. The first one I have been able to hold in my head.*

*Nine days on this side, counted off the sun twice and off the shadow of the weir hut once. The sun is the same sun, and I have been under it nine days and out of my own county the same nine days, and there is nobody in that county with any idea what that means, because the sun does not change country.*

*Warning, printed at the foot of the terms in an office's hand, in a smaller type: a road held, three weeks. Twenty-one days. It has not moved in a year and the office cannot say whose figure it is and I am not going to say that in a room a third time in a month.*

*Ten days gone, against a printed twenty-one, is **minus eleven days**, and the warning is longer than the absence, and it is the first crossing in this case that has come back inside the printed warning, and it is the only one in this case I can hold in my head. The error is the absence and not the interval, and the interval is nowhere in the sum: it is nine, and nine is not a number that can be set against twenty-one, and that is the whole of what is wrong with this page and the whole of what is right about it.*

*And the ten is not known yet. It will be known in about ten days, if I come up in about ten days, and that is the half of this entry I am writing on faith, and I am going to write it anyway, because a man who waits to write the figure is a man who has had the answer first and learned nothing afterwards.**

***A good result teaches a man nothing. A bad one teaches him everything.*** The last one was eighty-eight days gone against a printed twenty-one, and the error was sixty-seven days, and the error was about three quarters of the absence. The one before that was twenty-six gone against twenty-one, and the error was five days, and about a quarter of the absence. **And the ratio is the thing and not the figure.** The figure moves. The ratio is the thing, and it is a quarter and then three quarters and now more than one, and it is not a constant and it is not going to be a constant, and a man who writes down a good figure has learned nothing at all, and a man who writes down a bad figure has learned the shape of his own error and can do something about the shape. **And this one I cannot do anything about, because there is nothing wrong with it.**

*And the half I cannot check at all is the half that matters more, and it is not about me, it is about the calendar I came out of: a rolling here is four weeks, and a month in the county I came out of is thirty days, and there is not one of those in the other. So the nine on this side and the ten on that side are not two halves of one figure. They are two numbers in two systems with nothing common between them, and no paper will make them comparable, and I am not going to try it again, because I did it twice before and both times it came out as an arithmetic with no answer in it and both times I threw the paper away, and both times I went on looking for a method, and there is not a method and the not having one is the finding. **Nine days inside ten is something a man can hold in his head, and a man who can hold a thing in his head stops looking for what makes it so.**

He put the lamp out. The water was up over the second of the lower posts at the weir, and the book in the hut had two more lines on it that day, and the council in the room over the tithe barn had not looked at it, and would not, and the road had got lost, and the stone had been sworn in a field he was not in, and a woman of about sixty on three hearths was not on the oath and had never been asked what she swore.

He had one question left and it was not the one he had come for, and it was the only one of the four hundred miles that could not be done by a man with a bag.
