# Chapter 0121

The room over the tithe barn smelled of warm straw under cold boards. Light came through a narrow slot at the back and fell across two door leaves on trestles where eleven people sat with a lamp lit in the middle of the afternoon, because the barn kept one dark end whatever the hour outside.

Aren stood by the slot with his bag at his feet. He had been in the county twelve days. He had a notebook in his coat and a pole he had borrowed and returned, and he had not asked this council for anything since the refusal on the sixth day.

They began with the boundary, because they always began with it. A man farming to the trough line at Cold Pit had taken a new plough to the edge, and the new iron bit deeper than the old by half an inch, and half an inch would not show in a season and would show in nine years.

The man who keeps the minute read the entry from the last sitting. Delya Munn corrected him on the season. Coll Ferrow stood against the wall because he always stood, sixty years old, a pale line across the back of his right hand, nineteen men somewhere beyond these walls and no wage to hold them with.

Ferrow said the remedy was to set a mark a hand back from the trough and let the man plough to the mark. Munn said the plough stayed where it was and the boundary stayed where it was. The table argued it the way a bench argues a road, point by point, without heat, and Aren listened and wrote nothing, because a stranger who writes during a boundary dispute becomes a record of it.

When the boundary was entered as a fact and not as a decision, Munn put both hands flat on the door leaves. The room settled.

“There is a man in this county,” she said, looking at no one in particular, “and the council will speak of him once and then go back to work.”

The minute-man lifted his pen.

“This council will not give a person from outside this county any standing in the matter of the Water,” Munn said. “It will not be asked to do so. It will not accept a thing done in its name.”

She said it plainly, without raising her voice, and the eleven heard it as they had heard it once before in a hall over a wharf four hundred miles off. That first time it had been a refusal of command before any question had been put. This time it was closer and quieter, and it went into the book with the hour against it.

Ferrow spoke from the wall. “I have nineteen men and no wage, and I said the same sentence to a bench in the north and was told I was right to be refused. I would like it observed that I said it first.”

“It is observed,” the minute-man said, writing. “It is entered as a fact.”

The woman with the slate, thirty-four, clerk of the council though not one of the eleven, asked who would pay for a rider to the north and back. The council had no money. The guild had no money. The nine men who renewed the stone had money, and they were nine out of forty who held water, and every charge in this county arrived at this table as a question with nowhere to go.

She worked the figure on the slate in front of them. A man at four pence a day and a horse at eight pence a day was twelve pence a day together. Sixteen days on the road was sixteen times twelve pence. She chalked the line and made them watch her add it, because no figure went into that book unfooted.

Twelve pence for the first four days was forty-eight pence. Twelve for the next four was forty-eight more, which made ninety-six. Twelve for the next four was forty-eight more, which made one hundred and forty-four. Twelve for the last four was forty-eight more, which made one hundred and ninety-two pence. She said the total aloud. One hundred and ninety-two pence for a rider there and back in a good week.

“And it does not fall on the four white villages,” Munn said at once. “Enter that. It does not fall on the sixty households that are on the roll and not on the oath, either, and enter the reason, because in nine years somebody will read charity where there was none. It does not fall on them because this council has never asked those people for anything, and I will not be the first to do it.”

The slate woman wrote it.

Ferrow then stated the guild’s terms, because the council had refused outside command and had to say what it would accept instead. He stated them standing, in the same words he had used in the north, and the room heard that they were better than anything the north had brought.

The water in a ditch was somebody’s job, and had been since his grandfather, and would be after he was dead, and was not a county’s mood. A stranger’s labour was taken at the guild’s rate and paid in the county’s coin at the crossing and not in goods. The county’s coin was salt, by the county’s own figure of nine pence the bushel, scraped in the low weeks and weighed by a man paid by the measure.

The council accepted the terms as the terms it would work under. The minute-man entered the acceptance as a fact. No vote was taken, because the chamber did not vote on facts.

Before the lamp was trimmed Munn set the next morning’s work. The whole bench would walk to the lowest field of Sallow Marsh with the guild’s two and the woman who read the water and the boy who read with her, and the stranger if he chose to come. They would stand in the field and look at the ground, and come back up and say what they had seen. It was nine years since the room had looked at anything together.

Aren said he would come if he was welcome. Munn said the walk was not a sitting and any person in the county might walk. That was the end of the sitting, and the minute carried the hour and no name in the decision line, because there had been no decision.

He walked down the stair behind the others with the smell of straw in his coat, thinking of the ditch eleven yards off the trough and the bank gone over on the same line, and of the one hundred and ninety-two pence nobody had agreed to pay, and of the sixty households nobody had agreed to charge.

The yard below was loud with evening work. The yard’s four men had laid out timber for a staging by the Water, squaring ends by lamp-light, speaking little. The man at the drawknife shaved a peg and tested it against a mortise with his thumb, frowning at the fit the way men frown at sums. Aren stopped to watch, because watching work was the one courtesy he could offer without becoming a document.

“You heard the sitting,” Aren said.

“We heard the boards,” the drawknife man said. “A room over straw carries.”

“Ferrow’s terms stand, then. Guild rate, county coin, at the crossing.”

“Aye. And the council’s refusal stands with them. No standing for outsiders in the Water.” He set the peg down. “Suits us. We came on the day the Marches named, on their road, under their hand. We are labour, not standing. A crew can be the best in a county and still be strangers, and that is a clean position.”

One of the other three, a younger man with tar on his sleeves, asked whether the rider money would come. Aren said the council had footed one hundred and ninety-two pence and assigned it to no one, which in his experience meant it would be argued three more times before it was paid. The younger man laughed without bitterness. Yard men knew the life of an unpaid figure.

They talked timber a while. The staging needed to stand in soft ground through the next rise. Aren showed them with his hands how he would spread the feet, and they showed him how the Marches ground differed, soft over hard clay that shifted under load. He learned more in twenty minutes with them than in two sittings overhead, and said so, and they accepted the compliment the way working men accept what is true.

Later, in his lodging above a storehouse, he wrote the sitting in his notebook with the hour against each entry. He wrote the boundary, the refusal, the guild’s terms, the rider figure footed line by line, and the walk set for the morning. He did not write the bank or the salt, because those belonged to the woman with the gauge column and to the field, and his book was not the place for them.

He sat long after the lamp burned low, hearing the Water move beyond the walls. Twelve days in the county, and the council had refused outside command a second time and meant it, and had said what it would accept instead, and what it accepted was better than anything he had carried in. That was the shape of the rolling so far, and it asked nothing of him except stillness, which was the one thing he had never learned to offer cheaply.
