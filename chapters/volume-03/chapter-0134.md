# Chapter 0134

It came in the wrong part of the year with a smell that four hundred people recognised and nobody had a name for, and it took about four hours, and all four of them were the sound of a bank.

Aren was on the staging at seven, because he was on the staging at seven, and the first thing he noticed was that the Water had stopped.

That is the fact about a river in spate. It does not rise gradually in anybody's experience. It rises and it stops and it stands there doing nothing at all, and every man who has ever worked a river knows that the stillness is not safety, it is the water deciding, and then it comes up over the bank in a hundred places at once rather than in one, because the bank is not one thing and a river does not come up where you are watching.

He had the sill for the north approach under his hand and the four men of the fen yard on the boards around it, and the boy with the pole came down the lane at the second hour with his boots in his hand and said, “It is doing the still thing.”

“I know. Go and tell Ferrow.”

“I told the boy at the weir and he is telling the woman and she is telling a man at a sluice and the man at a sluice is going to tell the guild and the guild will tell you in an hour.” The boy was fifteen and wet to the knee. “Is the still thing the bad thing?”

“The still thing is the last of the quiet,” said Aren, and the boy went, and did not tell anybody that he had been told a thing he did not understand, which is what a gauge boy is for.

Then he did the thing that made the next four hours what they were, and he has been asked about it since in a county four hundred miles off and in two rooms he has never stood in, and it is in his own book with the hour against it, and what he wrote was four words and no reason.

He wrote: *A man who is not asked is not to ask.*

Because it was coming, and the deck was above the old bend, and the deck was Ferrow's work on a four days' notice, and Ferrow had not told the council why and had told the four men of a fen yard a date and no reason, and if Aren went to the council and said the Water was about to come into the old bend, then the first thing the council would do was ask him how he knew, and the second thing would be to send for Ferrow, and the third thing would be that the man in the yard of four hundred miles who has been in this county seven rollings would have told this council a thing about this county's water that no man of this county had told them, and he had promised a yard and not a book.

He put the notebook in his coat and went and told the only person in the county who was entitled to know, which was a man of sixty at a staging two hundred yards down with nineteen men and a bar.

Ferrow was already moving when he got there and he was not moving because anybody had told him anything. He was moving because he was sixty and it was the second hour and there was something in the way the water was sitting that a man who has been on that bank since he was twenty-two can feel in his feet.

“Say it fast and say it once,” said Ferrow.

“The old bend. It is coming into the old bend and not staying in the present channel. I do not know how I know. I have been seven rollings in this county and I am telling you the water is coming and I am not able to give you a reason and I am not going to give you one that sounds like a reason.”

Ferrow looked at the water for four seconds. Then he said, “That is all I needed and I would have found it in an hour and I have saved an hour and that hour is the guild's and yours and not the county's, and I am going to spend it now and you are going to hold that deck with me, and afterwards you and I are going to have a conversation about the four words in your book and I am going to lose that argument and I want you to know that before I start.”

Then he was gone down the bank, and the next four hours were the guild's.

What the guild did in four hours is not a thing the county has ever had a name for, because it is not a thing, it is a sequence, and Ferrow had it in his head and not on a slate and could not have written it down, and he said so afterward to a stranger and meant it as an account of the whole method.

“First thing: the bed. There is a line of dead alders along the old bend, the oldest line of dead alders I have ever seen, and the youngest of them is older than the bound, and I have known about that line since I was twenty-two and I have never said it out loud to anybody and I have just said it out loud to you, and you may have it. Alders die when a bank goes over on them and the water goes off them, and a line of them is a bank that went over, all at once, in a year long before anybody remembers. So the bed is there. The bed is the only bed that Water has ever had up there, and it has been a mile away from where it has been running for a hundred and forty years, and there is a new bank between the two, and the new bank is a hundred and forty feet of stuff we threw up out of a trench three weeks ago, and there is nine feet of pond water standing on the low side of it.

“Second thing: the new bank is the weak part. Not because it is bad. Because it is new, and a bank that has not been through a winter is not a bank, it is a heap. So the Water does not have to beat the hundred and forty years. It has to beat three weeks and a wet month. And it would rather do it at four in the morning in the wrong part of the year, and it is going to do it this morning, and it is going to do it where the new bank is thinnest, which is at the downstream end, which is where I told you the ditch has been moving for a hundred and forty years, which is the same place where the new bank is the newest.

“Third thing: nothing upstream of the deck can be saved and nothing below the deck can be saved, and the only thing in four hours that can be done is to put the deck where the old bed is, and hold it, and let the Water go over it instead of round it, because a river in spate in a bed it has not had for a hundred and forty years will find that bed and go down it, and if we are not on it with a deck it will find it anyway and take the four villages in an afternoon.”

The guild got eleven men onto the causeway at the fourth hour and the four men of the fen yard and the stranger and the boy with the pole, and that was the whole crew, because the other eight were on the bank above the deck throwing up a temporary lip out of what was in the yard, and doing that with sacks of gravel and stone and no earthworks and no tools, which is a thing you do once in a life and which is not a trade.

Ferrow would not have the bar on the deck. He put the bar through the rail of the upstream side and made two men take it, and he would not have anybody on the deck who was not either holding a rail or holding the end of a line, and he said the reason out loud, and the reason was that a man who is helping and not holding is a man who is about to fall, and a man who is about to fall on a causeway in a spate takes the rail with him.

At the fifth hour the first of the Water came over the new bank at the downstream end, and it came over in a place about four hundred yards below the deck, and it did not look like much. That is the thing about a river finding a bed. It looks like a man going into a swimming bath. It looks like nothing at all, and then an hour later there is a mile of it moving where nothing was moving, and the sound changes, and the sound changing is the thing that tells you, because the sound of water over new ground is higher and more broken than the sound of water in a channel it knows, and every man on that deck heard it inside about two minutes and none of them could have said what they had heard.

At the sixth hour the sound of it came up the bank to the staging and the four men of the fen yard felt it in the soles of their feet, which is a thing that happens about a quarter of a mile from a river that has started to move in a bed it has not been in for a hundred and forty years, and the man at the drawknife said one word, “She,” about the Water, which he had been doing all week and which nobody in the county has ever corrected him on.

At the seventh hour a man came up the bank on a horse that had no business on a bank like that and said that the four lowest villages were under water at the bottom end and that a man in a doorway at Sallow Marsh had said the water was coming up the lanes from the ditch side and not down, which was true, and which told Ferrow at once that the trough had filled, because a ditch that fills from the top sends the water backwards, and if the trough is full then the trough is the top, and if the trough is the top then the boundary has stopped being a boundary and there is no boundary in this county tonight.

“Down,” said Ferrow, to everybody, and everybody went, and the deck was held with eleven men for one hour and forty minutes and then the bank went in on the upstream side four feet from the upstream rail and the deck moved three feet sideways in about the time it takes to say a word and the four men of the fen yard went down with it and the boy with the pole went down with it and came up eleven yards away in about the time it takes a man to say a thing twice.

Nobody was hurt on the deck. That is not because they were good at it. It is because Ferrow had put the bar through the rail, and the rail is what the deck turned about, and a deck that turns about its rail moves eleven yards in one direction and not four in two, and eleven yards is the difference between a man in a doorway and a man in the water.

At about the eighth hour the Water came into the old bed above the town. It came into it the way it comes into anything, which is all at once and without noise for about four minutes, and then the noise started and did not stop for two days.

Aren had the near end of a line round his forearm and one foot on the deck and one foot on the approach, and when the deck went he went with it and got the boy of fifteen off the boards with an arm across him and put him on the approach where the approach was eleven feet wide and above the sill, and the boy was sick over the side and apologised for it, which is a thing boys apologise for in a flood because they have been raised in a county that has never had one.

Then he went back onto the deck, which was the wrong thing to do, and Ferrow took him off it by the back of the coat with one hand and put him on the approach and said one word, “Down,” and the word was the same word he had said to everybody, and Aren went down.

What he did for the next two hours was stand on the north approach eleven feet wide and above a sill of oak with a bar he was not supposed to be holding, and look at the Water go into a bed that had been under a meadow since before the oldest man in that county was born, and count load points, because counting load points is what he has done since the first chapter of his own book and nobody has ever told him to stop, and there was a point where the approach should have shifted and did not because the sill held, and the point where the bank under the approach should have gone and did not because it had been an eleven-foot head for two years.

The whole of his trade is a capacity for seeing where a thing is going to fail while it is still standing, and he had a use for it for about eleven minutes and then the deck came off its abutments entirely and there was nothing to see and nothing to hold, and a man who has been on a structure for four hours in a spate and has watched it go does not go after it.

The deck went at about the ninth hour. It went the length of itself, from the north end to the south, and it took the rail with it and the bar with it and the four men who were on it, and they were all on the gravel rise on the far side by then because Ferrow had them off it twenty minutes before, which is a thing Ferrow did and does not talk about.

And then the Water had the old bed and the old bed was above the town and the old bed went south, and the town was four hundred yards below it on higher ground, and the seed room was a stone room behind a chapel four hundred yards below and to the east of the deck with a foot of water in it and a bench in it and nine jars on a shelf, and the water in it came up through a gap in the floor at the far end, and the woman of sixty-six had been moving nine jars one at a time from a shelf to a higher shelf since the sixth hour.

Merefen, four hundred miles off, in a room over a weigh-house, a keeper of records read a page of a day-book and put her pen down, because a line in it said that a road held, three weeks, printed at the foot of a form in an office's hand, and she had entered that figure against a return that came back in the summer and had been carrying ninety-six days of error against a printed three weeks ever since, and on the morning of the same day the boy of the Old Toll rang the bell at the fourth hour and a half because that is the day the far end keeps, and nothing at the far end of that bell moved, and she entered the hour against nothing and did not write the word she wanted to write.

She entered: *Entered, at the fourth hour, against no person: the bell was rung at the fourth hour and a half, and at the far end nothing was struck, and the two of those are one fact and I am not joining them in a book, because a book is a place where things get joined whether they were joined or not.*

And she wrote, under it, in the same hand, and did not read it back: *and I am aware that this office is now keeping a column about a road in a county that is not on any map, in a room where nobody works, and that the county has never been asked anything and has answered nothing, and that the only two documents that have ever come into this room out of that county are a fair copy of a column of eleven counties and a four-line sheet with no name at the top of it, and that both of them are on a shelf, and that I am the shelf.*

Then she did the thing she does at the fourth hour whatever has come in the bag, which is to go down to the market and look at a pig.
