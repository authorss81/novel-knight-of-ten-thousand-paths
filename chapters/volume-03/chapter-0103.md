# Chapter 0103

The fourth of Longlight, YR 313, was a Saturday and a market day, and the market in Merefen runs in one street with the water on the left of it, and by the second hour there were about two hundred people in it and eleven of them were arguing about a pig.

Aren Kest came up the two flights of a stair over a weigh-house at about the fifth hour with a wet sleeve and put his tool bag down outside a door that was already open, because a keeper of records in this county has never once in four years and a half shut a door on a man who came up that stair with a wet sleeve.

Mara Vey had four leaves on the table and the fair copy beside them, and she had been at it before the market, because the market is the one day in the week that the county's whole population is in one street and a keeper of records who wants to be left alone wants the opposite.

"You have not eaten," she said.

"I have eaten at the second hour like a person who is not thinking about it," he said, "and I have come up here to look at a column, and I am not going to pretend that is a different reason."

She gave him bread and a piece of cheese and he ate both standing up, and then she put the fair copy down in front of him, and it is the fourth leaf, and it is the same leaf she has read four times in four minutes on the twenty-seventh of Greenmonth and has not been able to put down since.

---

**Eleven counties. Eight stones. Three blanks. And the answer does not change.**

*Copied from a copy of a copy. County column, fourth leaf, a schedule of dispositions. Eleven counties are entered in it. In eight of the eleven the entry carries, in a registrar's hand, a short formula — a long field, a boundary, a season, a stone renewed in public — and the eight are given by name and a year is given against each name, and the year is a year anybody in that county can name. In three of the eleven the entry is the county's name and a dash.*

He read it with his finger going down the column and stopping at the eighth and going on, and he read the eight formulas twice, and then he put his hand flat on the paper the way a man puts a hand on a wall in the dark.

"It is the same," he said. "You counted it on the twenty-seventh of Greenmonth and you have not moved one mark, and I have gone over it and I get eight, and I would like to say something about that which is not the same sentence I would have said a month ago."

"Say it."

"Eleven is not a small number of counties to have in a column of a schedule of dispositions in a book that was sold for a farthing of salt to a man who wanted a canal wharf," said Aren Kest. "And eight of eleven is not a third, and eight of eleven is not a half, and a man who goes looking for a third and a half in a column of counties will go looking until he finds something. **Eight of eleven is what a column looks like when whatever puts men in it has been doing it for a long time in the same places.**"

"That is an argument about why and you have not got one."

"I have got two things and one of them is a question and the other one is a thing I learned in a yard on this street." He turned the leaf a quarter turn towards the lamp. "**Here is the question. What is the column headed.**"

Mara Vey did not answer him at once, and she was not slow. She was doing the thing she does, which is looking at a document she already knows better than the man looking at it, and then she said:

"Nothing. There is nothing at the head of it. It is a copy of a copy of a copy and the copy was made off the foot of a page and the foot of a page does not carry the head of a column, and the head of that column is on the parent leaf, and the parent leaf is on a shelf in a press in a city that does not admit to the Crown that it exists, in the hand of a registrar who sold it."

"So we have a column of eleven counties with no heading over it."

"We have eleven counties and eight formulas and a heading that is four hundred miles off in a press that cannot be served, in a city that is not on any map in this kingdom," said Mara Vey. "**Which means that the single piece of paper in this whole business that would tell us what the column is for is the one piece of paper that nobody can get.** And I have wanted that leaf since the twenty-seventh of Greenmonth and there is no instrument in this kingdom that will get it for me, and the reason is that a creditor's press is a thing that can be asked and not a thing that can be made to answer."

"And the other thing."

"The other thing is what I learned in a yard on this street," said Aren Kest, "and it is a bridgewright's thing and not a filing room's thing, and I am going to say it badly. **A list is a thing you can refuse. A column is a thing you get put in.** A list has a person in it and the person knows they are in it and can say the whole of what the line says. A column has a county in it, and the county does not know it is in it, and if the county is a person it can be asked and if the county is a court it cannot be sued, and if the county is a piece of ground with a stone in it then it cannot do anything at all except be in the column."

He put his finger on the eighth formula.

"And this," he said, "is the only thing in this column anybody can be asked about, because it is the only line in it that anybody made by hand and in public and can still be asked what he swore. A bench of nine, twenty-six days, and a man with a ledger who would tell me — I can go back to those men and ask them what they said and they will tell me differently from each other, which is what a fact does. **Eight counties have a thing in this column that a man could be asked about. Three counties have a dash. I am not going to say what the dash is and neither are you, and I am not going to say the eight are a cause, and I want it written down in this book today that I did not say it.**"

She wrote it down. That is the entry, and it is the first entry in that day-book in some months that is about a piece of arithmetic and not about a thing she had chosen not to do, and there is a reason for that which she is not going to say out loud and which she entered on the next line, because she always enters the reason.

*Fourth of Longlight, YR 313, sixth hour, a market day, claim number against the office and against no person, no opinion. Asked the question and did not know the answer: the column of eleven counties has no heading on the copy and the heading is on a leaf in a press in a city that is not on any map, and there is no instrument in this kingdom that will get it. The eight entries and the three dashes stand as they stand. He has asked me to enter that he did not say the eight are a cause, and I have entered it, and he did not ask me to enter anything else and I have not entered anything else, and I am aware that the not entering is the part of it that is a decision, and I have entered that this time it was a decision about a column and not about a person, and the difference is that a column is not anybody's business and a person is.*

**And then the market sent word up the hill at about the seventh hour, and it changed the whole of the rest of the day.**

---

It came in with a carter who had brought a load of reed down from the upper fen and had been told to mention it to somebody.

"There's a delegation on the road," he said, in the street. "Came in at the upper post at first light. Eleven of a council and two more, and a wagon with a board in it, and the fella with the board says they're four hundred miles from anywhere and they've been on the road eleven days and they want the guild hall and they will not come to a house."

"Which guild hall," said Bram Ottery, who had been standing in the market for about an hour with nothing to do and who was a man the county had elected once and paid fourpence a week out of a private box ever since, and who was the man a count had gone up by one for.

"The one over the wharf," said the carter. "The first of the month they wrote to the council and the council passed it on, and the clerk read out that they'd come on the ninth, and it's the ninth of Longlight and it's a market day, and the delegation told the post they'd be there on the ninth and the post said the ninth is two days' off and the delegation said they would wait, and they're waiting, and one of the eleven has been sitting on a bench by the weigh-house since first light reading."

"The post has counted from the ninth twice," said Aren Kest, from the rail. "They said eleven days in a waggon and they were written in the second week of their own year, and eleven days from a day I cannot name is not a day a post can count to. They are five days early against our clock and they do not have our clock."

"They have a board," said the carter, "and the fella with the board says they are not selling anything and they would like it read out in a room with the guild in it and anybody who comes may come, and he said one more thing which I did not understand and which I am going to say anyway because it seemed to me to be the sort of thing a man says twice on purpose." He shut his eyes for a moment and got it. "**He said: the stone is not to be sworn in the presence of anybody from this side.**"

The market did not stop. Two hundred people went on doing what two hundred people do in a street on a Saturday. But about nine of them looked over, which in this county is a crowd.

**And by the second hour the sentence had been round the market and had changed, which is the thing that happens to every sentence in this county and the reason the county has never once been able to be warned about anything.**

The sentence that went round was that a delegation from the west had come to tell Merefen what to do with its bridges. A woman at a fish table said it out loud to a man she knew, and he said *we'll see about that*, and she said *they'll have the wharf and the reach and the Old Toll inside a year, they've brought a board*, and neither of them had been in the hall, and both of them were repeating the word *they* about a council of eleven householders and two bridgewrights and a seed-carter, and it was not a lie, and it was not a rumour either, it was the ordinary arithmetic of a street where a thing said twice is a thing about other people.

Bram Ottery came and stood beside them and said, "They said six lines. I heard all six from the fishmonger who was in the doorway and I have written them on the back of my hand and I am going to a meeting on Thursday about the chest, because this bench has been asked four times and has said yes four times and I am not going to be one of the men who was not in the room when the first delegation in eleven years came up this hill."

"You are one of the men who elected himself to a penny a quarter," said the man at the fish table.

"And I am one of the men who is going to a meeting," said Bram Ottery, "and you are one of the men who is going to say in a month that the fish was bad in Longlight, and we shall see which of us was in the room."

Nobody wrote the six lines down that day except Bram Ottery, on the back of his hand, in pencil, and by Thursday morning the third line had worn off his hand and he got it back out of his own head at the door of the hall, which nobody in the hall thought was remarkable except the road warden, who thought it remarkable, and who has said in one notebook line that the only copy of that document in this county on the fourteenth of Longlight was in the head of an unemployed man in a market, and that a document in one head is a record and a document in a hand is a claim, and that the difference between those two things is the entire difference between a person and an office and neither of them can be got at.

---

**And at about the eighth hour, in a room over a weigh-house, a keeper of records and a road warden stood at a table with a fair copy of the fourth leaf of a purchase between them, and neither of them said the obvious thing for about nine seconds.**

"You told me on the first of this month that a county clerk filled in the third column of that return out of a printed example," said Aren Kest.

"I did."

"And the example was your own words in a yard, and the office printed it, and the office prints it at the foot of every form of the series."

"Yes."

"Then the office did not have to do a single thing," said Aren Kest. "**The office did not have to send a man into that county, and it did not have to write a letter, and it did not have to decide anything, and it did not have to be in a room. It printed a form with a real name and a real guild on it, at the foot, as the way of doing the thing, and a clerk in a county four hundred miles off did the rest on his own initiative on a wet day with a quill.** That is the whole of how a form becomes an instrument, and I have been looking for a man in a coat for two years."

Mara Vey did not answer that, and she turned round in her chair and looked out of the window at the weigh-house yard, where a woman of thirty-eight was sitting on a bench with a board across her knees and a strap of a bag over her shoulder, reading.

"She has been there since first light," she said, "and a keeper of records in this county is a woman who is paid fourpence a year, and that woman has been sitting in my yard for eight hours reading a board and has not come up, and I am going to go down there and ask her whether she wants tea, and I am going to do it because I am curious, and I am going to write down the fact that I was curious, because the day I stop being curious is the day this office starts being a thing that answers a figure."

"Ask her what she is reading," said Aren Kest.

"That," said Mara Vey, standing up and straightening the front of her coat, "is a question I have never once in four years and a half asked anybody who came up that stair, and I have asked you every question there is, so you may draw whatever comfort you think that is worth."

She was at the door when she turned round and said:

"They will be read in this hall on Thursday and the bench will be in it and you will be in it, and I have read this letter of yours and the yard's answer of Thursday, and the answer the yard gave was the rate and the rate is a number, and the county that wrote that letter has written us a thing that is not a question and is not a complaint and is the first paper out of a place I have had in four years and a half that asks for a **price** instead of a fact." She had her hand on the door. "I do not know what to do with a paper that asks for a price. There is no column for it."
