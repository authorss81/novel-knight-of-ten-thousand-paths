# Chapter 0107

The nineteenth of Longlight, YR 313, was a Sunday and it was not a market day, and the moot hall of this county stands above the wool exchange with four steps up and a door that has been painted nine times, and on a Sunday there is one magistrate in it and no clerk, because the clerk comes on the market days and on the Mondays, and a magistrate who works on a Sunday is a magistrate with something she has not put down yet.

Lysa Fenn had the paper open on the bench in front of her. It had come into her hands on the Friday before, and it had been read out in the guild hall over the wharf on the Wednesday, four days ago, and the reeve of this county had been in that same building on the Wednesday afternoon about a toll matter and had heard the whole of it read aloud by the man who keeps the minute of a council in a county that is not on any map in this kingdom, and had said nothing, and had gone out, and was going to be in this room again this morning and neither of them knew it yet.

The paper is one leaf and a half and it is a request, and it is addressed to the moot of a fen county, and it asks for two things.

*That the boundary of the Hearth Marches, being made of water, be noted by this court, in the manner in which this court notes boundaries; and that a man be appointed to go and look at it.*

And the second half of the paper, which is the half that is polite, is a paragraph about the cost of the man and about how the court might be recompensed for the trouble, and there is a sum in it in the county's own small money, and the sum is not large, and the paragraph exists because whoever wrote it has been told that this is how courts are spoken to.

---

**And at about the ninth hour on that Sunday a man of about fifty came up the four steps with a bundle and had been in the wool exchange buying wool that morning, and stood in the doorway, and did not come further in, because in nine years she has watched men stand in that doorway and not come further in and has learned that the not coming in is nearly always about money.**

He had a boundary. He had had it for thirty-one years and there were two fields and a hedge and a stream and his neighbour's brother-in-law, and he could not pay the fee for a plan, and the plan is the thing this court makes and the plan is what the note hangs on, and the fee for a plan is four shillings and eightpence, which is fifty-six pence, and he could see from the doorway that it was four times what the note would cost him, and he had brought five and fourpence, which is sixty-four, in a twist of paper, and had counted it on the steps.

"I will not take it off you," said Lysa Fenn. "Sit down. Tell me the four corners."

He told her the four corners. It took about four minutes and it was good, and it was better than most, and she wrote it down in the ordinary form and then stopped and put her pen down.

"I can note it," she said. "I can note it in a line and a quarter of an hour and it will stand as well as a plan, and the fee for the note is one shilling, and you have five and fourpence, and I am going to take the shilling and I am going to enter that I took the shilling, and I am going to enter it against this court and not against you, and the reason is that I have wanted for nine years to make this instrument useful to a man who cannot afford a title and a patron and today is the first day I have been able to do it, and the reeve's office calls a one-shilling note a discouragement and she is right about the arithmetic and wrong about what a court is for."

He gave her the shilling and she entered the note and it stood, and he went down the four steps, and that is the whole of the reason she was sitting in that room on a Sunday, and the paper from a border county four hundred miles off was an accident of the day.

**And it is in the same room, in the same week, in the same hand, and it is the reason the note on the back of the border county's paper is not a paradox and is a job.**

A man who can pay five and fourpence and has been in that doorway can be a claimant. Eleven people in a county four hundred miles off, of whom not one is a person under this court's practice, cannot. And she built the first thing in nine years and a man took it out of the room inside half an hour, and on Friday she spent four minutes killing the second, and both of those are the same sentence doing the same work, and the sentence is that a person can be heard and a person can be refused and a person can be appealed, and a thing that cannot be refused and cannot be appealed is not a court and is a form.

---

**A boundary dispute is not a dispute in this county's law. She has known that for nine years and has never once wanted it to be otherwise.**

The reason is old and it is not anybody's fault. A boundary in this kingdom is a thing a court *notes*. It notes a boundary when two people who both claim it come before it and say so, and it puts the line on a plan and enters the year, and after that nobody in the county can move it without a second note. The note is the safest instrument this court has and she has built nine years of her practice on it.

What a court cannot do is note a boundary on the application of nobody. A note requires a claimant. Two claimants, in fact, and they have to be people, because the whole of the proceeding is that a person can be heard and a person can be refused and a person can be appealed, and those three things are what make a line stand for a hundred years.

And a person in this county's law is a person. Not a body. Not an office. Not a bench, not a council, not a guild, not a village, and certainly not a river.

**The paper asks her to note a boundary held by a council, on the application of that council, against nobody.**

She wrote for about an hour. And what she wrote was correct.

*That this court has before it an application by a body of eleven householders of a border county, whereof no member is a person under this court's practice, and that no person is in default in respect of the matter complained of, and that no person having a right in the boundary named has come before this court, and that there is therefore nothing pending.*

*And that the instrument is accordingly spent upon this answer and requires no further returnable date.*

It is the construction. It is the same construction, in the same words, that she used on the ninth of Greenmonth to beat a count of persons out of existence in about nine minutes, and she wrote it out again in about four minutes this time, and it worked, and she read it back to herself twice in an empty room on a Sunday and it is good, and it is the best piece of legal writing she has done in two years.

And then she sat with her hands flat on the bench and was frightened, and it took her about nine seconds to work out why, and she had known the answer before she started writing and had written the answer down anyway because the answer was correct and she is not a woman who lets a correct answer wait because she does not like where it goes.

**A body is not a person. She has said that in eleven rooms and has won eleven arguments with it, and it is the reason a court cannot be sued, cannot be refused, and cannot be injured.**

And on the ninth of Greenmonth, YR 313, at about the fourth hour, in a room in this county, a county council resolved that the Bellweather Claims Archive is a body corporate, with a door, a number, and the capacity to sue and to be sued. **The same county. The same court. Four months apart.**

She wrote out a second note, on the back, and it is in the file, and it is the only thing in nine years of her practice that she has written for nobody to see:

*I have made two instruments this year out of the same sentence and they cannot both be right, and the one I made on the ninth of Greenmonth was made by a council and is in force, and I made it and I asked the question that produced it, and I do not know how to unmake it and I do not think there is a bench in this kingdom that could. If a body corporate is a person for the purposes of a court, then a body is a person, and then the first thing that follows is not the nine of Greenmonth. The first thing that follows is that a court is a person, and if a court is a person then somebody can sue it, and I have spent nine years telling this county that nobody can, and I was pleased about it.*

*And I cannot find the sentence in my own reasoning that lets me keep the first half and drop the second half. I have looked for it for an hour. It is not there. I am recording that it is not there, on the back of a paper, on a Sunday, where nobody will find it unless somebody goes looking, which is a thing I have now done once.*

---

**And at about the eleventh hour on the nineteenth of Longlight, four days after she had stood in the same building and heard the paper read out, Reeve Aldis Fenn came into the moot hall on the way to a room upstairs, and neither of them had arranged it.**

"You are working on a Sunday," said the reeve.

"I am working on a Sunday," said Lysa Fenn, "and you are going upstairs to a chest that is not in this room, and neither of us is going to stop, and I would like that understood at the start of it."

"I am going upstairs about a chest," said Aldis Fenn, "and I would like one thing said before either of us says anything else, and it is a small thing and it is not about the paper."

"Go on."

"**I am not going to help you.** I have known about that paper since the Wednesday and I have not sent for it and I have not asked you for a copy and I am not going to, and I would like you to know that I have made a decision about it and that the decision is to have nothing to do with it, and I have made it because I sat on a bench in this county for nine years and I know exactly what a paper in that room does to the people it names, and I am not going to be on the same side of that as you."

"You have never been on my side of anything," said Lysa Fenn.

"No," said the reeve. "**And I have got a bench in this county that has been asked four times and has said yes four times and is not a bench, and a man in a yard has said that out loud and been right, and I have had it in the minute four times now and I have not improved on it, because the day I improve on it I will have decided that it is a bench, and it is not, and if it is not then the only thing it is for is being asked.** I have a delegation from that county sitting in my hall being told what it may not do, and I have four names in a subscription book and two of them are the same man twice, and I have four marks and six a year voted to spend on a crossing I cannot afford, and I am not going to stand in this room on a Sunday and help my sister find a construction."

"You have not helped me find anything," said Lysa Fenn. "I found it this morning and I did not like it."

"Then I will say the one thing I will say, and then I am going upstairs." The reeve put her hand on the edge of the bench, not on the paper. "**You have made this office a person. That is what the council did on the ninth of Greenmonth and I voted for it and I would do it again, and I would like it noticed that I voted for it and that you are the one who is now frightened of it, and that you did not consult the bench and I did not ask you to.** And I will tell you why I voted for it, and it is not the reason you gave in that room, and my reason is that a thing with a door and a number can be written to. A keeper can only be written about. I wanted this county to have one building that anybody can send a letter to, and I have got it, and I have got it in a fortnight, and it has taken four months to come back and it is coming back now, and it is coming back as your handwriting, which is the handwriting I would expect."

She stopped.

"I am not saying it to be cruel," she said. "I am saying it because you are going to go on and do whatever you are going to do, and I would like one sentence in this room that is a fact about you and not about a document."

"Two," said Lysa Fenn, "and I will give them to you, and they are not about a document either. One: the thing I am frightened of is not that the office is a person. It is that the office is a person and I am the person who is in it, and in about four years somebody is going to bring a form to the door of that office and name me in the first line of it, and the office will be able to be sued and I will be the one who answers."

"Two."

"Two: **I have been a magistrate for nine years on the sentence that a court is not a person, and that sentence is now the most expensive sentence anybody in this county has ever been given away for nothing, and I gave half of it away in a room on the ninth of Greenmonth and I did not see what I was doing, because I was pleased.**"

The reeve looked at her for about four seconds, which is longer than either of them normally allows herself in a room.

"I am going upstairs," said Aldis Fenn.

"Go on."

"I am not going to say it is all right," said the reeve, "and I am not going to say you are right, and I am not going to put a line in the minute about either of them, and I would like you to understand that I have made a decision about that as well and that the decision is that neither of those sentences is going in a book in this county while I am in it, because a minute that carries my sister's fear is a minute that carries my sister's fear, and there are eleven people in this county who would find out what it was in a fortnight."

**And the two of them conceded nothing to each other on the nineteenth of Longlight, YR 313, and both of them were right, and neither of them said so, and the paper was answered on the back of it in four minutes on a Sunday morning and filed on the Monday, and the answer was correct, and the instrument was spent, and there is no name in it anywhere, and no Crown's hand has written a name down in this county since the twenty-seventh of Fallowmonth, and there is still not one.**
