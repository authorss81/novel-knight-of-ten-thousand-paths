# Chapter 0146

The fair copy went on the board under the bench at the sixth hour of the last day of the thirteenth rolling and by the seventh hour there were four people standing under the stair reading it, which is the most a fair copy has ever done in this county.

At about the ninth hour Thessa Wend came down off the water with her boots in her hand and a lamp in the other one, and she did not come to the room. She came to the guild room, and she came in the way she comes in, which is at once and without asking, and she put the lamp on the bench and said the number.

“The water at the head has come up a foot and a half since the fourth hour and it is not the water that has come into the ditch. The ditch is dry. It is coming over the bank above the ditch, on the north side, where the bank is a month old and has never taken a flood, and it is coming over it the way it comes over everything, which is at once, and it is not falling again.”

Ferrow was on his feet before she had finished and was at the door and the door was open and the outside stair was empty and that part of the county was asleep in the way a farming county sleeps in the last week of a rolling.

“Get the men,” he said, and then stopped in the doorway with his hand on the frame, and said the next thing to the room and not to the lane, and it is the only time in nineteen years that he has made a first hand stop in a doorway to say a thing.

“The plan holds,” he said. “The trench above the town is dry. The four villages' ditches are dry. The trough is dry. The boundary is a mile of new ditch with new banks on both sides of it and there is not one yard of it running, and nine days after we made it, it holds. I am putting that in the guild's book tonight in that order, and I want it in the book before anything else happens, because a plan that fails gets a book written about it and a plan that holds gets nothing, and in about forty years there will be a man in this room who will find the failure and not the holding, and he will be right about the failure and wrong about the year.”

He went down the lane.

It was the span at the trough.

Nobody built it to be in a river. That is the entire of it and it needs saying in one breath and not improved on.

In the seventh rolling, in the four hours of the flood, the west bank of the head ditch at the trough was taken back nine feet to take the weight of a landing, because a landing is what a man needs when he is carrying a barrow of elm in the dark and the far side of a wet ditch is a thing a man does not step onto. It was a good job, made by the guild, made in a hurry, made in a flood, and it was made nine feet into a bank that had been there for a hundred and forty years.

In the ninth rolling Ferrow opened four hundred and forty yards of ditch and put the head back at the trough, and the trough is eleven yards from where the head had been, and the opening for the landing is on the new line and the landing is on the old line, and the distance between the two is a bank that is a month old.

In the tenth rolling, in four days' notice, in a wet week, with four men out of a fen county and about nine of the guild's, the landing was made into a footbridge. Two bays, five yards, elm, on two oak sills, pegged, with a handrail on the north side only because a handrail on the south side would have been in the water in a flood and a handrail in the water is a thing that drowns a man by being useful to him.

Four days' notice is what happened, from beginning to end. Four days' notice is enough to set two sills, to make two bays of elm, to dig two holes, and to peg. Four days' notice is not enough to find out where a river goes at night in the wrong part of the year, and the man who set those sills set them where a ditch goes, and a ditch and a river are the same channel on nine days of the year and are not the same channel on the tenth, and the tenth one is the one that came over a bank a month old at about the ninth hour of the last day of the last week of the last rolling of the year.

Ferrow said it in about nine sentences on the bank and he said it flatly and he said it to a stranger who had asked him nothing, and he did not say it to excuse anybody, because there is nobody to excuse.

“A bank that has not been through a winter is not a bank, it is a heap. I have said that sentence in this county twice and I am saying it a third time and I am sick of it. The elm is green. It was felled in the week before the flood and it was in the water for two days of the seventh rolling and it went up in the eighth and it came down in the ninth and it has been in a stack in the wet for four rollings since, and green elm in a wet stack is not a beam, it is a thing that will be a beam in a year.

“The sills are oak and the oak is good and the oak is in the wrong hole. The bed under that sill is what was under it in the fifth rolling and it has had nine days of new trench opened in it in the twelfth, and new trench is loose trench, and a sill on loose trench in a river goes when the river moves the loose part, and the river has about nine hours to do it.

“I put the span there. I gave the order and I did the order and I had four days and about nine men and the four best hands anybody has sent me in nineteen years, and I built the best thing four days' notice allows, and it is a lid, and a lid is the right thing, and a lid is now taking a whole night river across two bays of green elm on two oak sills in a bed that is moving, and there is no third thing to do with it.”

They got about thirty men onto that bank in an hour, which is a county that has never had a night alarm in its life getting thirty men onto a bank in an hour, and what they did was the oldest thing in the trade and the reason Ferrow said the plan held before he said anything else.

They put sacks of gravel on the north lip, and they took the new bank down in two places to let the water have the low side, and they cut a channel through the month's old stuff where the water came over so that it came over somewhere chosen instead of somewhere chosen by the bank, and the three men who did that were on it for a quarter of an hour and one of them is the man who scraped ground for forty years and he came back with both hands bleeding at the nails and did not mention it and nobody mentioned it.

And the water came over where they had chosen, and the trough filled, and the ditch at the head ran, and the boundary ran, and a mile of new ditch took the night river in about a minute and a half, and the plan held for about two hours after that, and the thing that did not hold was five yards long and had a handrail on one side of it.

He went out onto it at the tenth hour.

Ferrow did not ask him. That is the thing about it that nobody in the Marches will believe in ninety years, and it is in the guild's book in nine words that Ferrow wrote himself and did not show to anybody until the morning: *and I did not ask him, and I would have had no standing to stop him, and he went because a lid is a thing a man stands on.*

“Why,” said the man of sixty-three, whose hand had been on the stone since he was nineteen, to nobody, from the bank, about two hours later.

“To be on it,” Aren said. “That is all of it. There are thirty men on this bank who can throw gravel and two of them can throw gravel well. There is nobody in this county who can read a deck that is taking water, and a deck that is taking water tells you where it is going to go by the sound it makes about a second and a half before it goes there, and if it goes there from under a man that man is dead, and it will not do it while I am on it. That is a bridgewright's job. That is the only thing I have ever been able to do that nobody else here can do, and if I do not do it then in about an hour this thing will come off its sills sideways and take the north bank with it and the four villages will be under it by morning.”

The man of sixty-three said, “Then say it to the room and let the room hear a stranger say it.”

“There is no room,” said Aren. “It is the middle of the night and there are thirty men on a bank and about nine of them are yours and twenty-one of them are not, and I am not going to make a speech about it and I am not going to take an instruction from a bench that has told me in a fair copy that I hold no office in this county. I am going out on it and I am going to call what I see, and if any man up here thinks I am taking over, he can come out here and take over, and I will get off it, and I would get off it inside four seconds.”

Nobody came out. That is entered in the guild's book without comment, and the comment is that nineteen men and a bound and a slate woman and eleven on a bench all understood at the same moment, in the dark, on a wet bank, that a stranger had just said out loud the one sentence that made him not a stranger for about nine hours, and that they had all heard it, and that not one of them took it.

A lid is not a drain. That is the working, entire, and it is nine sentences long and Aled Rhys said them from a boat on the north side, at about the eleventh hour, with his pole in his hand, and he did not vote once, then or afterwards, and nobody in that county has ever heard him vote and he has been in the county since the second rolling.

He said them to a bank and not to a room, and he said them in the flat voice of a man who is paid on the day the water goes down.

“A boat is a thing that cannot be stopped, and that is my trade entire and it is the reason I am paid to be in it. Everything else a man does in a boat is a way of not stopping. You cannot anchor a river. You cannot get behind anything. You can get under the lee of a bank for four minutes and you will spend the rest of the night on a wheel and you will be cold and you will be paid, and the reason you are paid is that you came down it and could not come back up.

“A bridge is the opposite of a boat and it is the only structure I know that cannot get out of the way, and that is why a man stands on it and does not stand in it. A boat with too much water in it throws something off. That is seamanship entire. A bridge with too much water in it cannot throw anything off, so a bridgewright has to decide in the morning what to throw off in the night, and the thing he throws off is people, and tonight the people are off it and the water is not, and so tonight this is a boat that has forgotten how.

“So a man on it is not holding it up. There is a difference and it took me twenty years to learn it and it is the only thing I know that a man from a fen county can tell a bridgewright from a county. A man on a span at night is not keeping the water off. He is keeping the deck in one piece so that the water has one way to go instead of four, and he is finding out which of the two sills is going first, and when he finds out he says it out loud, and everybody on the bank acts on his mouth and not on the wood, because the wood is four hundred yards away and his mouth is not.

“If he says it wrong, everybody on this bank dies of being wrong in the correct direction. That is the trade I am in and it is the only sentence I have ever had that is worth saying about it, and I have said it, and I am not voting on anything and I never do.”

Then he went back to his pole.

The things he called out, in order, are in the guild's book, and he called each of them about four seconds before it happened, and the book has eleven of them and the last one is at the fourth hour of the morning.

The joint at the near end lifted twice and he said *down* both times and both times thirty men put gravel on a lid instead of a hole. The rail on the north side took a strain that came up through the pegs rather than the feet and he said *rail, not deck* and they put four lines on it off the far bank in about nine minutes and it took every pound of the strain out of the deck and left the deck free to do the one job it has.

At about the second hour he called the near sill, and he called it as a thing that had not moved yet, and Ferrow said afterwards that he did not hear how he could know it and that he stopped asking about nine minutes later when the whole bank heard it.

“Near sill. Soft under the heel. It has not moved. It will move in the half hour and it will move toward the ditch and it will take the near bay with it, and the near bay is the one you are standing on. Move the men off the inside edge of the bank in about the length of a count of ten and do it now and not when he says so, because he will say so when it is too late to matter for the bank and in time for him.”

They moved the men off the inside edge and it made the difference between a bank that lost a strip of turf and a bank that went into the ditch with thirty men standing on it.

He counted load points the whole time, four at the near sill and two under the heel of it and two at the far end, and he counted them the way he has counted them since the first chapter of his own book, which is to say twice, to see whether he had got the same ones, and he had, and there is no book in Merefen or in this county that any of it goes in.

The water came over the lid and went across it and off the far end, which is what a lid is for, and it did that for hour after hour with about thirty men putting gravel on a bank on the other side of it in the dark, and a mile of re-cut boundary took the night river, and the salt under four villages did not move one inch, and by the second hour of the morning the four villages were not under water and the four villages had not been under water all night, and a man in this county who had scraped ground for forty years put the reason in nine words on a bank and the reason was that a mile of ditch that was dug nine days ago is a mile of ditch that takes the water and takes it away instead of standing in it and letting the ground drink it, and the ground drank about a foot and a half in nine days and will drink about a foot and a half in four years, and that is the whole of what anybody in this county can honestly say about a salt table in the fourth week of a year.
