# Chapter 0140

The second paper was in the same basket and it was under the onions, and it stayed on the table in the room under the tithe barn for a day and a half because a four-line notice was in the room and there is only so much a room can hold at once.

It came wrapped in oiled paper with a wrapper round it, and on the wrapper there was one line in the same small upright hand, and the line was the reason it had been sent, and the reason was the reason a copy is addressed to a shelf and not to a person.

*A copy is sent to a shelf and not to a man, and a copy that is sent to a man becomes a message by the act of sending it, and this is sent to a shelf, and the shelf is the room it is now in, and if you are not the shelf then do not open it, and it has been opened, and I would like to know that.*

Under the wrapper, in the same hand, the entire cost, costed because the woman who keeps that end has been told by a keeper of records four hundred miles off that a keeper who sends a thing four hundred miles and does not know what it costs is not a keeper:

*Two men on the road, thirty-two pence the day, three days: thirty-two, thirty-two, thirty-two, which is ninety-six pence. Sheet, wax, and the boy's copying at the office's rate, six pence. Ninety-six and six is a hundred and two pence, and it is a hundred and two pence of a bench's money, and a bench's money is not a road warden's money and the bench has paid it.*

And the copy itself. Eleven counties. Eight with, in a registrar's hand, the short formula that says a long field, a boundary, a season, a stone renewed in public, in a year anybody there can name. Three with the county's name and a dash. And no head over the column, because there has never been a head over the column, and the copy says so at the foot, in the copyist's hand, in four words: *the head is not here.*

And under the column, on the same sheet, in a second hand and a clerk's flat upright, a minute.

The minute was the answer, and the keeper who sent the copy to a shelf in a county four hundred miles off had asked for it, and the question was nine words long and it was what a stone in this column is, and the bench of nine in the county of clay sat on it and answered in their own form and sent the answer four hundred miles to a county that is not on any map, without being asked to by anybody in that county, because a bench that is asked a question it can answer is the only kind there is, and this one has been asked four times and has said yes four times and is not a bench and knows it.

*Entered, in the third week of the ninth rolling, in the long room, by the bench: that the county was asked what a stone is in a column of eleven counties, and that this county has nine stones entered in its own roll, and that eight of the nine are between two holdings of the same watercourse and one is between two holdings of the same seed, and that not one of the nine is between two fields.*

*And that a boundary between two fields stands where the water stands and is a thing of the ground, and that a stone that fixes a share of a watercourse, or of a sowing, is not a boundary and is a claim, and that it is a promise made by two men to each other about a number, and that it is renewed in public in a field by the men who hold the water, in a year named so that anybody can name it after, and that it can be kept for a hundred and forty years.*

*And that the county cannot say why a registrar in a press four hundred miles off has written the same six words against eight counties, and does not know the head of the column, and has not asked, and will not now ask, because a bench that has been asked a question and has answered it is not a bench that goes and asks a second one for a different reason.*

He read it in the cold room under the tithe barn, standing up, with the sheet on the table, and the minute-man came down the outside stair at about the second hour of the afternoon because the room was open and had been open all morning, and nobody had sent for him and he came anyway, which is a thing that man has done twice now in eight rollings and has never once explained.

“Read that again,” the minute-man said, from the bottom of the stair.

He read it again. Then the minute-man came and stood at the table and read it himself, all of it, twice, slowly, the way a man reads a thing he intends to be the only other person in the world to have read.

They were in that room about two hours and nobody came in. The door is open and the stair is outside, and a man coming up that stair in the second hour saw two men over a table with a paper on it and did not interrupt, which is a thing this county has been doing for a hundred and forty years.

The finding did not arrive whole. It came in three pieces, and each piece was a fact that one of them already had, and it took two hours to find out which two of the three were load-bearing, and what stopped it was arithmetic, which is the thing that had been sitting in both their heads since the first rolling in a room four hundred miles off with a fourth leaf of a purchase on the table.

The first piece was the bench's minute, and it is that eight of the eight are not boundaries. Six fix a share of a watercourse. Two fix a share of a sowing. The column does not say which; the column says *a long field, a boundary, a season, a stone renewed in public*, in a registrar's hand, in six words, and a registrar's hand is a hand that writes a shape, and a shape is a category, and a category is a thing made on purpose so that two unlike things can be put in one column and the column can be counted.

He had been trying to get eight of eleven twice for eight rollings. He had got it four times, in four rooms, on two sides of a line, with two different figures, and each time it had been the same eight, and he had written down in a notebook on the sixth side of that line that eight of eleven is what a column looks like when whatever puts men in it has been doing it for a long time in the same places, and that eight of eleven is not a third and is not a half.

“Give me the register again,” he said, and the minute-man went down and brought the roll chest and unlocked it, because the roll of this county is the only book in it with a stitch in it, and they put it on the table beside the fair copy, and the roll has about sixty households of the four white villages on it with no oath mark, and the fair copy has eleven counties, and the two of them were on the same table for the first time in the world.

“Three counties with a dash,” he said. “The third piece is that a dash is not a zero.”

“It is a name and a dash.”

“A dash in a registrar's hand says there is nothing to enter, and it does not say there is nothing there. This county has about sixty households on a roll with no mark against them because nobody asked, and they are alive and they are paying, and if this county had been in that column it would be one of the three with a dash, and it would be a dash about a county that has never once been asked anything by anybody including itself.”

“Get to the point of it,” said the minute-man, in the voice.

“The three are counties where nobody survived to swear anything. Not counties where nobody swore. Counties where there was nobody left to do the swearing, and a stone that gets sworn in a field in a year anybody there can name cannot be sworn in a county with nobody in it, and so the entry is a dash, and the dash does not mean those counties did nothing. It means nobody is left who can be asked what they did. That is all a dash is and it is the same as what my own notebook says about a number, and I have been on both sides of this table and I did not see it.”

The minute-man put his hand flat on the fair copy.

“Go on,” he said.

“Now the finding,” said Aren Kest, “and it is small, and it took two hours, and I am going to say it in about nine sentences and you are going to tell me if I have said it wrong.

“An oath renewed in public by men who are still alive is the only durable consent a rural county can produce. That is the first half and it is not a discovery, it is this county. Ask nine men in a field what they swore and they will tell you, and they will tell you differently from each other, and a fact is a thing that can be told differently.

“And the second half is why eight of eleven counties are in a column of dispositions at all. That same oath is the only document in the countryside that names a body. A field, a boundary, a season, a stone, a year, nine men, is a name, and it is the only place in this whole business where a body is written down by name in the countryside itself and not by an office. And a body can be served. A county that has a name in an office is a county that can be got at, and an office with eight of them can begin to do whatever it is going to do with a district of eleven.

“So the records gather where the oaths are. Not because an oath causes anything. Nobody in this room has any evidence that an oath causes a severance, and I have said that out loud in a market town and it is entered in a book and is not to be improved on. They gather where the oaths are because an oath is a name, and an office can only act on a name, and a county with no oath in it has no name an office can use, and so it is a dash, and so it is left alone, and being left alone is the worst thing that has ever happened to it and is also the only reason it was ever left alone.”

The room was quiet for long enough that the outside stair made a noise with somebody's boot and went away again.

“And that is why the arithmetic stops,” he said. “Not because there is no pattern. There is a pattern and it is filing, and filing is the only honest reason a record ever has a shape. It stops because eight of eleven was never eight of anything. Six of them are promises about a share of water and two of them are promises about a share of seed, and in the Marches the water ones are the ones that have been kept for a hundred and forty years by men who are still alive, and a promise about a share of water is a claim, and a claim is not a boundary, and a claim kept for a hundred and forty years by men who are still alive does not just sit there. It moves the thing it is a claim about. A share of a watercourse is fixed, and the watercourse is not, and after a hundred and forty years of a share being sworn over a channel that is not in the place the share says, the share is eleven yards off the water it is a share of, and a mile of boundary has a pond on the wrong side of it, and four villages have the salt coming up out of ground that always had it in it.

“So the county I have been sitting in for eight rollings is not a curiosity in that column. It is the sixth of the eight. A claim about a share of a watercourse, renewed in public for a hundred and forty years by living men, and the thing that has been renewing it is the thing that has been killing the ground, and those are the same sentence.”

The minute-man did not write anything for a long time.

Then he said, “That is the question you put to a woman at a weir in the first rolling and then wrote in your own book and you have just answered it in a room with two people in it and no instrument at all.”

“The answer is not a good one.”

“No,” said the minute-man. “It is the true one, and I have spent twenty-two years learning what the difference is between a true one and a good one, and a true one is a thing you can be shown the second half of.”

They went at the fair copy for another hour and the last thing they did with it was arithmetic, because that is what the two of them do.

He could not get eight of eleven twice, and that is the plain finding, and he wrote it in his own book that night and it is all the ninth rolling did to him. Four attempts, four rooms, eight rollings, two sides of a line, eight of eleven out of the column every time. He could not get it a fifth time in a cold room with the county's own roll open on the table, and the reason is that the eight were six and two, and the column is a registrar's shape, and you cannot count a shape into a cause.

He wrote the number he could foot, because a figure in a book without a reason is a claim.

*Six of the eight fix a share of a watercourse and two of the eight fix a share of a sowing. Eleven counties, three with nothing in the leaf against them. I have had eight of eleven four times in eight rollings and I cannot get it a fifth time, and the reason is that the eight are not eight of one thing, and the reason the eight are not eight of one thing is that a registrar wrote six words in a hand, and a hand that writes six words is making a shape, and a shape is a category, and a category is the only reason a column has a count in it.*

And under it, with the hour, and then the reason, because he had learned in a room over a weigh-house that a figure in a book without a reason is a claim and a claim is a thing nobody can be put in front of.

*And the finding, which is not a cause: an oath sworn in public by men who are still alive is the only durable consent a rural county can produce, and it is the only document in the countryside that names a body, and a body can be served, and that is why the records gather where the oaths are. It is not because oaths cause anything. It is because an oath is a name.*

The minute-man read that second paragraph, in his own hand, out of a book in another county, at about the second hour of the third week of the ninth rolling of a county that is not on any map in this kingdom, and then he said the thing that is the last cost of this block and the first act of the next one.

“I am not going to put it in the roll,” he said.

“Why not.”

“Because the roll is of households in this county, and a column of eleven counties is not of this county, and the roll is the only book in this county that a person can be put in front of, and there is a rule in this county older than me and older than the stone, and the rule is that nothing goes in the roll that is not a household of this county, and I have kept that rule for twenty-two years and I have been tested on it once and I did not know I was being tested.”

“Who tested you.”

“The roll had a column once,” said the minute-man, “in the year before I came, and there were four names in it for four years, and the four names are men who had asked to have a second thing recorded. They are not in the roll now and the column is not in the roll now, and the two men who took it out were the two before me, and one of them told me why on the stairs in my first week. He said a roll of this county is the only thing in this county a person can be shown, and a second column is where a person goes to be got at, and he was not going to hand that to the next man in a book he could not serve. I have been here twenty-two years and I have never once wanted to put anything in that column and I have wanted to put something in it four times.”

He put his hand on the roll chest.

“This is not going in the roll and it is going in the book, with the hour, and against nobody, and the book is mine and it is the only book in this county that cannot be shown to a man, and if I put it in the roll then in nine years there is a column in a stitch-bound book in a county that has no office in it, and the column is about eleven counties and a claim about a share of water, and somebody four hundred years from now with a form in his hand will read it as a claim about this county, and he will be right about the form and wrong about everything else, and he will not be able to find me, because a stitch-bound roll in a county that is not on any map cannot be served and cannot be produced and I have just spent twenty-two years arranging that.”

The minute-man entered it, and read it back, and the entry is the longest in the book and it is the only one in twenty-two years that has a finding in it and no person in it, and it ends: *and the keeper has entered that he is not entering it in the roll, and the reason, and the reason is that a roll of this county is the only thing in this county a person can be put in front of, and there are already four names that went into a second column and came out of it wrong, and I know that because I was told on a stair in my first week and I have not forgotten it in twenty-two years and I am not going to forget it in the ninth year.*

Then he went down to the guild room, where Ferrow was doing something to a barrow with nine men, and told him all of it in about nine sentences in the doorway, because Ferrow had said the Water was coming on a morning in the seventh rolling and had been right, and a man who is right is owed all of a finding and not the interesting half.

Ferrow listened with his hands on the barrow and did not interrupt, and when it was finished he said, “Then we are six, and I have been six for nineteen years, and I did not know there were seven others. I would have gone and asked two of them and I would not have gone and asked the county of clay, because they would have told me the truth, and then I would have had to decide what to do about it, and a man who decides about a thing he has been keeping for nineteen years has become an office.”

“You might have come and asked me,” said Aren.

“I might have,” said Ferrow, “and you would have told me, and then there would be two of us in this county who knew, and two men who know is a thing a person can be put in front of, and one of them is a stranger from four hundred miles off with a bar he is not entitled to, and a man may be shown you.”

He put both hands flat on the barrow.

“Tell me the one thing in it that is about this county and not about the column,” he said. “Not the six and the two and not the dashing. What is the thing in it that means the ground under four villages is going to take four years to come down and that nothing anybody does in the ninth rolling will make it come down this year.”

Aren told him.

Ferrow said, “Then the third rolling was the right of it, and Ordmar Gace's proposal is a thing that can be broken, and I have been calling it dangerous for four rollings and it is the least dangerous thing in this county, and I will take that back in a room and not in a gateway, and I have not said it to him yet, and I am not going to, because a man who is told he is not dangerous by the person he has been frightened of for four rollings will believe it for about a day.”

Then the last hour of the ninth rolling, in a lane, with the four men of a fen yard off the north approach and the guild's nineteen out of the yard, and the Water four feet below where it had been and going down at a rate a boy could watch, and Thessa Wend's board four inches under the winter mark with a new reading in her hand that nobody had asked for, and four white villages with eleven places under water and nine gone, and the newest white in the county with no dark inch on the top of it, and a man at a sluice saying that one of the two will grow something in four years.

The arithmetic had stopped.

That is the ninth rolling of the Hearth Marches and it does not look like a thing. A man had done arithmetic on a column of eleven counties for eight rollings on two sides of a line, in four rooms, wanting a cause and a third and a half, and it stopped because six of the eight were promises about a share of water and two were promises about a share of seed, and because the county he was sitting in was the sixth of the six, and because an oath sworn in public by living men is a name, and a name is the only thing an office can act on, and the records gather where the oaths are because of that and not because of anything the oath does, and the three with a dash are the three where there was nobody left to ask.

He had come four hundred miles with a road to open and a book to make askable, and he would go back with three things he had not had when he came, and none of them was a road.

He had a number in a fair hand that eight thousand and thirty-one people in a hut and a field knew and had asked for, and nobody had ever asked him for it.

He had a refusal in a county four hundred miles off in nine words that had been read out in a room in front of about nine people and was in no book and could not be produced, and the woman who had copied it out of a minute had asked a bench for four lines and had paid a hundred and two pence of a bench's money out of a county that does not admit to the Crown that it exists.

And he had the finding, in his own book, with the hour, and a keeper of a book under a tithe barn had entered it with the hour against nobody and had refused to put it in the only book in that county a person can be put in front of, on the grounds that a second column is where a person goes to be got at, and had told him the reason out loud, in a room, twice, and had not improved on it.

The guild's first hand had told him, in a doorway, that the gateway was still standing and that a county which answers a child in a gateway and is then proved right for seven rollings about a different thing has not become harder to find.

And the county had a name, because it had a stone, and a name is the only thing that had ever made anybody come four hundred miles to it, and it was going to go on being a name, and the last thing a man can do for a county is take the name away.
