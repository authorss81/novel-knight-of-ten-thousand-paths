# Chapter 0145

The last day of the thirteenth rolling is the last day of the year, and the room under the tithe barn found that out about two hours before anybody had finished saying good afternoon to each other, and nobody made a speech about it, and the only person who put it in a book was the minute-man, and he put it in at the second hour with the hour against it, and then shut the book and took it out again about a quarter of an hour later because there was one more thing to enter.

The form is the county's own. It has been in Ferrow's book since a landlord asked this council to put a ditch on his account, and it is nineteen years old, and it is one page, and it is the only piece of paper in this county that anybody has ever used to refuse a thing.

It is ruled in seven columns and the seven of them are: *what is given. To whom, in the county's words. By whose hand. The day. The hour. A witness at this end. A witness at that end.*

That is seven and there are seven, and there is no column for an office, because a form that has a column for an office is a form that has been supplied with an authority, and there is no column for a vote, and there never has been one, and nobody in nineteen years has asked for it, and the reason it is not there is the reason this instrument works at all, which the minute-man said out loud before anybody had put anything in it.

“A vote is a thing a man carries into a room he is standing in,” he said. “There are about nine people in this room and nine of them carry one thing they could put in a column and this form has not got a space for it and does not need one, and in about nine years a man in another county with a printed space is going to fill one in, and this county is going to be a curiosity to him, and I have made my peace with that.”

Then he read out the eighth rolling, because it had to be read out before this one could be.

“In the second week of the eighth rolling, in this room, at the fourth hour, a man came up this stair with a bar and a sheet and asked this council to put its hand to nine days of the water, and this council declined, and the council's decline is in this book with the hour against it, and the reason is in this book in the words of the woman who gave it. And a fair copy of the whole entry is on the board under this bench and any person coming up two flights may read it, and it says that a stranger is of no council and holds no office in this county and may not be given one by this council, and that he asked to be put under the council's hand and was not.

“The same man is in this room. He was not asked for this. Nothing has been asked of him in four rollings and I have been the one who made sure of that, and I am entering today that he is here and that he was not asked and that what he is doing about it is not carrying anything up this stair.”

“I am giving the road away,” said Aren Kest, “and I want it in the county's form, and I want the day and the hour on it, and I am not going to argue for a minute about whether I am allowed.”

Munn said, “You are not on this council and you are not on the roll and you hold no office here and you may not be given one, and a form in this county is the council's to fill in and not a stranger's, and I will fill it in if you tell me what to put in it, and I will read it out, and I will not be hurried.”

“That is exactly right,” he said, “and I am not going to be able to help you with the last of that.”

What went into the first column took about forty minutes and it is in the fair copy in Munn's own words, and she read it out twice before she let the minute-man have it, and it is this.

The causeway and the waterworks above the market town, and the mile of boundary on both sides of it, and the trough, and the ditch at the head of it, and the four hundred and forty yards already opened in the fifth rolling, and the new work of the sixth, the eighth and the tenth, and the list of households to be helped with seed and with labour, and the seed in nine jars kept in a stone room behind a chapel.

“All of that, in one column,” said the bound, from the end of the bench, when she had read it the second time. “That is a mile of water and a board with twenty-nine thumbs on it and nine jars in a room.”

“It is what a road is in this county,” said Munn, “and if I put less in it I am making a smaller thing than I have got, and this council has spent nineteen years being careful about the difference between a thing it can refuse and a thing it cannot, and it is not going to be careful in the wrong direction on the last day of the year.”

The second column is to whom, in the county's words, and it took less time and it is where this county's year actually turns.

“To the guild,” said Munn, “at the guild's terms, and the guild will hold it, and the guild is a trade and the trade cannot be made to answer to this council and this council does not want it to be able to, because a thing this council can make answer is a thing this council has begun to govern, and it has said so in this book twice.”

“And the north side of the line,” said Aren. “The seed, and the ditch work when this council names a day, and the standing of a yard of a fen county four hundred miles off, at a yard's rate, and the yard is the yard of Tovan Kest of Weir Street, and the name is the yard's and not mine, and I am not the yard and I am not on it and I hold nothing in it and there is no vote of mine in it.”

The room did not make a sound. The minute-man wrote the name and Ferrow wrote the name in the guild's book, which is a different book, and neither of them looked up.

“And a witness at that end,” said the minute-man.

“A witness at that end is a yard,” said Aren. “Four men of it went out of this county at the start of the rolling with ninety-six bushels of salt in a handcart and the fact in their heads, and a yard's book holds things set and only a thing set, and a salt cart is a thing set, and a fact in a head that is going four hundred miles in a cart is a thing set. The four of them will enter it in their book, or they will not, and there is nobody in this room who can be told which, and there is no instrument in this kingdom that can make a yard's book in a fen county answer to this room, and I am not asking it to and I am not going to be disappointed in it.”

The minute-man wrote that down. He wrote it in a good hand and slowly and he read it back twice, and what he read back was that the far-end witness is a yard four hundred miles off which has been sent ninety-six bushels of salt and has not yet entered anything, and that nobody in this room can find out whether it ever does, and that the instrument is entered as being valid on a fact carried in a cart by four men and not on a signature.

“If that is not a witness then this form is not filled in,” said the bound.

“It is a witness,” said the minute-man. “A witness is a living person or a durable record that can confirm a promise was made and whether it was kept. I have been the keeper of this county's only record for twenty-two years and I will tell this room what a record is worth when it is in another county in a cart with salt in it, and it is worth what a cart is worth, and that is the honest figure and it is better than nothing, which is what the eighth rolling gave us.”

Then the witness at this end, and Munn asked for it, because a column that is not filled in is a hole in a document.

“I will not,” said Munn, “and here is why, and I have thought about it for four days and I am going to say the reason out loud instead of just doing it. The person who keeps this road is the guild. The person giving it away is a stranger with a bar. And the third person in this column has to be neither, and there is one woman in this county who has said out loud in this room that a person not on the oath is not a person this bench asks things of, and she said it about herself, and she asked for it to be entered, and it is in this book, and if I put a column full now and then do not put her in it, I have made a rule with an exception in it and the exception is the person it is about.”

The woman of about sixty on three hearths was on the sill with her basket on her knees, which is where she has been at every sitting since the eighth rolling, and twice before that she was outside that window with her ear to the boards, and she said one thing and it was four words and it was not a speech.

“I'll hold it,” she said.

Nobody improved on that either.

Ferrow raised the name.

He did it at about the fourth hour and he did it in the way he does everything, which is to put the objection on the table before the work is finished so that the work is not finished on top of it.

“The fair copy of this goes on the board under this bench,” he said, “and any person coming up two flights may read it, and it is going to carry two names with the same surname in it. The yard's first hand is Kest of Weir Street. The man standing at the end of this room is Kest, of a county four hundred miles off, on a bar he is not entitled to. In nineteen years I have never had two of anything in a book in this county that anybody could put side by side, and in nine years a man who has heard nothing and wants to know something will hear two and put them side by side, and he will be right to, and what he will make of it will be a thing neither of the two of them had anything to do with.”

He put both hands flat on the bench.

“I am not saying take a name out. I have no power to take a name out and neither has this council, and a name in a document that is true about a man is not this council's to rub out because the next name on the page is inconvenient. I am saying that it is going to be on a board in a county with no office and no writ and no bell and no schedule, in a county that is not on any map, and that in about ninety years there will be a man who can read both names and cannot find either of them, and that is a thing this council has done on purpose today with its eyes open.”

Nobody argued with him. Ferrow wrote the objection into the margin of his own book's page with the hour against it, because a first hand's objection to his own document is a trade matter and does not belong in a bench's minute, and the minute-man entered in the council's book that the first hand of the guild had objected, on the ground of the two names, before the fair copy was made, and that the council proceeded.

Then the minute-man asked the question, and he asked it in the voice he uses for facts, and it was the third time in nine rollings that he had put a question into this room that everything after it turned on.

“Who is this to be, in the book,” he said, and then stopped, and took it back. “No. That was the eighth rolling and I am not asking it again, because you answered it then and the answer is on the board under this bench and it has not changed. I am asking something else and it is smaller. The yard is Kest of Weir Street. You are Kest. Are the two of you anything to each other that this council is entitled to enter?”

“No,” said Aren Kest.

The minute-man wrote it.

“That is the answer of a man who is not going to improve on it,” he said, “and I am going to enter it exactly as given, with the hour, and I am not going to ask you a second time, and I am going to tell you why in the room instead of in a book, because you would otherwise spend the rest of the day wondering whether I was about to.

“Four rollings ago the woman on that sill asked this council for one thing and it was for a class of persons and not for herself, and it is in this book, and the thing it says is that a person not on the oath is not a person this bench asks things of. And you are not on the roll and not on the oath and you hold no office here and this council may not give you one, and so in the strict sense of what she asked for, there is a woman in this room who could not be asked about your family and a man in this room whom she has just protected from being asked about it, and neither of you has noticed that it happened in that order and I have noticed it and I am going to put nothing about it in the book.”

“You have put the first half of it in the book,” said Ferrow.

“I have put the first half of it in the book,” the minute-man agreed, “and I am not going to put the second half in, and the reason I am telling the room is that a keeper who enters the half of a thing that makes him look careful and withholds the half that makes him look something else is a clerk, and I have been a clerk for one afternoon in twenty-two years and I did not like it.”

Then he read the fair copy out in full, column by column, at about the fifth hour, and the room heard it, and the woman on the sill heard it, and nobody improved on any of it.

And the last thing on the sheet is not a column. It is a line under the rule, in the minute-man's own hand, because Munn wrote the body of the form and Ferrow checked the two figures in it and the last line is the keeper's, and he said where it came from.

“Coll asked me for it an hour ago and I have written it and I am going to say that I wrote it, because the county's own hand on this document is worth something to whoever reads it in ninety years, and it is not worth anything at all if a stranger wrote it and a keeper copied it.

“The road is not to be asked for back by anybody, and least of all by him.”

The room was quiet, and the Water was at the trough and going down, and the last day of the thirteenth rolling was the last day of the year, and the first sowing of the next one was within a week of it, and a mile of boundary had been re-cut in nine days by the people on both sides of it, and the salt under four villages had not moved, and a man in this county with a bar he was not entitled to had given away the only thing he had come four hundred miles to have and had done it in a form with seven columns and none of them for him.

The minute carries the hour. It carries no name.
