# Chapter 0137

He had a bar and a timetable and he came up the outside stair of the tithe barn at the fourth hour of the second week of the eighth rolling, and the room heard his boots on the boards before he got to the door, and eleven people looked up, and one of them had been listening for them since the third hour.

What had happened in the four days before the fourth hour was not a conspiracy and it was not a meeting. It was a county doing what this county had done for nineteen years, which is to say the ditchmen had come in on the day the Water went down and the forty of them had stood about the room under the barn with nothing to say, and Munn had given them the room and a bench and asked them whether they wanted one, and they had not wanted one and had sat on the floor.

There are about forty men in this county whose job is a length of water and whose wages have been paid out of a rent roll that has been going down for nine years, and they had four hundred and forty yards of ditch in the wrong place, a mile of boundary bank with a foot of new stuff on the downstream end of it, a deck at the bottom of a reach, four villages under eleven inches of water, and nine days of standing water in a trench above the town, and it was the second week of the eighth rolling and there are five rollings left in the county's year.

And the reason they were all in that room is not a decision anybody took. It is that a thing that has happened to everybody in a county at the same time gathers everybody in one room within about a day and a half, and this county had not had anything happen to everybody in one day and a half since the boundary was sworn the first time, and the eleven people on the bench had noticed it by the third hour and had not been able to get rid of it.

He came in with the bar under his arm and a sheet of paper in his other hand and did not stop at the bottom of the room, which was where a stranger stops, and he was not a stranger any more and had not been for seven rollings, and that was the difficulty.

“Munn,” he said. “I am going to take the water for nine days and I am going to do it on a timetable and there is a sheet of it here and every hour of it is on the sheet. I need the council to put its hand to the order so that every man in this room who is not on the council and not on the oath and not on the roll is working under somebody. That is all I have come for.”

Munn did not look at the paper. She looked at him.

“You know what you have just done in front of eleven people,” she said.

“I know what I have just done in front of eleven people,” he said.

Ferrow, at the end of the bench, said the sentence that made the room.

“He said in the fifth rolling that he would not come up those stairs with anything in his hands again unless a person on them asked him for it by name. I heard it. I heard it at a staging in the dark with a plane in his hand and I have not said a word about it since and I am saying it now because he has just broken it in a room and I am not going to let a room find out about it from the minute.”

The room did not make a sound. It is not a big sound, a room of fifty people not making a sound, but it is a sound.

Munn said, “Is that true.”

“It is true,” said Aren Kest.

“Did anybody in this room ask you for anything by name.”

“No.”

“Then say it to the room,” said Munn, “because the room is going to have to decide something in a minute and I am not going to have it decide it without knowing that the man with the bar came up a stair he promised himself he would not come up, with a thing he promised himself he would not bring, and that nobody asked him, and that the reason is a flood and not a person.”

He said it. He said it flat, in about six sentences, standing at the bottom of a room with a bar under his arm, and he said the four words and he did not give a reason, and the reason was in his own notebook in a coat four hundred yards away, and it was that no day had been named for a line and there was no line and there was no call and there was no second route, and a man who is not asked is not to ask.

The minute-man uncapped his pen. It was the first time in six rollings that Aren had seen him do that before anybody had spoken.

“Entered, in the second week of the eighth rolling, at the fourth hour, against no person: a man who has entered in his own book that he will bring nothing else to this chamber unless a person on it asks him for it by name has come up the stair with a bar and a timetable and has not been asked, and has said so in the room, and has given no reason, and the reason is not asked for.”

“It was not asked for,” Aren agreed.

“That is not the same as it not mattering,” said the minute-man, “and I am going to enter the second half of that sentence as well, and I would thank you not to argue with me about it, because you have spent seven rollings being careful in this room and I have watched every one of them and this is the first one that was not careful.”

Then he read the timetable out.

It was a good timetable. It was better than anything the council would have produced, because he had made it the way a bridgewright makes a sequence, in load order, working from the thing that must be out of the way first, and it had the standing water above the town first because the trench would fill and take the new head with it, and it had the trough in the second week because the trough is the only fixed point on the boundary and everything else is a consequence of the trough, and it had the four villages' ditches in the third week because a mile of boundary bank with a foot of new stuff on it is going to go somewhere in the fourth week of a rolling whether anybody is standing under it or not.

It had nine days in it and it had four places in it where the work stops and the men go home, and Ferrow read those four places and looked at them, and then read them again.

“You have put four stops in it,” he said.

“Four stops. If the water is above a mark at any of them, the men go home and it does not matter what the sheet says.”

“Whose mark.”

“The board at the weir. Thessa's board, in her own hand, read twice a day, and I have asked her for the readings and she has given me nine of them in her own hand and will not give me the book, and the nine of them are on the sheet with the days against them and there is no name on them and I did not ask for a name and I did not ask for the book.”

The room made a small noise, and it was not a noise of surprise, and the minute-man wrote nothing, and Aren went on and knew exactly what the noise was: four voices in a gateway six rollings ago, one of them his own, and the county had been right about the list and had been right about everything else and this was the first time any of them had heard a thing they had said out loud come back into a room as a fact.

“There is your gauge,” said Ferrow, “and there is your gauge, and there is a man of fifty-two in a yard four hundred miles off reading a board of paint in a country that is not his, and that is three ways to know the water in one room and not one of them is a man who can be asked what it means, and I want that in the minute too, and I want the man who wrote the sheet to say that he has just put three claims in a room where he promised to bring none, and I am not the man who will do that for him.”

“No,” said Aren. “Enter it exactly as I have said it, and put the hour, and do not put a name on the nine readings, and do not enter that I asked her for them.”

“It is entered,” said the minute-man, and it was.

Then the minute-man asked the one question that decided the eighth rolling, and he asked it in the voice he used for facts, and it was the question he had asked in the second rolling about a household that said nothing, and it was the question he had asked himself in the fifth rolling and answered in the yard, and nobody in the room knew that and it did not matter.

“Who is this to be, in the book,” he said.

Aren looked at him. The bar was under his arm and the sheet was in his hand and the room was full, and the answer to that question was not a matter of administration, it was the whole of the fifth rolling, and the man who kept the minute had known that before he asked.

“Under nobody’s name,” said Aren. “Under the council’s. It is the council’s order and I am the man who brought it up the stair and I hold no office and I am not on the roll and I have no vote, and if the minute says otherwise then in nine years there is a book in this county with a man in it who ordered a mile of bank, and the man in it is a stranger, and the next stranger will be a stranger with a paper, and a county that can be given an order by a document is a county that has an office, and this county does not have one and is not going to get one out of me in the ninth week of a rolling because I was on a staging at seven and the Water was in the old bed.”

Munn said, “That is the right answer and it is not the answer that will be read in this room tonight, and I want the room to hear both of those things said out loud by two people, and I am one of them and I am going first.”

She stood up, which she does not do.

“I have held this stone for nineteen years and I have said no to a bench twice over asking for it and no to a stranger offering to carry something into this room, and I have said in this room in the second rolling that there is a working that would take the water out of a ditch in an afternoon, and the man off the road refused it, and he gave a date and a community and a forged witness and did not explain it, and I took the refusal without asking him for the rest of it, and I was right to take it. And now the man off the road is going to stand at the bottom of my room with a timetable and ask me to put the council’s hand to nine days of the water of this county, and I am going to say no, and I am going to say why, and it is not because it is a stranger, and it is not because of the gauge, and it is not because of the four answers in the gateway, though I know about the four answers in the gateway, because I gave one of them.”

The room did not know about the four answers in the gateway. That was a thing said in a gateway to a child of eleven, and the minute-man had refused to enter it, and the woman of sixty on three hearths had heard every word of it through the boards of her own house.

Munn looked round the room and nobody said anything, and the boy who read the gauge was on the sill with his boots in his hand.

“I gave that boy an answer in the second rolling and it was true and it was in a gateway and it is in no book in this county, and I gave it because I have been asked nothing for fifty-five years and I wanted one true thing said out loud in my own county while I was still the one who renewed the stone. And I have thought about that every day since and I have come to the end of a thing about it, and the end of it is that a county which answers a child in a gateway has already said yes, and has said it to a person with no standing here, in a room it does not rule, and it cannot take it back, and it is not in a book, and the not being in a book is not a protection, it is only a way of not being able to produce it.”

Nobody in that room had expected that from Delya Munn and two of them looked at the slate woman, who is the only other person in that county who knows exactly how much is not in a book.

“So that is the reason,” said Munn, and sat down. “And I am not going to say the decision in my own words at the bottom of it, because I have been in this room fifty-five years and there is a form for it in Coll’s book and it was made the last time anybody in this county asked this council to take a thing onto itself, which is nineteen years ago and was a landlord, and he wanted a ditch put on his account, and the form has been in the book ever since with the year against it. You know the form. Say the form, Coll, and let the keeper enter it, and let the stranger watch a form be said in nine words in a county that has had nothing to refuse for nineteen years and now has something.”

The minute-man was writing, and his pen stopped, and he said, without looking up:

“Stop there and let me get this down, because you have said four things and two of them are reasons and the county has never once had a reason minuted by me and I do not know the form for it and I am going to enter the words and not a summary of them, and if the form is wrong this is the first wrong form in twenty-two years and I would rather it was wrong here than right somewhere I could not see it.”

Nobody in the room objected to that either, and Aren stood at the bottom of it with a bar under his arm and understood, standing there, that the room had not needed a debate and that he was not going to be given one, and that the one question in this room that had ever been worth asking had been asked by the man with the pen, and that the answer to it had been about a name in a book, and that his timetable, which was a good timetable and the best work he had done in seven rollings, was not a fact about the county's water and was a fact about a man from four hundred miles off, and that he was about to be refused for a reason he had given himself in a notebook in the fourth rolling, and that Munn had found it in five seconds and had not even had to be told what it was.

Ferrow had the book in his hands before Munn had finished, because he had been the one holding it, because a form is a thing a first hand keeps in a room as well as a keeper does, and he found the page and turned it round to face the table, and put his thumb under the line, and looked at the minute-man and not at Aren.

The minute-man read the line to himself first, because he is a man who reads a thing before he says it, and then he said it, and it took nine seconds, and he said it in the voice he used for facts, and the eleven people on the bench and about forty on the floor heard the same nine words at the same time, and not one of them spoke, and there was no argument, and it was not defeat, and he had it on the page before the last of it was out of his mouth.

“The council declines, and the guild will do it.”
