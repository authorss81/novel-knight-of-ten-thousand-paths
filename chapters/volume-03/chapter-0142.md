# Chapter 0142

The list was made in the eleventh rolling, three weeks before the day, and the reason it was made three weeks before the day is the only interesting thing about the timing.

A household that declares itself loosed from the boundary on the fourth week of the twelfth rolling is a household that has been told what it gets for it. If the list is made on the day, in the field, with forty men watching, then the household that would use it has to be told in a field, in front of its neighbours, and there are about sixty households on the roll in four villages and this county has never once asked any of them anything, and a county that has never asked is not a county that can ask at a gate in front of a crowd.

So the list was made in the eleventh rolling, in a room, with the door open, over three days, and nobody was sent to anybody.

The form is Ferrow's, and it is four inches deep and it is on a board, and it has three columns and none of them is a name.

*Roll number. The household's own mark. What the household would be helped with.*

That is all of it. There is a fourth line ruled underneath the last of the three and it is empty and it is not a heading, and the minute-man asked about it and Ferrow said it is for a household that would rather say its need out loud than write a mark, and about four of them will use it, and it is a line and not a word because a word on that board is a thing a person can be shown in nine years.

The list is keyed to the roll. The roll has the numbers and the roll is a stitch-bound book against the wall of the room under the tithe barn, and the roll is the only book in this county a person can be put in front of.

The minute-man raised that on the first day, in front of the council and the forty men on the floor, and he raised it flatly and without heat, and it is the third time in nine rollings that he has objected to a thing before it was done instead of after.

“Two books in one room,” he said. “This one and the roll. Anybody who has been in this room for a day and a half can go and get the roll out of the chest and put the two side by side, and a household on this board with a mark against it becomes a person with a name in it in about four minutes. I have kept that roll for twenty-two years on the ground that a second column in it is where a person goes to be got at, and I have never once wanted to put anything in that column, and I have wanted to put something in it four times, and this is the fifth thing and it is the first time I have wanted to put a hundred and thirty of them in it.”

“Then what do you propose,” said Munn, who had been in that room when he made the speech about the second column and had not forgotten a word of it and had never referred to it.

“I propose that we write it at the head of the list,” said the minute-man, “in my hand and the council's, that the list is keyed to the roll, and that both books are in one room, and that any person who has both can make a household into a person, and that this council has done it knowing, and has entered the knowing at the top of the thing where a man who does not like it can find it.”

Nobody argued with that either, and the knowing is at the head of the board in the minute-man's hand and it is the first line of it and it reads:

*Entered, in the eleventh rolling, at the head of this list: that this list is keyed to the roll of the households of this county, and that the two books are in one room, and that a man with both of them can make a household into a person, and that the council has done it knowing and enters the knowing here so that it is the first thing anybody reads and not the last.*

Ferrow looked at that for a while and then said, “That is my trade in one line and I would like it in the guild's book as well, with no names on it.”

The first mark on the list was put on in the second hour of the first day, by a man of sixty-eight who has been on the ground fifty-one years and who has a thumb in a flood year on the roll, written in a barn with the water under the door, and who has refused to have it corrected twice.

He came in and did not sit down and he did not speak to anybody except the minute-man, and what he said to the minute-man took about nine seconds.

“That mark is mine and it is not a good one and I am not having it argued about,” he said. “It is a thumb and it was taken in a barn in a flood year and I have not been to that barn since. What I want off this board is a mark of my own, in front of witnesses, that anybody can look at and see is not that one. I will not give my name. I do not want my name on it. I want a thumb.”

The minute-man read that back and put it in and did not improve it, and the man of sixty-eight put his thumb on the first line in wet chalk, and it came off the board on the shoulder of his coat as a mark that anybody could look at and see was not the smudge, and he said that out loud to the room, and about eleven people heard it.

Then he went out, and he did not say the four things he has said once to one man in a year, and nobody asked him for them, and they are in nobody's book by a stranger's own decision and there is no order out of this county that could produce them.

The second mark was the woman of about sixty on three hearths, and she came up the outside stair on the second day with her basket and put it down by the door where it had been the two times before, and she is the only person in this county who has ever been asked by this council for a thing and got it, and the thing she asked for was an entry about a class of persons and not about herself, and it is in the book.

She put a thumb on the ninth line and then asked a question, and the room went quiet, because nobody in that room had expected anything of that morning.

“Your list is keyed to the roll,” she said. “And the roll has got me on it with no oath mark against me, because I hold no water and I have never held water and I will never hold water, and that has been true for forty-one years and nobody has ever asked me what I swore. And I am not on the oath. So when I put a thumb on this board, what I am putting a thumb on is a list of households that are not on the oath.”

“Yes,” said the minute-man.

“Then the list is not a list of people who broke something,” she said. “It is a list of people who were never asked to swear it. And those are two different lists and this county has only got one of them and it has had it for nineteen years without knowing it had it.”

She put her hand flat on the board.

“I want the asking entered,” she said, “and I want it entered that the asking came after the mark. Not before. After. I have had a thumb on your board and you have asked me whether I want seed, and that is the first time in forty-one years that a bench in this county has asked me a question, and it is a seed question, and I am not going to be a woman who says thank you for it in a room, because you did not ask me to say thank you and I would not have.”

The minute-man entered it and read it back twice, and it is the second entry in twenty-two years in that book with a thing in it that a person asked to have put there about a class of persons and not about herself, and the first one was in the ninth rolling and is the reason this one was possible.

Twenty-nine.

That is the figure and it did not take a day and a half to arrive at. It took the three days of the eleventh rolling and it arrived on the second evening, and the minute-man entered it with the hour against no person, because a number of marks on a board is a number and not a household.

Twenty-nine of the fifty-eight on the roll. The two doubtful are the man of sixty-eight and a household of Sallow Marsh whose mark is a nail and whose name nobody has had in a book in nineteen years, and the nail-household put a mark on the fourth day and came back on the fifth day to ask whether the mark could be taken off, and it can, and it went, and the minute-man entered the asking and the taking off and the reason, and the reason is that a thing a household can take off is the difference between this and the other thing and everybody in the room knew it and one of them said so afterwards.

Ferrow had said in the ninth rolling that in five rollings, when a list did come along, half of four villages would sign whatever was read out to them because the council was right and not because anybody had deceived them. It has taken eight rollings instead of five and the number he gave is the number that happened, and he did not gloat about it and he did not say that he had been right, because the man who put the sentence on the stair in the ninth rolling also said in the same breath that the gateway was still standing and that a county answering a child in a gateway and then being proved right about a different thing for eight years had not become harder to find.

What he said, in the guild room, to four men and a stranger, on the second evening, was this.

“Twenty-nine. I said half. And here is what happened, entire, and I want it in a book somewhere, and I have never once known where to put a thing like it, so I will say it to a man with a board.

“Four of us told a boy of eleven in a gateway in the second rolling that there was no list. Four voices in a passage, and nothing written, and every one of us telling the truth, and the council has been right about that for eight rollings and has never been contradicted about it by anything. And then a board came along with three columns on it and none of them a name, and a household put a thumb on it of its own hand, and twenty-nine of them did, in a room, with the door open, and nobody sent anybody.

“Not one of those twenty-nine was deceived. Not one of them thought there was a list because we had lied to them in a gateway, because there was no list in that gateway and there is no list in this county now, and the thing on this board is a list of households to be helped and not a list of persons to be struck, and those are not the same thing and no child in this county was lied to about either.

“They did it because we were right. And a county that has been right in a gateway and is then shown a board with a thumbhole in it is a county whose people do not need to be frightened into a thing they have already agreed with. And that is not a victory and I am not going to call it one in a room, because a thumb on a board is a household saying it would like seed, and a household that would like seed is a household that is going to be on this ground in four years when the water has come down, and if the water does not come down then twenty-nine households will have been helped once and will still be on white ground, and this is the first thing I have said in nineteen years that I am certain of and have no idea what to do with.”

The woman of sixty-six came at the second hour of the third day, which is the hour she came in the eighth rolling, and she came to the room under the tithe barn and not to the guild room, and the minute-man read out loud, in full, the knowing at the head of the list before she said anything at all, which he had asked somebody to do and would have done himself if he had not been the one with the book.

She looked at the board for about as long as it takes to read a line twice.

“That is a good form,” she said. “Three columns and no name and a fourth line for a person who would rather say it. And the knowing at the head in the hand of the man who keeps it, which is the only hand in this county anybody can be got at and is also the only hand that cannot be got at, and both of those are true at once and it took me forty years to understand that about a keeper of anything.”

Nobody improved on that either.

Then she said the thing, and it took her about four seconds, and it was one sentence, and she did not say it again that day or the next day, and nobody in that room asked her a second question about it, and there is no version of it in any book in this county in her own words or anybody else's.

“A list of households to be helped you can take back, and a list of persons to be struck you cannot, because there was never anybody at the other end of it to take it back to.”

The room did what a room does. The minute-man's pen did not move, because he had asked himself once in nineteen years whether a thing said in this room was a thing he had heard from the person who said it, and he had, and it went in the guild's talk and the council's talk and the minute was a different kind of book, and this sentence belonged to the wall of a seed room and not to a minute and he said so out loud and did not write it down.

Afterwards she went up behind the chapel and into the stone room, and he followed her because eleven rollings ago she had given him a question nobody had a right to ask a stranger and he had asked leave to look at her wall, and the room had a hard floor now and no mud in it and nine jars on a shelf in the order they had stood on the shelf before the flood, because she had put them back in that order and had counted them, which is a thing she does.

He did not bring a chisel. He stood where a man stands in a room that is not his.

“Three things,” she said, “and then I am going to work, and you are not to write any of them down and you are not to say them to that room.”

“All right.”

“The first is the seed. Nine jars, and not in a wall. A wall is a good place for a seed and a terrible place for a keeper, because a wall does not let anybody take a jar out and a man in a hurry will not break a wall for nine jars, and I have had two answers built into this room over forty years and both of them are a man putting a thing into a wall and not being able to get it out, and I am not going to be the third one and I am not going to be a woman who says afterwards that the wall asked me to.

“The second is the name of it. When this county makes the thing it is going to make out of a list and a seed and a mile of ditch, I want to be the last keeper of a seed room and not the first keeper of a seed house. I have kept a room for forty years. A house has a door that a person comes to and asks a question at, and in about four years this county is going to have a door and I am going to be standing behind it, and I will have been the first one, and the first one is the one they send for when they want the thing that is in the room. I would rather be the last one. There is a difference and it is that the last one is not asked.”

“The third,” said Aren, “is the thanks.”

“The third is that I do not want the thanks,” she said, “and I am telling you now so that when it comes, which it will, in about four weeks, in front of the same nine people, you are standing in a room and you are not surprised, and if you look surprised I will know that you are managing a room and not a county, and I have not got four weeks of a man to give to that.”

She turned round and picked up a jar and put it four inches to the left, and then put it back where it was, because a thing in a seed room goes where the keeper's hand puts it and not where a visitor thinks it should go.

“And the marks on the wall are a different thing and I have decided nothing about them,” she said, without turning round, “and I will decide in the spring or I will not, and neither of those is yours to hear about yet, and you can come in the spring if you are still a man who asks leave.”

“I will ask leave.”

“You will ask leave and you will get it,” she said, “and that is nine words more than a stranger gets in this county in a year, and I have not decided that it is a kindness to you and I am not going to.”

He went down the lane from behind the chapel and the Water was four feet below where it had been and going down at a rate a boy could watch, and Thessa Wend's board was four inches under the winter mark with a reading in her own hand from the morning that nobody had asked for, and the four villages were white the way they had been white, and one thumb's worth of chalk on a board in a room under a tithe barn was not going to make any of it grow anything, and nobody in that county believed it would, and twenty-nine households had put it there anyway.
