# Chapter 0130

The fourth rolling came in with the Water still high and the council’s book holding three things it had never held before: a page in the gauge woman’s hand, a question about sixty households raised and not decided, and a tenant’s proposal entered without opinion.

Aren walked the bank alone in the early light, thinking of Ash. In that clay county a person struck was removed from a count, alive and employed and not consulted, and the fourth of its five terms said a person struck might answer, and answering restored. He had seen men answer and return. He knew the instrument. The Marches were not Ash, but the shape of the unasked pressed on him, and he resolved to offer what he knew, though offering another county’s remedy to this one had failed him before.

He asked leave at the sitting and got it. He stood where Gace had stood and spoke carefully.

He offered the fourth term of Ash, that a person struck might answer, and answering restores. He said any household not on the oath might answer for itself before the council, and answering would restore it to the oath’s protection, and the restoration would be entered with two witnesses whose names stood in the book. He laid it out as a bridgewright lays out timber, piece by piece, and sat down.

He knew it was wrong within a breath and a half. The knowledge arrived the way a misstep arrives on a plank, before thought. A person in Ash was removed from a count. A person in the Marches was not on an oath. One was a wound and the other a promise, and a promise could not be mended by answering. Answering belonged to a roll that could be read twice. The Marches had a stone and a ditch and forty men, and no roll for an answer to land in. He had offered the wrong instrument, and the room knew it before he finished explaining why.

Munn said gently that the council thanked him for the thought and would not take it, because taking it would make the oath a count, and a count was a thing an office could hold, and this county had no office and would not make one to hold itself. Ferrow said the guild would not witness answers because witnessing belonged to the oath’s own men. The slate woman asked whether answers would be entered on the roll with a mark, and answered herself that there was no mark for answering, only for swearing, and the sheet had no room for a sixth space. The minute-man entered the offer and the declining as facts, with the hour.

Aren inclined his head and withdrew it, and the sitting moved on. Nobody reproached him. That evening he wrote in his notebook that he had attempted the one conversion the rolling allowed, from a county of counts to a county of promises, and had been refused, and the refusal was correct, and he would not attempt it again. He wrote that a register was what a county had instead of a refusal, and that he had no register to offer these people and they had not asked for one.

Nine days later a fair copy came down the line, on a scheduled day, in a hand that was not a clerk’s. It was from the woman four hundred miles off who kept the road at the far end, and it was two lines long.

The first line gave the reading of the line and the hour it was kept. The second line said: we did not lose our register, we lost our refusal, and a register is what you have instead.

He had read that sentence once before, in a fair copy in a room over a cooper, without knowing where it came from. Now he knew. It belonged to a woman of about sixty on three hearths in a clay county, entered against her name though she had no trade for it, and it had travelled four hundred miles to correct him. He folded the leaf and put it in his notebook, and told nobody in the Marches what he knew, because the county did not know he knew, and knowing was his burden and not theirs.

The rolling closed with the proposal still on the table, the jars still sealed, the sixty still unasked, and the ditch still eleven yards off the trough. He stood at the slot after the sitting and listened to the barn settle, thinking that the attempt had been his and the correction had come down a line, and that the distance between those two facts was his schooling.

He spent the evening with the yard’s men, helping plane a sill for the staging, saying nothing of fair copies or far counties. The drawknife man told stories of fen work, of fortnights lost to weather and wages footed in pence by lamp-light, and the younger men listened the way apprentices listen, storing method. Aren planed and listened, content for an hour to be hands and not reasons.

Later alone he opened his notebook and wrote the refusal of conversion plainly: no rolling would be rendered into days of his own calendar, no count of rollings into figures of months, because the two frames shared nothing and paper could not make them share. The nine days to the fair copy were nine days on the line’s own keeping, and that was all any book could honestly hold.
