# Chapter 150

The twenty-fourth of Hearthmonth, YR 313, was a Friday and it was not a market day, and the fourth hour is the hour the river is in its bed and the deck is dry under the third bay, and about forty men are on the Sedge Reach with stone in four carts, and a road warden of the Merefen bridge guild is standing on the deck of the third bay with a tool bag at his feet, counting load points.

He counts load points because he counts load points. Four in the toe of the north pier, four in the toe of the third, two under the cutwater, and he does it twice to see whether he has got the same four, and he has, and there is no book in this county that it goes in.

The stone went down on the twenty-first and the twenty-second and the twenty-third and was being pointed on the twenty-fourth, because pointing is the one job on a bridge that is never finished on the day it is begun. It has rained about four times in three weeks and the lime is standing in the courses and the fourth of the eleven tons is in the toe of the north pier and the rest of it is spread along the cutwater.

He got up onto the deck off the gravel in three moves instead of two, which is a thing nobody in that yard can see, and he stood on the third bay for about a minute and a half and then came down and sat on the parapet with the tool bag between his boots and let the man who keeps the eels go past with a barrow.

A stick is in his hand. It is a ferrier's stick, ash, four feet nine, with a rubber ferrule, and it was cut out of a fence rail in a county that is not on any map, and he does not need it for anything. He cannot put a bar in a joint with it. He cannot lean on it, because leaning on it puts the weight through the wrong hip, and that is what the stick is for now, entire. It is for four hundred yards of road.

The guild's man with the levels found him at the ninth hour by the cutwater with a rule in his hand, which is what he was there for, and he had it out and had it against the string course before he said good morning, which is a thing he has done in this county for about two years.

“Your legs are not the same,” he said.

“No.”

“You want a figure.”

“I do not want a figure,” said Aren Kest.

“You want a figure and you are asking me to make it, and I am going to say no in about nine seconds, and I would like to say no first and then the reason, and if you want to argue with the reason that is a different nine seconds and you can have those as well.”

He put the rule down on the parapet.

“No,” he said. “And the reason is not that it is a bad leg and it is not that a figure would be wrong. The reason is that I keep my own book. I have kept it for two years and the office has paid me six pence twice out of a box for two written reports and I put both of them in my own book with the hour on them, and I have never put one of them in anybody else's book, and that is the only reason there is a man in this county who can be shown a hole under a bridge on a date.

“If I measure you and write it down anywhere, then in a year there is a figure of your leg, and in about four years somebody asks where the figure came from and the answer is a man with a level, and the answer is me, and I am not going to be the man somebody can go and ask. And in about nine years a surveyor from the county will come with a chain and want a section, and a section wants a figure, and if there is one of mine in a guild's book he will use mine, because it is there and it is nearer. And then it is not a man who wrote down a thing he saw on a morning. It is a decline. And a decline is a survey. And a survey goes in a form.”

He picked the rule up and put it away.

“I have one of those already,” he said, “in a file two hundred miles off, in a column with a head on it, and I did not write it and I have never seen it and I cannot get at it, and it is the reason I do not do this for a friend any more than I do it for a stranger.”

“All right,” said Aren Kest. “Thank you for the nine seconds.”

“I have not said the reason for the nine seconds,” said the guild's man with the levels. “The nine seconds are because I would have to look at it twice to be sure, and a man who has to look at a thing twice before he says a number is a man who is about to put a number in a book, and I do not want to be that man on a Friday. That is all of it. I would have said no in about four if it had been a Tuesday.”

The hall over the wharf at about the second hour, and the reeve had a sheet in her hand, and she had it in her hand for about a minute and a half and then she put it back inside her coat, and the bench-clerk entered what happened, in the ordinary form, with the hour.

The number is four.

She came up two flights of that stair on the twenty-fourth of Hearthmonth with the fifth asking in her hand, and she did not put it, and the minute says so in her own words and at the hour, and the minute says that the reason is not hers and is a sentence a carpenter said in this hall in the fourth of Frostmonth, and the minute carries the sentence, word for word, and there is no second version of it in this county or in any other and there is not going to be one:

*That a bench asked three times and saying yes three times is not a bench, it is a subscription.*

“And I want the number entered as well,” said Reeve Aldis Fenn, from the end of the table, “and the number is four, because the fifth is in my coat and is not on that table. I have said yes four times and this bench is not a bench and I would like that on a page as a fact and not as a complaint, because a bench of nine that knows what it is and writes it down is the only kind there is and this one is the only one I have ever sat on. And I would like it entered that I did not put the fifth, and that the reason is in my own minute, and that a bench that has been asked the same thing five times has not been asked five things.”

The bench-clerk entered it and read it back and nobody improved on it. The chest has two marks in it and it has had two marks in it since the ninth of Fallowmonth, and the asking is for money for a crossing that this bench has no power over and cannot have any power over while the trust is in abeyance, and about forty years of that and no answer.

He was in the room. He is on the roll. He has a vote and he did not use it and nobody asked him to and nobody has asked him anything, and a man on a roll of nine with a vote and not an office is everything the county has ever given him, and it is not nothing, and it is not enough to build anything on, and he has never once said so out loud in this hall and he is not going to start.

Four hundred yards up the road from where the hall sits is the Old Toll crossing, and it is in municipal trust, and the trust has been in abeyance since before the bench existed, and the tolls on it are suspended by an instrument a court has found lawful and has said it has nothing further to say about at any time, and so the crossing has no owner and no money and no seat on that bench and no glass in the sill and no third seat, and it is four hundred yards up the road.

He walked it at about the fourth hour, on the left, with a stick, at about the pace of a man who is not late for anything.

There is a dressed block in the abutment and forty yards of tarred hemp, put there on the fourth of Hearthmonth of the year before by four men and a boy, and a road warden who had said in a hall that he would come when he was wanted.

At about the fourth hour and a half the bell went.

It is a bell with a band of forty-one rivets round it and a dull red seam in the metal where the band was closed, and it is nine stone of green-black bronze, chest-high, split lip to shoulder, and it is rung by other hands.

On the near abutment there is a boy who is seventeen now and has been the steward's apprentice for two years and three months and is still not good at it, holding one end of a line, and beside him a carpenter who is a hand and not a keeper and has a bar he is not using. They rang it because that is the arrangement. The arrangement has been a boy and a ferrywright since the twenty-fourth of Fallowmonth and a boy and a carpenter since the ninth of Hearthmonth, and there is a page at the head of the yard's book for the week with the hour against it in a hand somebody would know, and nobody wrote a word in it about who rang the bell, and nobody is going to.

The arrangement is not his. Nothing in the sound of it is his. He is twenty-five yards from that bell and he has not rung it in four years and he did not ring it in the ninth month of the year before either, and the fourth hour and a half on a Friday is a bell in a fen town and it is not for anybody.

And at the far end of the line, four hundred miles off, on a coping on a river this county does not have, there is a tile, a hand's width, with a rope on it in two loops and no yoke and no headstock and no striker, carrying a device nobody in this county has ever been told the name of, and the county's own kiln's mark on the back, and its maker has been dead since the eighth of the last rolling, and a man of sixty-four swept round it for forty-one years and wanted it lifted, because a lifting is a job and a taking away is a throwing away, and the coping is the county's and it is not his to lift.

A Monday is the day that end keeps. Today is a Friday, and the two of them are one fact and they are not joined in a book, because a book is a place where things get joined whether they were joined or not, and so nothing was struck at the far end of that bell this afternoon, and the coping has a tile on it and the tile has a rope on it and nobody has looked at it, and the road to that end of the line is not there, and there has not been any road to it since the middle of this year, and a woman who keeps that end has not been asked a question about it in about two years and has not asked one either.

He stood at the end of the line.

That is all of it, and it is what the last two volumes of this case have been about, and there is no instrument in this kingdom that can make a road answer a man, and there is no way to call one, and a man who wants to know whether a road is there goes and stands at the end of it in the rain on a Friday afternoon and waits, and if nobody comes that is a fact and not a failure, and if somebody comes that is also a fact.

Then he went back down four hundred yards to the water, because the stone is going down and he is on the roll of a guild of nine, and there were about forty men on a bridge in the rain and a bench that had been asked five times.

Up two flights of a stair over a weigh-house there is a room with a door and a number, and in the room there is a day-book that nobody can serve, and in the day-book there is an entry made on the twentieth of Hearthmonth against nobody and against nothing, and the entry is that the not asking has been entered so many times in a year that a keeper in nine years will count the entries instead of reading them, and that a keeper of records enters the not writing because the not writing is the part of it that is a decision, and that the day this office wants to stop entering it is not going to be the day anything has changed, and that it is going to be the day nobody has asked anything for a long time, and that she will not know the day it happened.

There are nine of those entries this year. She has entered nine of them and she has said out loud, in advance, more than once, that she is not going to enter a tenth, because entering a tenth would be entering a question, and because a rule about what is written down is not a rule about what is done.

There is a fair copy in the room as well, four hundred and eight words and a working, and anybody could check it line by line with nothing but that sheet and about four years, and there is one line in it that is a number and not a name, and the line is four men and none.

And the printed figure on the leaf of returns with a printed three weeks at the head of it has not changed. It has been three weeks in an office's hand for two years and it is three weeks, and the office still cannot say whose figure it is, and no one in this room has said the word about it that a person says about a figure that was out by ninety-six days, and the reason nobody has said it is that a figure is a figure and the man who wrote the other one is a man in a county four hundred miles off with a stone in a field and a book that cannot be served, and a warning that is wrong is a warning that somebody wrote.

He came up the two flights at about the eighth hour on the twenty-fourth with a tool bag and a stick and did not knock, because the door of a body corporate is a door anybody may knock on and his is not the only knock.

“Two figures for the book,” he said. “Both of them are the same as last time. The print is three weeks. It has not moved. The office cannot say whose it is. And I am not going to tell you what the number of days is this time because you have it, and you have had it since the summer, and you told me on the fifth that you did not have a figure for the absence to put it against and that is the only sentence anybody has said to me about it in four months, and I am not going to improve on it by being right about it twice.”

She put her pen down.

“Then the not asking is the tenth,” she said, and stopped, and picked the pen up again, and did not write it, and neither of them said why, and it is still the ninth, and the ninth was entered on the sixteenth of Longlight four months ago, and it stands.

“There is a stick in this county that does not have to do with a bridge,” she said.

“There is a footbridge of two bays and five yards in a county that is not on any map,” he said, “and the near bay hangs four inches and the far bay is sound, and a man stood on it for nine hours and came off it with one leg, and he is standing in your doorway now, and I have a number for the days and no figure for the leg, and the figure would be yours to keep and it is not a fact about a person and I am not going to put it in this book.”

She did not ask him about the morning. She asked him whether the tool bag was full, and he said it was, and she said that the man in the guild room had been asking after the levels and that the levels had declined, and he said he knew, and she said that the levels had told the bench-clerk the reason in nine seconds and that the bench-clerk had entered it, and he said that was the right thing to have done, and she said that she had not known it was entered until this afternoon and that a report in that county takes about three days to come up a stair and that this one had taken a year and a half.

The rain went on the window. A room over a weigh-house in a fen town, a fair copy anybody could check line by line, one line in it that is a number and not a name, and a keeper of records who is at the end of what a day-book can do for her and who has not once in four years and a half asked a man a question in a doorway, and the ninth is entered and there is no tenth and there is not going to be one in this year.

Four hundred miles off there is a county of clay with a roll of nine hundred and forty-one households that a man on a coast bought for a farthing of salt and does not know what he has bought, and a road that is not there, and a woman who keeps the end of it, and a tile on a coping with a rope on it and no yoke that nobody has looked at.

The road up from the water is four hundred yards long and it is there.
