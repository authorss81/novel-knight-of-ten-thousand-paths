# Chapter 0135

The first hour of the Water in the old bed belonged to the seed room, and the second hour belonged to the lanes of Sallow Marsh, and the rest of it, for two days and most of a night, belonged to the four lowest villages and to about two hundred people who had never in their lives seen a river do anything but be a river.

The seed room had a foot of water in it at the sixth hour and a woman of sixty-six in it up to above the knee with a lamp held over her head, moving nine sealed jars one at a time from a shelf to a higher shelf, and there was no shelf above the shelf.

She had been in the room forty years and she had a plan for it, and the plan was not a plan for a flood, because there had not been a flood in forty years, and the plan was a plan for a leak. A leak comes from above and a leak has a place. A flood comes up under a floor and has no place, and there is nowhere in a nine-foot room to put nine jars that is not the floor.

At about the seventh hour, with a foot and a half in the room and the door under, four men came along the back of the chapel with a plank and got the low door open with a shoulder, and the woman of sixty-six made them take the jars out one at a time and put them on the chapel step, and the nine jars went up on the step in the order they had stood on the shelf, and she counted them, which is a thing she does.

The chapel step is a foot and a half off the ground and the room's floor is three feet off it on the inside of the third course, and nine jars of wheat that has not been grown in this county for forty years went up onto a chapel step at the seventh hour of a morning in the wrong part of the year, and on the third trip in one of the four had a lantern, and put his hand on the wall to find his way, and did not know there was a floor, and went down on his hands and knees the way a man does in a flooded room, and came up standing on a floor three feet off the ground with the nine jars over his head, and he told nobody about it for the rest of that year, and the only person he ever told was a man with a bar, standing on a causeway deck about an hour later, in about nine seconds, and he did not know the man's name either and the man did not ask.

And the Water came into the seed room at about the ninth hour, and it came in the way it came in everywhere, which was all at once and without noise, and it filled a stone room that had been built up twice by a woman with her own hands, and it did not read the three marks on the outside of that wall and could not.

The nine jars stood on a chapel step at the ninth hour with a foot of water in the room behind them and the ground all round that chapel gone, and nine sealed jars of a wheat that has not been grown in that county for forty years were the only thing in four hundred yards that was above the Water, and there was a woman standing over them who had put them there herself and would be the last of them to leave the room.

What came up next was the lanes of Sallow Marsh, and it came up them from the ditch side and not down, and that is the fact that four hundred people in that county could not have told you before it happened and can tell you now, and it is the reason a trough that fills from the top is not a trough any more.

The ditch had a head at the trough for the first time in a hundred and forty years and the head had taken all of the trough’s water in a morning, and by the seventh hour the trough was a hole with the Water standing in it, and at about the eighth hour the Water began to come out of the trough into the boundary bank on the low side, which is a mile each way from the trough and four inches deep, and at about the ninth hour a mile of boundary bank four inches deep had a foot of moving water on the inside of it and eleven inches of standing water on the outside of it, and the outside is the four villages.

The lane of Sallow Marsh is the lowest lane in that county and it is a good two feet below the field it comes off, and at about the tenth hour the water came up the lane from the ditch side at a walking pace, and the boy who reads the gauge was on the causeway at the time and everybody else in the lowest village was in it.

Nobody died.

That has to be said plainly and it has to be said first, because in the rest of this account there is a boy of fifteen in the water for nine minutes and it is the only thing in two days in that county that came near it, and there were two hundred people on that water and all the difference between two hundred and one is not luck.

The four lowest villages went under between a foot and three feet in eleven hours and the water stood in the lanes of Sallow Marsh for two days and went down over four, and the lowest field at Sallow Marsh was under nine inches of it for three days, and the salt came up in the new land. That last is the one that matters and it is the one nobody saw, because it cannot be seen in a flood: a mile of boundary that has had eleven inches of standing water on the outside of it for two days is a mile of ground that has been washed, and the washing takes the top inch and a half of soil off it, and under the top inch and a half in those four villages is salt that has always been there, and the salt comes up out of the bottom of a scrape the way water comes up out of a floor, and the ground that was the colour of a bone before the Water came is the colour of a bone after, and it is worse, and it is worse because the flood took away the thin dark ground that had been forming on top of the salt for four years.

The woman of about sixty on three hearths had let her lowest hearth lie and sown barley on the middle one, and the middle one was under a foot and a half of the Water of the lane for a day and a half, and she stood in her own doorway at the fourth hour of the second day and looked at a lane she had walked every day for forty-one years with a foot of brown water standing in it, and her whole face did one thing and then went back to being a face.

“My grandmother’s hearth is the other one,” she said, to nobody, in her own doorway. “Four years I have had the barley in and the beans off it, and the beans were the only thing on that ground worth eating, and the beans are in the lane.”

Nobody in that lane had ever asked her what she swore, and the flood did not ask her either, and the four hundred yards of ditch above the town that carried it all down was the ditch that nineteen men and four men out of a fen county and a boy with a pole had put back in the trough in three days in the fifth rolling, and it had done exactly what it was cut to do.

The boy of fifteen went in off the deck at about the sixth hour and a half.

The deck had come off its abutments at the ninth hour of the previous morning and had gone the length of itself and taken the rail and the bar and the four men who were on it, and by the sixth hour and a half of the second day the new deck was lying where it had stopped, which was eleven yards downstream and canted, and the reach of it that was above water was the third bay, and the boy was on it because he was fifteen and there was a rope and eleven men and a man on a bank in the wrong month telling a fifteen-year-old to go and see if the third bay's foot was still in its mortise.

He went out along the deck on the boards. The far abutment was under nine inches. He got to the third bay, got down on his hands to look at the foot, and the boards under him were not boards any more, they were a raft, and the raft had been in a river for a day and a half and a raft in a river is a thing with a man on it, and it went.

He was in the water about forty yards and the current off a bend at a mile and a half with a foot and a half of the Water on it in the wrong part of the year will take a man at three feet the way a man walks.

He went under twice before anybody on the bank had decided what to do about it, and the second time he came up he was not shouting, and the man who noticed it was not Ferrow and not Aren and not the four men of the fen yard, and did not shout either, and did what men do on a bank in a flood, which is to look at the next man.

The next man went in off the near bank, which is a foot and a half of mud and then a foot of water, and a man who is going into a bend of the Water in that condition is going to have about four seconds of useful life, and the four men of the fen yard had been in a fen for twenty years and knew it, and the drawknife man was the one who knew it. He went in off the near bank on his feet and did not dive and did not swim up, and went down the way you go when you are saving somebody rather than going somewhere, which is head up, feet down, one arm, and he reached the boy in about nine seconds and had another eleven to get him to the near bank, and the boy was fifteen and heavy with river and there is no way to get a fifteen-year-old out of a bend in a flood without taking most of the water on yourself.

The woman of sixty-six rang the chapel bell.

Nobody had thought about the bell. It is a chapel bell and it is rung for a burial and a wedding and a saint's day, and it has not been rung in a hurry in this county in living memory, and the rope is in the porch and the bell is a small one and a woman of sixty-six who has not rung it in nine years can ring it, and she rang it nine times and stopped.

She is the only person in the county who has ever said that the number of times she rang that bell is a number and not a ceremony.

“Nobody was counting anything,” she said afterwards, to Ferrow, in the yard, with her hands ruined. “There was a boy in the water and four men on a bank and a thing in the Water with a door in it, and a bell is the only thing in this county that can be counted without anybody having to stop doing what he is doing. I rang it nine times. And the ninth time he came out, and I do not know that it was the ninth time that did it, and I am not going to tell anybody that it was, and if you put the number of times I rang a bell in a book I will find the book and I will not be pleasant about it.”

Ferrow said, “Nothing goes in a book.”

“It goes in mine,” she said, “and mine is not a book. It is a column of a gauge and a thing about a floor and the number of times a bell was rung, and it has no head on it and nobody has ever been asked to read it, and I have been the only person who can be asked about it for eleven years, and that is my whole holding in this county and I am not giving it up to a man with a pen in a county of no office.”

The boy was nine minutes in the water and the number is nine and it comes from three independent places and none of them is a record.

The first is the chapel bell, and the woman who rang it will not have the number written down and has said so in a yard to the first hand of the bridge guild.

The second is Ferrow, who had a watch on a thong under his shirt and who was standing on a bank with his back to about nine other men and who could not swear to nine seconds of that morning. “I will tell you what I will swear to. I will swear that I did not feel the watch on me for about half of it and that the half I felt, the watch said eight minutes, and that when I looked at it after it said something else, and that a man who has been on a bank for four hours in a flood is not a man whose watch is a record of anything. Put nine in and be told I gave you nine and know that I did not.”

The third is the boy, who was asked, and who said four minutes, and who was asked how he knew and who said that he did not know and that he had been somewhere else and that nobody was counting where he had been.

Then the third person who gave nine was a man with a boat who had come down the lane from the north village in the second hour of the second day and who said he had been in the Water off the bend from about half past five and had come in and out of it four times and that the last time he came in the boy was in and the next time the boy was not, and that it is nine minutes because he was in there twice and counted.

The number that went into the council's minute was nine. It is the only number in this account that four people gave and the only one that is not in a book anywhere in the world except that one, and it is in the council's minute in the county's own form and reads:

*Entered, in the third week of the seventh rolling, against no person: a boy of fifteen was in the Water off the old bend for about nine minutes and was got out on the near bank, and no name is entered, and the reason no name is entered is that the man who went in after him did not give his name to anybody, and the council has not asked for it.*

And the minute-man said the thing about it that he said about a child's question in a gateway two rollings before, and he said it to the room and not to the man who had gone in, and he said it in the plain voice he kept for things.

“I entered a silence once in the fifth rolling because a man told me on the chamber steps that a household had said nothing at all, and I entered it and I have thought about it every week since. This is the same entry and it is easier, because nobody said nothing this time. Somebody did something and I do not know who, and I am going to write down that I do not know who, and a man who writes down that he does not know a thing is doing the entire trade of this office in one line, and if it is wrong then it is wrong in a way nobody can be got at.”

The man who went in after the boy was not named, and he was not asked, and he was not asked by the minute-man or by Ferrow or by Aren or by the woman of sixty-six or by the boy, and the reason he was not asked is the one that a man who has been in a county for six rollings and has not been asked one question would give if anybody in the world put it to him, which is that he was not asked, and that a man who is not asked does not volunteer, and that the arrangement is the only thing he has in this county.

He did not say it. He was asked nothing and he said nothing and he went back to the staging at seven the next morning, and he worked the north approach for three weeks, and a man who went into a bend of the Water after a boy of fifteen and came out of it with the boy is a man who does not go and stand in a gateway and be thanked, and nobody thanked him, and nobody mentioned it, and the two of those are the same arrangement as the one that keeps his name out of every book in that county, and he is a man who has never in his life asked for a name to be written down.

The crew's ninety working days ran out in the fourth week of the seventh rolling, and the four men of the fen yard stayed.

Nobody asked them to and nobody paid them and nobody told them to go. What happened was that the work was not finished, and a yard's rule is that the yard does not leave a job, and the yard was in a county four hundred miles off with its tools and its own keep and no money coming in after the first week of the third rolling, and a man who has come four hundred miles does not walk back four hundred miles over a river in spate with a wagon that has no second axle worth the name.

So they stayed, and the guild found them beds in the tool store, and the council found them work at the guild's rate on the council's account, and the rate is eight pence and the money is not there, and the minute-man entered the four men as four men of a yard in the county's own hand, on the council's account, at the guild's rate, from the fourth week of the seventh rolling, and he entered the days as they went, and by the end of the eighth rolling there were four men on the council's account in a book in a county that has never had a man on its account from outside it in nineteen years.

“I am going to tell you what this is and I am not going to be able to make it come out right,” the minute-man said to Aren in the room under the chamber, with the book open. “They are four men and there is no sum against them, because I cannot foot a sum for men who are not being paid, and a man who is not being paid is not a debt, it is a favour, and this county has never had a favour in it in nineteen years that anybody can point at. So the book has four men in it and no figure, and in nine years somebody will read that and find four men who were in this county and cannot say what they were owed or to whom, and I have had the choice of writing a figure I cannot foot or writing nothing, and I wrote nothing, and the nothing is in the book with the hour against it, and the hour is the hour I noticed.”

“That is the right judgement,” Aren said.

“It is the only one I had at the time, which is a sentence I have said in this room before this rolling and I am not going to enjoy saying it twice.”

The flood went down over four days, and on the second of the four the Water was back in the channel it had been in before, four feet lower and going down at a rate a boy could watch, and the old bed was a mile of wet gravel with a line of dead alders along it that was a foot taller than it had been that morning, and the deck was eleven yards downstream and canted, and eleven men and four men out of a fen yard and a stranger and a boy with a pole spent all of the second day getting eleven jars off a chapel step and into a room that had a foot of mud in it and a floor three feet up inside a third course.

The woman of sixty-six would not go into the room until the water had gone, and then she went in and looked at the wall, and the plaster on the inside of the third course had come away in four places in a sheet, and the marks were in the stone behind it, three of them, in three bits of tin, and she put her thumb on the low one for a while.

“The man on the boards,” she said, to nobody, in a room with a foot of mud in it. “The first one. The one that came in with the lantern. He did not know there was a floor, and he put the lantern through it, and he did not know it was there and he stood on it.”

She washed her hands in the bucket because there was nothing else to wash them with, and she did not say anything else about it, and she has not decided anything yet, and she said so when the drawknife man asked her in the yard in the fifth week, in front of Ferrow, in about nine words.

“I am not deciding anything this month. I said in the first week of the fourth rolling of the year before that I would decide in about a year with the water down, and the water has been down four days, and a woman who decides in four days what she gave herself a year to decide has decided about the deciding and not about the thing. Ask me in the spring.”
