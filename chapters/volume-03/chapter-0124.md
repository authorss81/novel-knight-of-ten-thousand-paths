# Chapter 0124

The second rolling opened with rain and a full room. The bench over the barn had asked for the gauge woman’s nine readings, and the slate woman read them aloud in Thessa’s hand, weir post and Sallow foot, two columns, seven days. The minute-man entered that a page in her hand lay in the keeping of the council for the first time in eleven years, and that the council had not asked for the book and would not.

Delya Munn sat with her shawl round her shoulders though the room was warm. She had renewed the stone in public in a year anybody there could name, and her reason was a grandmother’s name, and she was right about the history of the boundary in a way nobody in the room disputed. The field walk had steadied her rather than shaken her. She had looked at white ground and at the ditch eleven yards off the trough and had come back ready to speak.

Aren sat by the slot again, with the yard’s four men on the bench below the window. They had come on the day the Marches named, on the Marches’ own road, under Coll Ferrow’s hand, and they were the best crew in the county by every measure that mattered: they handled timber in standing water without waste talk, they kept a line, they wrote nothing down that did not need writing. They were not on the bench and not on the oath, and nobody had ever asked them what they swore. The man at the drawknife nodded to Aren once and went back to listening.

A water-barge hand sat near the door with his hat on his knees. He was twenty-seven, broad through the shoulders from the pole, and new to the chamber. His name was Aled Rhys, and he had plied the Water since he was fifteen, and he spoke only when the talk touched freight.

The council took up the ditch. Ferrow said a mile of it in standing water with both sides standing in it and neither willing was nine days for nineteen men in a good week, and he had the nineteen and not the week and not the wage and not the far side. The minute-man entered nine days and no wage. The slate woman asked whether the far side had been approached. Munn said the far side was a man on his own ground being asked to give up four inches of field for ever, and there was nothing to put before such a man except water rising faster on him than on anyone else, which he knew and would not say because saying it could not be undone.

Then Munn said the obvious thing, in front of eleven people, looking at the table and not at Aren.

“There is a cut that would take the water out of that ditch in an afternoon, and the man off the road has done worse in a shorter time.”

The room went still. It was the first time in the rolling that the trade Aren carried had been named as a remedy rather than a threat. The yard’s men looked at their hands. Rhys turned his hat once on his knees.

Aren felt the weight of it come onto him the way a load comes onto a span, evenly and without a single point to refuse. He had opened roads and held spans and translated between benches, and every person in the room knew the stories the delegation had carried home about what a bridgewright could do when he chose.

He did not answer at once. He looked at Munn, who was right about the stone and wrong about the soil, and whom no person in this telling would call a villain, and he let the silence show that he had heard the proposal for what it was: reasonable, public, and impossible.

“I will not,” he said.

Munn lifted her chin. “Say why, then. This council has refused you and has said what it will take instead. You owe the room a reason if you refuse the room.”

“The reason is not a rule,” Aren said. “The last time one of these was cut, the anchor tore and a community lost contact for ever, and the man who did it was told afterwards that the witness had been forged, and he wrote it down.”

Nobody understood the sentence. He saw it in their faces, eleven of them, and in the slate woman’s pause with her chalk half raised, and in Ferrow’s frown. He did not explain it and would not. Some sentences could only be carried, not handed over.

Rhys spoke from the door without heat, in the tone of a man stating freight. “The road is refused on your terms here and taken on ours, and a working refused is still a working named. I have carried salt for men who call a loss a losing. This county had to go and find the word for refusing. A barge hand has always had it.”

The minute-man wrote, and the hour with it. Munn looked at Rhys as if seeing him for the first time, and then at Aren, and then down at her hands.

The sitting ended without a position on the working. The minute carried the naming and the refusal and the hour, and nothing else. Aren walked down the stair with the yard’s men, none of whom asked him what anchor meant or what witness meant or what community he spoke of, because men who work water know when a man has laid down a thing he will not lift again.

Outside, Rhys fell into step with him a little way along the lane. The barge hand carried his pole over his shoulder and smelled of river and tar.

“You named nothing and refused plain,” Rhys said. “That is a barge man’s way. A landing that will not take is refused, and no reasons read out to the tide.”

Aren said the reason had a date in it that would mean nothing here, and so he kept the date and gave the refusal. Rhys nodded as if that were ordinary seamanship.

“They will say you were asked for a miracle and declined,” Rhys said. “Let them. A man who promises an afternoon’s remedy for a hundred and forty years’ creep has not measured the ditch.”

They parted at the water, Rhys to his boat, Aren to the bank where the yard’s staging rose. The drawknife man was waiting, and asked no question about anchors, only whether Aren would stand the next morning’s work with them, holding a line while they set a foot. Aren said he would, glad of labour that asked hands and not reasons.
