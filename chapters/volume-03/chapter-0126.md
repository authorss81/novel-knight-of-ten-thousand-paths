# Chapter 0126

The council met again with the door open because the air below the barn had grown close. Pei Tarrow’s counterpart in this county, for there was always such a man where freight moved, brought in a cask and went out. The yard’s four men sat together as they always did, apart and attentive, the best crew in the room by common consent though no one had voted on it.

The minute-man read back the last sitting: the naming of a working that would empty the ditch in an afternoon, and the refusal with a date and a community and a forged witness written down. He read it flat, as fact. Nobody asked for the sentence to be explained, because the man who spoke it had said he would not explain it, and the room honoured refusals even when it did not understand them.

Aled Rhys asked leave to speak about freight, and got it. He laid out his want in plain terms. A man in a boat should be paid on the day the water went down and not on the day the council sat. The council sat when it could. The water went down when it went down. Between those two days a barge hand fed a family on credit, and credit in this county was salt promised before it was scraped. He spoke nine short sentences about poles and draught and bars and tides on the Water, each true in his own trade, and did not vote on anything, because barge hands did not vote in that chamber.

Ordinary business followed, or what passed for ordinary with white ground spreading. Then the slate woman put down her chalk and said the thing that made the sitting matter.

“We have sixty households on our roll that we have never asked what they swore,” she said. “I have written the rider so it does not fall on them. That leaves the larger debt. When does this council ask a person a question it has never asked?”

Silence came down. Ferrow looked at the floor. Munn looked at the table. The minute-man kept his pen still, which for him was a kind of speech.

Munn spoke at last, carefully. “The oath is renewed by men who hold the water. That is the custom. A boundary here is a channel and a man who cannot dig is no boundary-keeper. The women on three hearths do not hold water. The tenants on short leases do not hold water. The young hands on the barges do not hold water. If we ask them, we change what the oath is.”

“Then the oath is a thing that cannot ask sixty households what they hold,” the slate woman said.

“It is a promise kept by forty men for a hundred and forty years,” Munn said. “It is the only durable thing we have. If we widen it we risk it. If we do not widen it we keep it and it ends us. Thessa has put the question better than any of us by asking none of us to answer it.”

Rhys spoke again from his corner, without heat. “I have used refused all my life for a cargo that would not shift or a landing that would not take. I did not learn it in this room. I am willing to be asked what I swore, which is nothing, and to have the nothing entered, if entering does anything.”

Several people shifted. The yard’s man at the drawknife said quietly that he and his three mates had never been asked either, and had not expected to be, being strangers on the county’s own calendar for a season’s labour, and that work asked its own questions and answered most of them.

Then the minute-man put his pen down flat on the page, which he had never done in a sitting, and said that before anything was entered he wanted the room to know what he could enter.

“A fact about a person I can enter,” he said. “A fact about this council I can enter. I cannot enter a question, because a question is not a thing that has happened yet, it is a thing that has not. And I will tell you the trouble of it in plain words, because there is nobody in this room better placed to tell you, and I have been keeping books here twenty-two years, and this is the second time in two days that this room has handed me a thing I do not know how to keep. The first one is in the book with yesterday’s hour against it.”

He looked at the slate woman. “If this council goes and asks a household on the roll what it swore, and the household says nothing, then what am I to write.”

Nobody answered him. Ferrow looked at the floor. Munn put two fingers on the table and took them off again.

“Then I write that the household said nothing,” the minute-man said, “and that is a true line, and it is in a book, and in nine years a man reads it and finds a household that was asked and did not answer, and he will read that as a household that did not care to be asked. And I will have written it, and I will have written it in a good hand, and I will have written it because I am a man who writes down what happens.”

He picked the pen up again. “So I am telling the room that I can do this and that it will come out wrong, and I do not know how to keep a fact and keep it honest at the same time, and I have never had to before.”

Aren felt the floor of the room move under him, in the way it does when a load goes onto the wrong side of a span. He had spent the previous evening listening to a man explain, in a wet yard, exactly how the asking would end, and here was the man who would have to write it.

The minute-man entered that the question of the sixty had been raised and not decided, and the hour, and that no person in the room had been asked what they swore. It was the first time the not asking had stood in the book as a subject rather than a margin. He read the two entries back and put his hand flat on the page, and Ferrow read them over his shoulder and said nothing, which from Ferrow was a thing done on purpose.

A boy came up the stair while the minute-man was shutting the book. He was about eleven, in a coat three sizes too large, and he had walked from one of the four white villages and he said his household wanted to know one thing and would not say it in the room. Munn let him stand at the bottom of the stair where the lane came in.

He said it out loud in the gateway, to nobody in particular and to everybody, the way a child delivers a message he has been given. His household was on the roll and not on the oath. The council was talking about asking. His household wanted to know whether being asked would put them on a list.

It was a fair question and it was the right question and nobody had thought of it in nineteen years, because nobody had ever had cause to.

Munn said there was no list. The slate woman said there was no column for it and never had been. Ferrow said the roll was a roll of households that paid and nothing else, and that a man was on it or he was not and that was the end of it. The drawknife man said his yard had asked a man’s father to sign a paper once and had got nothing and stopped asking, and that a yard that kept a list would be a different kind of yard.

Four people answered one child’s question in about a minute, and every one of them told the truth, and not one of them wrote a line.

Aren asked the boy what his household wanted done about it, and the boy said they wanted to know whether being asked would put them on a list, and said it the second time exactly as he had said it the first, which told Aren that the boy had been given the words and would be given the answer in the same way.

So he told him. He said the question deserved the answer it had been sent for, and then he said the thing he had learned in four hundred miles and a year, which was that in the county he came from a man was a number in a column and offices did not keep names, and that a person could be moved out of a count by a man with a pen and no reason given, and that it had been done in his own county within living memory and had been lawful, and cheap, and quick.

The boy listened to all of it. He was eleven and he was not stupid and he was frightened by the end of it, and Aren could see the exact moment it went in, and it went in at the words lawful, cheap and quick, because a child from a county where nothing has ever been refused has no word for the difference between a thing that is fast and a thing that is only fast.

When the boy had gone down the lane at a run, Ferrow said, “You will want to be careful about that.”

“I know what I did.”

“No, you do not, and I will tell you why you will want to be careful.” Ferrow kept his voice low, for the stair was empty by then and empty was not the same as private. “Four of us have just told that boy there is no list. Four voices, in a gateway, and the county has never once had a reason to doubt any of the four. That answer is going into four villages tonight and there is no book in this county that will ever hold it, and in five rollings when a list does come along — and a list will come along, this county cannot stand another decade of white ground — half of them will remember that we said there was none, and they will sign whatever is read out to them, and they will not do it because they were deceived. They will do it because we were right.”

Aren said he had told the boy the truth about a column and a pen because the boy had been sent to ask for the truth and he was not going to hand a child a comfortable answer on a matter of a name.

“You were right and you have spent it,” Ferrow said, “and the spending was yours and I would not have spent it, and I am telling you it was not cheap for you either, because a man who has said a true thing in a gateway to a child is now a man who has something to prove about it. That is how a county starts keeping books about men who talk in gateways.”

Inside, the minute-man was standing at the table with the book shut and his hand on it. Aren asked whether the boy’s question and the answers had gone in.

“The question has,” the minute-man said. “A question put to the council and not decided is a fact about this council, and I can keep that, and it is in the book with the hour, and I have written that no answer was given in the room, because no answer was given anywhere, and that is a true line and I know what it will be read as in nine years.” He took his hand off the cover. “The four answers are not in there. I did not hear them from the people who gave them. I heard a child repeat a question and a room answer it, and a man who writes down what he has not heard is a man who has stopped being a keeper and started being a clerk.”

“That was the right judgement.”

“It was the only one I had at the time.” The minute-man put the lamp out. “Go to bed. There is a bucket under the window that has been dripping since the first sowing and I have never once entered it.”

On the stair above, waiting out the rain, Ferrow gave Aren the other of his two standing arguments, the one he had made twice and had been heard once.

“Delya is right about the stone,” he said. “A promise renewed a hundred and forty times is a claim on every household under it, asked or not. She is wrong about the soil, because she thinks holding the line holds the ground, and the ground is telling another story.”

Aren said Munn had named the working openly and had taken his refusal openly, and that was more than most rooms managed. Ferrow agreed, and said the room would now live with three sentences, the naming, the refusal, and a boy’s question in a gateway, and that the third of them was the one that would still be standing in five rollings.

At his lodging Aren added a third line under the other two: the not asking is now the matter before the council and not a detail, and the barge hand has the word the barn had to go and look for, and uses it as his own. Then he stopped, and left the boy and the four answers out of it entirely, and wrote one more line about them instead, because he had promised a yard and not a book. Four persons had answered a child in a gateway and no line of it was in any book, and a sentence said in a gateway in this county was now the only kind of record it had, and it could not be taken back and it could not be corrected.

He found Rhys mending a sail by the water and sat on an upturned boat to talk freight. Rhys showed him the sail’s wear and the pole’s banding, explaining draught and bar and landing in nine short true sentences, each complete in itself. Aren listened the way he listened to timber, for load and give. When Rhys finished, Aren said a boat paid on the day the water went down would change the county more than a term debated overhead. Rhys said pay followed power and not water, and both men left it there, the agreement between them unspoken but firm.

The rain eased. The yard’s men shouted measurements at the staging. The bucket under the chamber window went on with its business unwritten.

He decided, before he slept, that he would go and see the woman who kept the seed room in the morning, and not because of the seed. He wanted to look at one thing in this county that had been kept for forty years with no book on it at all, and to be able to say, after an hour in a stone room, whether that was a kind of holding or a kind of hiding. If he could not tell the difference by then, he would stop asking this county questions and take the standing work at the staging and hold a line, and he would go on being useful in the only way the county had ever accepted from anybody.
