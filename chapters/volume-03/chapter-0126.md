# Chapter 0126

The council met again with the door open because the air below the barn had grown close. Pei Tarrow’s counterpart in this county, for there was always such a man where freight moved, brought in a cask and went out. The yard’s four men sat together as they always did, apart and attentive, the best crew in the room by common consent though no one had voted on it.

The minute-man read back the last sitting: the naming of a working that would empty the ditch in an afternoon, and the refusal with a date and a community and a forged witness written down. He read it flat, as fact. Nobody asked for the sentence to be explained, because the man who spoke it had said he would not explain it, and the room honoured refusals even when it did not understand them.

Aled Rhys asked leave to speak about freight, and got it. He laid out his want in plain terms. A man in a boat should be paid on the day the water went down and not on the day the council sat. The council sat when it could. The water went down when it went down. Between those two days a barge hand fed a family on credit, and credit in this county was salt promised before it was scraped. He spoke nine short sentences about poles and draught and bars and tides on the Water, each true in his own trade, and did not vote on anything, because barge hands did not vote in that chamber.

Ordinary business followed, or what passed for ordinary with white ground spreading. Then the slate woman put down her chalk and said the thing that made the sitting matter.

“We have sixty households on our roll that we have never asked what they swore,” she said. “I have written the rider so it does not fall on them. That leaves the larger debt. When does this council ask a person a question it has never asked?”

Silence came down. Ferrow looked at the floor. Munn looked at the table. The minute-man kept his pen still, which for him was a kind of speech.

Munn spoke at last, carefully. “The oath is renewed by men who hold the water. That is the custom. A boundary here is a channel and a man who cannot dig is no boundary-keeper. The women on three hearths do not hold water. The tenants on short leases do not hold water. The young hands on the barges do not hold water. If we ask them, we change what the oath is.”

“Then the oath is a thing that cannot ask sixty households what they hold,” the slate woman said.

“It is a promise kept by forty men for a hundred and forty years,” Munn said. “It is the only durable thing we have. If we widen it we risk it. If we do not widen it we keep it and it ends us. Thessa has put the question better than any of us by asking none of us to answer it.”

Rhys spoke again from his corner, without heat. “I have used refused all my life for a cargo that would not shift or a landing that would not take. I did not learn it in this room. I am willing to be asked what I swore, which is nothing, and to have the nothing entered, if entering does anything.”

Several people shifted. The yard’s man at the drawknife said quietly that he and his three mates had never been asked either, and had not expected to be, being strangers on the county’s own calendar for a season’s labour, and that work asked its own questions and answered most of them.

The minute-man entered that the question of the sixty had been raised and not decided, and the hour, and that no person in the room had been asked what they swore. It was the first time the not asking had stood in the book as a subject rather than a margin.

After the sitting Aren walked back with Ferrow. The first hand was grave.

“Delya is right about the stone,” Ferrow said. “A promise renewed a hundred and forty times is a claim on every household under it, asked or not. She is wrong about the soil, because she thinks holding the line holds the ground, and the ground is telling another story. I have told her so twice and she has heard me once.”

Aren said Munn had named the working openly and had taken his refusal openly, and that was more than most rooms managed. Ferrow agreed, and said the room would now live with both sentences, the naming and the refusal, and with the sixty unasked, and that was the second rolling’s whole burden.

At his lodging Aren added a third line under the other two: the not asking is now the matter before the council and not a detail, and the barge hand has the word the barn had to go and look for, and uses it as his own.

He found Rhys mending a sail by the water and sat on an upturned boat to talk freight. Rhys showed him the sail’s wear and the pole’s banding, explaining draught and bar and landing in nine short true sentences, each complete in itself. Aren listened the way he listened to timber, for load and give. When Rhys finished, Aren said a boat paid on the day the water went down would change the county more than a term debated overhead. Rhys said pay followed power and not water, and both men left it there, the agreement between them unspoken but firm.

The rain eased. The yard’s men shouted measurements at the staging. Somewhere above, the council’s lamp was lit though daylight remained, and the minute-book lay open on the door leaves waiting for the next fact worth keeping.
