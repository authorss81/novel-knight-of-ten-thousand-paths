# Chapter 0132

The work began on the first morning of the first week of the sixth rolling and it was done in three days, and the county understood none of the three of them while they were going on.

The guild did not start at the trough. Ferrow started at the top sluice, four hundred and forty yards down, and he put in three pegs before he let anybody touch a spade, and the pegs were not on a line. They were on the old bank, because the old bank was the only record of where the ditch had ever been, and a bank that has been moved a hand's width a year for a hundred and forty years has not forgotten its own line. It has it in its shoulder. You find it with a boot.

That was the first thing the north would have wanted a drawing for, and Ferrow said so, standing over the first peg with his hands behind his back.

“A man in a fen county would have wanted a chain and a boy with a tape and a line to hold, and he would have got the same three pegs in a day and a half and he would have got them a foot off, because the chain measures the ground and the ground here is not the thing. The shoulder is the thing. The ditch has been in this line so long that the line is in the ground, and the ground is the only drawing this county has ever had and nobody had to keep it.”

The bank came down easily. It was a barrow's width of soil on the top and then a foot of the old, packed stuff under it, and under that the ground the county was made of, which was gravel with clay in it, and it came up whole in slabs and the men broke the slabs over their knees because a slab of that ground weighs about as much as a man can lift in two hands and no more.

Sixteen of the guild were on it and four of the yard's men were on it and Aren was on it, and the yard's men worked the way men work when they have been in a foreign county for six rollings and are the best crew in it and nobody in the room has ever been told so in words. They were faster. That was all. Two of them took the barrow-load and swung it out of the trench and set it down, over and over, at a rate the guild's men could not have matched in the first week and did match by the second, and Ferrow watched them for about a quarter of an hour and then went and stood somewhere else.

The boy who read the gauge came down with the pole on the first morning, because a ditch that is being moved eleven yards wants a man with a pole, and Ferrow had asked for a pole and the boy had one. He was fifteen now and he was taller than he had been at the Sallow foot in the first rolling and he read the water without being asked and said the number out loud, the way he had been taught, and then said it again before anybody had asked him to.

And on the second morning the boy read it and stopped.

“Two inches,” he said. “Two inches over the winter mark.”

The boy of fourteen who reads a gauge is taught to be looked at, and this one was being looked at by sixteen guild men and four of a fen yard and a stranger, and he did not wait to be asked the second time.

“Two inches on the board that is painted in inches, and it was at the mark at the first sowing, and it has gone up two inches in six rollings, and nobody has ever asked me what two inches means and I have known for five rollings that it means the ditch is filling up at the head.”

The head of the ditch was eleven yards from the trough, and eleven yards is not far, and it is the whole county’s argument. Aren looked at the head of it and saw nothing, because he had never once in his life looked at a ditch head and had spent two volumes looking at books.

Ferrow looked at it for a while and then said, out loud, to nobody in particular, “The pond is filling.”

Nobody asked him what pond.

“The low side, from here to the foot, and a mile each way,” he said. “It is not water you can see from here because it is four inches deep at the ditch and it is under the grass. That is the white, and the salt in it, and the four villages, and it has been filling for a hundred and forty years and it has nine inches in it now and every year it takes a little more. It is a pond. It has no name because nobody has ever looked down.”

Then he went back to the pegs, and that was about nine sentences, and not one of them was addressed to the council, and the guild's men heard all of them, and none of them said a word.

The line came out two fingers left of where it should have been.

Not the pegs. The pegs were on the old shoulder and the old shoulder is not a matter of opinion. It was the new work, where the fresh trench was brought down to meet the trough. It came down two fingers to the left. The left is downstream. Downstream is the way the ditch has been moving for a hundred and forty years, and so after three days of work the ditch was half a hand’s width narrower than it should have been at the head, and the head was a half-hand’s width downstream of the trough.

Ferrow looked at it and said one word, and the word was not a word any of the four men of the fen yard had ever heard a first hand use, and then he said the rest of it in the voice he used for figures.

“The old line. I have had this wrong before, on the Tow, eleven years back, on the same system, and I was wrong the same way. Two fingers to the left, twice, and I know the reason both times, and the reason is that a man brings a new trench down to meet an old one and comes at it from the side he is standing on, and I was standing on the left because the left is where the spade goes in easy and the right is where the stone is. A leaf is not a survey. Thessa’s leaf says a hand’s width a year on that bank, and the leaf is right and my line is not, and the leaf is right because it is a record and mine is a decision, and a decision made in a day on a wet bank is two fingers out and always in the direction of the man’s own comfort. It will be a finger and a half by the middle of the next sowing and a hand by the fourth year, and the bank will tell us in a year and not before, and I am writing the expectation in the book tonight with the hour against it, and a thing you expect to be true in a year is worth more in a book than a thing you found out in a day.”

“What is it worth,” said the bound.

“It is worth that in a year the guild comes back here with a rope and a reason, and does not have to stand in front of nine men in a field and be told that the boundary has been moved by the guild.” He put his thumb on the two fingers. “That is what it is worth. Nobody in this county is short of reasons. We are short of a line of our own that somebody else has to answer for.”

There were three marks cut in the outside of the seed room's wall in three bits of tin in a mason's language, above the line the water had come to the year before, and there was a woman of sixty-six in the room with nine sealed jars in it and a wall she had built up twice with her own hands, and Ferrow's line two fingers to the left was a foot and a half north of the outside of that room's near corner, and everybody on that work had walked past the corner twice a day for three days, and not one of them had looked at the wall, because there was a ditch to open and a wall is a wall.

The third day they brought the new trench down into the trough and the water in the trough came up into the head of it and went down, and it did not stop, and the sound of a trench taking water for the first time in a hundred and forty years is a thing that four hundred people came to stand in the lane and look at, and then went away, because there is nothing to look at after the first hour and a county has other work.

The guild's nineteen and the yard's four and a stranger and a boy with a pole, three days, and the money came off the slate in the guild room on the third evening with the hour against it and the room full and the bound reading it out in the best hand of the nine.

“Sixteen men at eight pence the day is a hundred and twenty-eight pence the day. Three days is a hundred and twenty-eight, a hundred and twenty-eight and a hundred and twenty-eight, and that is three hundred and eighty-four pence. Iron and elm, at what our rack would fetch, ninety-six pence. Three hundred and eighty-four and ninety-six is four hundred and eighty pence. Ten marks. Fifty-three bushels of salt at nine pence and three pence over.”

“Three pence,” said Ferrow, “and the three pence is ours, and I will take it in beans at the market and I will not have it in the book twice.”

The book had it in the book twice, because a man who has had a remainder argued about once does not enjoy it twice, and the minute-man from the council was not in the guild room and heard about it from four separate people in a lane and came and got the number out of Ferrow, which is what he is for.

Ferrow laid the money out on the board that week and the board was chalk, on a board of lath and lime in the guild hall that had the six lines of the Marches' terms on it in a copy, unaltered, dated, and the two boards had been standing eleven yards apart for five rollings and had never been in the same room.

Aren put his hand flat on the chalk and did the arithmetic the way he did every arithmetic, out loud and in front of the room, and then he added the year to it, which is a thing he has never done in a room in four hundred miles and which he could not afterwards account for.

“Four hundred and eighty pence, in the year this rolling ends, and the salt it is paid in comes off the ten villages and not off the four, and the four villages are the four this is for. In a year, when the pond has gone down two inches and the ground has come up a foot, in a year the four villages will have a crop that is worth saying, and in that year this kind of work will be wanted again, and there will be salt. And in that year somebody will be asked for salt, and the person who does the asking will not be the person who signed this board, and there will be nothing on paper to show anybody why it was not asked this year. That is a fact and not an argument and I have said it out loud once and I am not saying it again.”

Nobody in the room took it up, because it was about a year nobody in the room was in, and Ferrow looked at him for a moment longer than a first hand looks at a stranger, and then said, to the bound, “Put it under the sum,” and the bound put it under the sum in chalk, and it is in the guild's book in the guild's hand under four hundred and eighty pence in nineteen words, and it is the only line in that book that was not written by a man of the guild.

The last hour of the third day the work stopped, the way Ferrow's work always stopped, with the men standing about not wanting to go, because the thing they had made was under water at the head and a ditch under water at the head looks like a thing that is finished, and one of the fen men said so in the gateway in the voice of a man making a joke, and Ferrow gave him the look a first hand gives a man who has said something in the wrong place.

“Water in a ditch is not work finishing,” he said. “Water in a ditch is the ditch working. And a man from a county four hundred miles off cannot say a word to me about it, because he is in a lane in a country that is not his and he has been here six rollings and he has not once been asked anything, and so he does not know that I know he does not know, and that is the arrangement, and I am not going to spoil it by making a point of it in a gateway in front of the boys.”

“You do know what I do not know,” the man said.

“I do,” said Ferrow. “And I have known since the second week, and I have said nothing, and you have had a standing in my yard and a wage at my rate for six rollings on the strength of it, and if you ever want it out loud you come and ask me for it in a room and not in a gateway, and I will tell you, and I will not thank you for asking, because a man who has to be asked for the truth about himself is a man somebody has been managing.”

The man at the drawknife looked at him for a while and then said the thing he had come out of the lane to say.

“Four hundred miles,” he said, to nobody, in a gateway, on a wet evening, with the third day’s work drying on his hands. “Four hundred miles and eleven days and a wagon with a board in it, and this county has a rail on the sluice and a trough with a hole dug in it that has not moved in a hundred years, and there is a man here who has told me all three of those things in a day and a half, and I have not written one of them down, and neither has anybody else, and that is going to be the first thing I miss about this county when I go home, and I am not going to say it twice.”

The lane heard it. Eleven or twelve of the county's own people were standing about in it at that hour and four of the yard's men heard every word of it, and in the morning one of the fen men was asked by a carter's boy in the market whether the fen county was a day's ride or two, and told him a day's ride and about two days' hard going back, and the carter's boy said it twice in the market within the hour in a way that made it four hundred miles, and by the end of the second week there was a market price on it, and it was a good deal too high, and there was no way on earth to put it right, and nobody made any effort to put it right, and the county got on with what it had.

The board went to the council on the fourth day, and the council's minute carried the offer, the accepting, the sum and the hour, and it said the council took the guild's plan and the guild did the work at the guild's terms, and it did not say the council ordered the work, because the council did not order it and could not, and if the minute had said it then in nine years there would be a book in this county with a council in it that had ordered four hundred and forty yards of ditch.

Then the water sat.

That is what nobody outside the guild understood afterwards, and it is the plainest fact of the whole business: after the third day the water in the ditch stopped going down. Nine days. The ditch had four inches in it on the fourth day and four inches in it on the fifth and on the sixth and on the seventh, with the head of it running clear and the trough running clear and the water going down the new trench at a rate a boy could watch, and the pond against the low side of the bank did not move, and Thessa Wend's board at the weir went up another half inch and stayed there, and the half inch was in her own hand and was the eleven hundredth line she had given to nobody.

Aren walked to the weir hut on the ninth day and stood at the sill in his boots and asked her the only question he had, which was whether the two inches on the board were hers or the board's.

“The board’s,” she said. “I only write down what the board says. The board has been painted at the same mark since before I started and the paint is four years old and the mark is the same mark and the mark is a finger, and the board is a foot lower than the ground it stands in, and that was a good decision made by a man who is dead. If the mark moves, the mark is wrong. If the mark does not move and the water does, the water is wrong. Nobody has ever asked me which of the two of those I would like to be.”

“Then you would like to be the board,” said Aren.

“I would like to be the woman who reads it twice a day for eleven years and is asked for one line of it once, and I have been that woman for a fortnight,” said Thessa, “and I am not going to be told by a stranger that I would rather be a painted board, because a board does not know what it is not seeing.”

She turned the book round on the bench, the way she had turned it round in the first rolling for him and not for anybody else, and there was the two inches, in her own small quick left-sloping hand, and under it a column of two hundred and something days in a single narrow line with nothing at the top of it and nothing at the foot of it and no name anywhere on the page.

“That is nine days of it standing,” she said, “and I have read it twice a day, and I am the only person in this county who knows that nine days is a thing that has happened here, and if I put that in a book in your county it is a number, and if I put it in a book in this county the book is the council's and the council will move the post, and I have told you both of those things and you have not once offered me a third.”

He took the notebook out where she could see it, and shut it, and did not open it, and she watched him not open it and something went across her face that he did not have the standing to interpret and did not try to.

“No,” he said. “There is no third. There is a thing I entered a week ago that I would like to take back, and I am not going to take it back, and I would rather you knew that I entered it.”

“You entered something about this county.”

“I entered a decision about me. It is the first decision I have made in five rollings and I made it after I offered this council a thing out of a clay county four hundred miles off and had it refused in a breath and a half, and the decision was that I would not bring anything else up those stairs unless a person on the stairs asked me for it by name. It has nothing to do with your water. It has to do with the room.”

“Then it is the right decision and it is in the wrong county,” said Thessa, “and I am not going to argue with a man about his own housekeeping. But you made it in a room where nobody asked you for anything, and you made it about a room, and there are nine people on that bench and about sixty houses off it, and the two of those numbers have never been in the same sentence in this county, and it is not my business and I have a pole to read.”

She went down the bank. The water came over the second of the lower posts and fell back, twice a day, and had done since the first sowing.

The guild's plan was in a book in the guild room, and it had a line in it two fingers to the left of true, and the expectation of the finger and a half was under the sum in nineteen words with the hour against it, and the man who had drawn the line was asleep, because a man who has been in a foreign county for six rollings and has not been asked one question in it can sleep at the end of the third day of hard work in a way that men who are asked questions every day cannot.

And in the morning the four men of the fen yard were on the staging at seven, because he had said he would be, and the water in the ditch had been four inches deep for nine days and the head of it was running and the trough was taking what it had not taken in a hundred and forty years, and the head of it was running, and the pond against the low side of the bank was a mile from the trough, and nobody in the county knew that it was a pond, and nobody in the county knew that the bank which held it was a hundred and forty feet of new stuff at the downstream end, and the youngest of the dead alders along the old line was older than the man who had moved it.
