# Chapter 0104

The ninth of Longlight, YR 313, was a Thursday and a market day, and the guild hall over the wharf held about forty people standing and eleven sitting, and the bench of nine sat at the long table with the reeve at the end nearest the door, and the hall has a press in the corridor where anybody may come and read the roll, and there was a queue at the press before the fourth hour.

The delegation came in at about the ninth hour and did not wait for the reeve.

That is the first thing that happened and everything else in that room happened because of it. Eleven of a council of a border county, two men of that county's bridge guild who had come with the council and not under it, and one seed-carter who was not on the council at all and had come because the council would not go four hundred miles without him. They had been in this county six days and had spent five of them on a bench in a weigh-house yard, and one of the eleven had read the whole of a board out loud to herself twice a day.

The woman at the front of them was about fifty-five and she was not dressed better than anybody else and she had a grey shawl on a day that needed no shawl, and she put one hand flat on the end of the long table, which is not a thing anybody in Merefen does, and she said:

"We will say it now, before you have asked us anything, because if you ask us first you will have asked us a question and we will have to answer inside your question."

The reeve put down her pen.

"We are the council of a border county called the Hearth Marches. We have no writ to answer and no bell to ring and there is no Crown's schedule in this kingdom that carries us, and we are the only body in our county that meets, and we meet because a county that has been cut off still has to argue about a boundary. **We are not asking for a command and we will not take one.** We have had a hundred and forty years of other people's commands and the last one that worked on us worked so well that the ground has gone white under four villages, and that is not a figure, that is a thing you can put a spade in."

Nobody in that hall had heard the word *white* put next to the word *ground* before, and about six people in the room understood immediately that the two words had nothing to do with each other, which is a different thing from understanding.

"And we are not a rescue," said the woman at the end of the table, "and we are not coming to be helped, and anybody in this room who is waiting to be thanked for the next four days is going to be disappointed in a way that will cost this county a great deal and cost you nothing."

---

**Then she read the six lines off a board, and the board is a thing that came in a wagon off their own road and cannot be copied out of.**

It was four feet by two and a half, lath and lime, and the lines were in chalk and the second line had been gone over twice. The woman read it standing, and she read it in the county's own words and did not translate it and would not have translated it if she had been asked.

*First. The road is not to be made to carry a person.*
*Second. No name is to be read out on the far side of the line by anybody from the north, and no name is to be written in the line's book at the north end.*
*Third. The north is not to send anybody who can be asked to come and look.*
*Fourth. The stone is not to be renewed in the north's presence.*
*Fifth. The road is kept by a hand at each end and a witness at each end, and the witness at the Marches' end is chosen by the Marches.*
*Sixth. The line is not to be opened on a day the Marches have not named.*

"That is six lines and there is no seventh," she said, "and they are not our conditions for a bargain. They are the conditions under which we will let a road be made at all, and they were written by two men in a room in our market town in the second week of the rolling that begins with the low water, and one of them is standing at the back of this hall and will not say so."

Nobody looked round, which took a certain amount of not looking round.

---

**And the road warden of the Merefen bridge guild said yes to the fourth line in about nine seconds and does not know why he said it so fast.**

He was standing at the back with his hands behind him, because he does not hold the crossing and does not vote and had said in a hall in Frostmonth that he would come when he was wanted and had come.

"Say the fourth one again," he said.

The woman looked at the board and then at him.

"*The stone is not to be renewed in the north's presence.*"

"Yes," said Aren Kest. "**In nine seconds. I do not know what I have just agreed to and I have not asked what it means, and I would like it entered that I did not ask.**"

"I will not enter it," said the reeve, "because it is not mine to enter, but I will tell you that a man who says yes in nine seconds to a thing he does not understand is a man who has understood it, and that this hall has watched him do that four times."

"Then it is the fourth time," said Aren Kest, "and I have got it out of him the way I get it out of everybody, and I am not going to do it again today."

He said it lightly and it was not light, and the man at the drawknife, who had come down from the yard at the fourth hour because his master had told him to, heard it and did not look at him.

**The reason he said yes in nine seconds was not in that hall and he is not going to say it here, and it is this: a stone is renewed in public, by men who are still alive, who can be asked what they swore; and if a man from the north is standing in a field when that happens, then in nine years there is a room in which a stranger stood in a field; and the stranger will be a document, and the document will be the only evidence anybody has about the stone, and the stone is the only thing in that county that has lasted a hundred and forty years. He did not think any of that. He has still not thought any of that. He said yes because in nine seconds his whole chest made the decision and his hands were behind his back so that nobody would see them move.**

---

**And the guild spoke before it was asked, and the guild's terms are better than anything the north has brought, and the bench of nine conceded nothing whatever.**

The man who spoke was sixty and had a spliced line across the back of his right hand and a piece of chalk on his coat, and he came to the long table past the delegation's own bench and did not stand at the end of it. He stood in the middle of the floor.

"You have had a paper from us," he said, to the room and not to the reeve. "A letter of business and a form. I am the first hand of the bridge guild of the Hearth Marches and there are nineteen men behind me and one of them is my brother-in-law and none of them is here, and I have come to say our terms before you ask, because you will ask me what I want and I want to be the one who has said it."

The reeve inclined about half an inch.

"First. We are going to do our own work. Not in a fortnight, not with a drawing brought from four hundred miles off. We cut a ditch in a day and a half that your craft would plan for a fortnight and a season, and we do it out of a book of water and a memory, and we have done it four times in nine years and twice it was wrong and both times we were wrong the same way, which is a thing about memory that your craft would find useful and mine would not."

"Second. The water in a ditch is somebody's job. It has been somebody's job since my grandfather and it is going to be somebody's job after I am dead, and it is not a county's mood. We have asked a wage three times. Twice of a landlord and once of our own bench, and I was the one who asked the bench, and the bench said no in a full room and was right to. **I do not want a stranger's money. I want the same nineteen men all year at a rate they can refuse.**"

"Then take the north's rate," said the reeve, and it came out harder than she meant it and she let it stand.

"I have costed the north's rate," said the guild's first hand. "I did it in a waggon on the way here, in your coin, out loud, to four men, and I would like it in a book."

He put a folded sheet on the table and did not open it, and the number in that sheet is the yard's number and came out of the yard's own book, and the yard does not know that yet.

"Third. A stranger's labour is taken at the guild's rate and is paid in the county's coin at the crossing and not in goods. **We have four hundred miles of road between us and anybody who wants to argue with us about the price of a bushel, and we have learned that in a waggon, and none of us would have learned it anywhere else.**"

"Fourth," he said, "and the fourth is the whole of it and there is no fifth. **We do not work with a stranger's oath in our water.** We have a word for a man who can make a ditch do what he wants instead of what the water wants, and he is not welcome in this county, and I am not asking you what it is, and I would be obliged if you did not tell me, because if you tell me I shall have to be civil about it, and I have nineteen men who would rather I was not civil about it."

The room did what a room in a fen county does with a sentence like that, which is to assume it is about a trade and go on.

---

**And then the man who keeps the minute of the Marches council asked the room a question nobody had asked, and it was not a good question, and it is the reason the sixth line exists.**

"The guild is paid in coin," he said. "The council has no coin. Who pays the guild?"

"Charter," said the woman at the end of the table. "The next full guild, cut, and a wage for a year, in writing, in a hand both benches can read. The council's minute has a line in it that says the council cannot pay a wage because the council has no money and the men who have money are the same nine men who renew the stone. That line is nine years old and it is the shortest line in our book."

"Then you have come four hundred miles," said the reeve, "to ask a yard in this county for a wage for nineteen men in yours."

"No," said the woman at the end of the table. "**We have come four hundred miles to ask this room a question, and the question is on our form and you have had a copy of it for six days, and nobody in this county has answered it, and the answer our clerk got back was that the entry is a standard practice.** We are not here for the wage. We will take the wage question home and lose it. We are here because the fourth column of that return has a man in it who says he did not put himself there, and he is in this hall, and I would like somebody in this room to look at him."

---

**Oswin Rill, forty-four, seed-carter, of Nether Dray, stood up at the back and came about halfway into the room and then stopped, which is a thing a man does when he has decided that the rest of the walk is the part where everybody watches him.**

"Oswin Rill," he said. "Nether Dray. I am on this delegation and I am not on that council and I am the fourth column."

Nobody in the room had expected a man to be pleased about that and two people were, visibly, and he saw them and it made it worse.

"Two years ago a man came round our village with a printed sheet and a bag of string, and he was very civil, and he read out the five columns and he wrote down the water and the date and the man he was talking to, and he came to my door after because I keep the seed and I keep the records of what everybody owes me and what I owe them, which in Nether Dray is the same list, and he asked me to be the one who reads the water, and I said I would think about it."

He put his hands flat on the front of his coat.

"I did think about it, and I did not put my name in it, and I went and asked the man for it back, and he said the column was not full and that without a keeper the return could not go up and that if no keeper was entered the office would enter one, and he was not threatening me, he was explaining a rule to me, and he was pleasant about it, and I have thought about that for two years and I cannot get past the part where being pleasant about it was the reason it happened."

"So it went up," said the reeve, "with your name in it."

"With my name in it, in a hand that is not mine, and I did not know for fourteen months, and I found out because a woman in a third press in a city two hundred miles off took a fair copy of a whole leaf because her grandmother was a copyist, and she wrote to a keeper of records in a fen county I have never been to and told her about it." He looked round. "**I have wanted for two years for one thing and I am going to say it out loud because there are forty people in this room and I have never said it out loud: I want a man's own hand to be the only hand that puts his name anywhere.** That is all of it. I do not want the sheet back. I want the whole habit stopped."

"And what does that cost," said Mara Vey, from the back, where she had been standing for ten minutes because a keeper of records in a room of forty is a woman standing at the back, and she had not said a word.

"It is already being paid," said Oswin Rill. "**The office that filled it in will send that county nothing at all, ever again, and the county will get worse, and everybody in that county will blame the man whose name is in the column. That is the price and I have known it for two years and I said it out loud to a man with a printed sheet and a bag of string and he wrote my objection in the margin and took the sheet away anyway.** I am telling it to a room because a room is where a price is worth anything. It is worth nothing in my own village. They all know why the returns stopped coming."

---

**And at about the eleventh hour, on a market morning, four hundred miles from the boundary, a road warden who does not hold the crossing asked a question of a woman of thirty-eight of Sallow Marsh, and it is the first question a stranger has ever asked her, and she gave him a number with a place on it, and the room heard it happen.**

She had been standing by the door with a strap of a bag over her shoulder for the whole hour and had spoken twice, both times about the board.

"How deep is it at the Sallow side," said Aren Kest, "in the last week before the low?"

"Eleven at the weir post," said the woman, "and nine at the Sallow foot, and the Sallow foot is the one that matters because the Sallow foot is the one the ground is under, and I read both twice a day and I have read them twice a day for eleven years and I write them in a book at the weir and the book is mine and it is not a county book and it has never been in this room."

"How many are in the book."

"Eight thousand and thirty-one lines," she said, "and I have never been asked for one of them, and I would like somebody from this side to ask me for one, and I have wanted that since the first year, and I am not going to tell this room why."

The hall did not go quiet, because a hall with forty people in it does not go quiet. It got a little more careful, which is a different thing and is harder to see.

"My name is Thessa Wend," she said, to him, not to the room. "Of Sallow Marsh. And I am the reason the council is in your hall, because I wrote the question on the form, and I have never been able to work out whether I did the county a service or a disservice, and I would like a bridgewright to tell me and I would like him to tell me in about nine seconds, the way you said yes to the fourth line."

Aren Kest did not answer her, and she did not press him, and she went back to the board, and in the notebook that night there is one line about it, and the line is not about the water.

---

Nothing was decided in that hall. The bench conceded nothing, and the minute of the guild's terms was read into the roll, and the six lines on the board were read into the roll as a paper from a county that is not on any map in this kingdom, and the reeve asked the bench-clerk to enter that the delegation had spoken before the bench had asked them a question, and the bench-clerk entered it, and the bench voted on nothing at all, which is a first in the roll.

And the last thing that happened in the guild hall over the wharf on the ninth of Longlight, YR 313, was that a woman of thirty-eight of Sallow Marsh, at about the twelfth hour, put her hand flat on a board that had come four hundred miles in a wagon and said the only question anybody in that room had not thought of asking, which was:

"Who is it that is going to read it?"
