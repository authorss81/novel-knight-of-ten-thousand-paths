The tenth day after the water went down, the two of them went to look at the wall.

Aren had asked for the leave and had been given it in a yard in about nine words, and the leave was the same leave as the first time, with the same condition, and the condition this time was worse.

“Not the chisel,” she said. “You are not putting anything on that wall. You are going to look at what is already on it and you are going to tell me what you see, and I am going to tell you what I see, and if we agree we have agreed in a yard and in a room and in two heads, and that is the only kind of agreement this county has ever produced and I want it to be the only kind for another forty years.”

He agreed to it, and they walked up behind the chapel at the fourth hour of a day in the second week of the eighth rolling, and the room had a foot of dried mud in it that had been a foot of water, and the plaster on the inside of the third course had come away in four sheets and was lying on the floor where it had fallen, and the marks were in the stone behind it.

Nine bits of tin. Three of them, in a mason's language, and a mason’s language is not a cipher, it is a set of marks a man cuts into stone so that a man who has never seen it can find it again in bad light and know within a hand’s width whether he is looking at the right thing.

The high one was the height of the older floor, which is the floor the room had before it was the room. The middle one was the line the water came to in the year before the marks were put there. The low one, three feet off the ground and inside the third course, was the floor inside the third course.

“They are exactly where they were,” said the woman of sixty-six, standing in the mud with her hand flat on the plaster, “and the last one is a foot under where the water stood, and the last one is the one that matters and it is the one nobody will ever look at, and it worked, and it worked on a man who did not know it was there, and that is everything I have got out of five rollings of a stranger in my county.”

She put her thumb on the low mark and left it there.

“He went down on his hands and knees,” she said. “That is what a man does. Nobody has ever told me that a man in a flooded room with a lantern puts his hand on the wall and takes a step, and I have now watched it happen twice, once in the sixth rolling in a wet week over his bootlaces and once last week with a foot of water round a man's middle, and the second time the water had come up through a gap at the far end and had not come up in the middle, because the middle is on gravel and the far end is not. Three feet of stone and a bed of gravel under a floor, laid in a year when the men laying it had a good season, and that is all that saved him both times, and it is a thing that happened forty years before he was born and it is not in a book.”

Aren said, “It is in two places and neither of them is a book.”

She looked up at him.

“In your head,” he said, “since the first rolling. And in mine, in a sentence, and not in the council's minute and not in the guild's, and it is not going in either, because you said it is not going in and because a measure in a book becomes an office and a measure in a head is a woman, and I said that sentence in the third rolling and I have not improved on it and I am not going to now.”

“Then say it now and make it true,” said the woman of sixty-six, and the drawknife man was in the yard with his back to them planing a rail and listening to every word of it, which is what he does.

Aren said, “I do not know how to make it true. I can do the other thing. I can ask you, once, for the number, and then it is in my head and in yours and there are two heads and not one, and that is worse than one and better than a book, and I do not know which of those two things it is worse or better, and I am not going to decide it for you.”

“Then do not ask,” she said, and picked her hand off the mark, and looked at the four sheets of plaster on the floor of her seed room, and said the thing she had said in the first rolling and had not said since.

“A scar is a thing a wall can have and an answer is a thing a wall cannot have. I have kept this room forty years and I have never once been asked to keep it from anybody, and in the third week of the fourth rolling a stranger asked me leave to mark it and I gave it on condition that I kept the measure, and I have kept the measure, and now the water has come and gone and there is a mark on my wall that has done one thing it was put there to do, and I am still not deciding anything.”

The drawknife man turned round in the yard then, and came over, and asked the question he had come to ask, and he asked it badly, which is a thing a man does at a drawknife and not in a chamber.

“Your name,” he said. “I have been in this county seven rollings and I have worked in your county on a keep a week and I have never once said your name out loud where a stranger could hear it, and there is not one book in this county with your name in it, and I have had that on purpose since the first of them because I did not want to hand a name to a man in a country with a book. There is going to be a room in nine months in which the two of us are in it, and I am going to have to say your name in that room, and I have never once said it, and I have just this moment found that I cannot say it, and I would like to say it once here in a yard where it does not go anywhere.”

The woman of sixty-six looked at him and then at Aren and then at the yard, and said, “Sian Pugh.”

“Thank you,” said the man at the drawknife, and did not say it twice, and it was the only time in seven rollings and it was the last time anybody in that county said it, and the man who had gone into the water after the boy of fifteen said it to a woman in a yard and went back to his rail.

She did not use it again herself for the rest of that day, and neither did anybody else, and on the following morning a woman at a sluice asked the minute-man which Pugh kept the seed, because there is a Pugh at a sluice and there is a Pugh in a lane above the market, and he said there is a Pugh who keeps the seed and she does not use it much and she does not want to be asked, and the woman at the sluice said that was a foolish thing to want, and he agreed with her and entered neither of them.

What the flood cost was costed in the fourth week of the eighth rolling, on the guild's board, in front of nineteen men and a slate, and it is the sum that decided the third and fourth chapters of the county's year and nobody in the room understood at the time that it had.

Ferrow put it up himself and did not want to do it.

“Ten days standing in the trench above the town after the fourth week of the sixth rolling, and I counted them and they are thirty-one, and the counting is in the book. At the guild's rate for a man standing in water up to the knee with a pole, which is the same eight pence because a man who wants more for standing in a river is a man who does not understand what he is being paid for, and it is not the guild's rate either, it is the rate of a man in this county, and I am not going to put a north rate on it in a north book.

“Two men, thirty-one days, at eight pence. Eight and eight is sixteen pence the day. Sixteen for thirty-one days is thirty days of sixteen and one more, and thirty days of sixteen is four hundred and eighty pence, and the one more is sixteen, and four hundred and eighty and sixteen is four hundred and ninety-six pence. Elm for two sets of revetment off our own stacks at what the stacks would fetch, ninety-six pence. Four hundred and ninety-six and ninety-six is five hundred and ninety-two pence.”

The bound said, “Twelve marks, one shilling and fourpence.”

“Twelve marks, one shilling and fourpence,” said Ferrow, “which is a sum with a remainder in it, and I have never in nineteen years finished a figure that would not land clean, and I do not like this one any more than the first. Now the second column, and this is the one the room is going to argue about, so I am going to put it up before anybody argues.

“The deck. Six hundred and eight pence of men, which is the four days of nineteen at eight pence, and ninety-six of timber, and seven hundred and four pence, and that deck is at the bottom of the reach in eleven yards of Water and it was the only thing in four hours that anybody in this county was able to hold. The cost of putting it back is not in this book and I am not going to estimate it, and I am going to say why I am not going to estimate it, and the reason is that a man who puts a number on a thing in the wrong part of the year is making a claim, and a claim can be looked at again, and I would rather this figure is the one that is wrong than the one that is right, because the one that is wrong can be corrected by a man with a rope in a year and the one that is right will be cited in nine years by somebody who was not here.”

Then the third column, and this is the one he made the room read twice.

“Four men of a yard from a fen county four hundred miles off, on the council's account, at the guild's rate, from the fourth week of the seventh rolling, and there is no figure against them and there cannot be one, because I cannot foot a sum for men who are not being paid, and a man who is not being paid is not a debt. So the third column is a number of men and a date and the word none, and it is the first time in nineteen years that this book has had a line with nothing in the money column on it, and the line with nothing in it is on the council's account and not the guild's, and the minute-man has put the same line in his book with the hour against it, and there are now two books in this county with a figure that is a number of men, and neither of them can be footed, and in nine years somebody is going to take those two books and put them side by side and say that the county has a debt of four men it cannot name.”

“Which it has,” said the bound.

“Which it has,” said Ferrow, “and I would rather it had it in two books where the figure is men than in one book where the figure is a sum nobody can check, and I would rather that by a long way, and I am not going to explain why tonight because I do not fully know and I have had a bad day and a worse night.”

The council took all three columns onto the council's account at the sitting two days later, which is the first time in nineteen years that the council has taken a debt onto its account that was not somebody's rent, and the minute says the council took the guild's figures and the guild's terms, and the minute says the third column is four men and none, and the minute-man read that line back twice and said in the reading that he was not reading it as a figure because it was not one.

The last thing the flood put in a book was put there by Thessa Wend, in her own hand, in her own column, in a hut at a weir, on the evening of the second day of the second week, and it is one line and it is the only line in eight thousand and thirty-one lines and eleven years that is not a reading of a post, and it is not a reading of a post, and she has not decided whether that makes it the worst line in the book or the only one that is in it.

*Off the old bend at about the sixth hour and a half of the second day. A boy of fifteen in the water off the bend. A man came in off the near bank after him. Read the board at the fourth hour and at the eighth hour. The board was under the winter mark at both. I have written this on a page with a rule on it and there is no column for it and the column for it is the width of the water.*

She showed it to nobody. She read it back herself, once, and she did not improve it, and she put a book-mark in it at the ninth line of the page so that she would find it, and a book-mark in a private book is a thing that cannot be served and cannot be produced and cannot be copied, and she has wanted a reading by somebody who does not own the weir since the first year, and she had one for about an hour and a half on the second day of the second week of the seventh rolling, from a man who does not hold any water in this county and was not going to.

She did not ask him for it. He did not ask her for a line of the book. That was the fourth time in eight rollings that those two people had not asked each other the same thing, and neither of them ever wrote any of the four down, and it is the only piece of work in this account that neither of them can produce.

The eighth rolling came in dry and the Water was four feet below where it had been and falling, and the barley in the four lowest villages was under water in eleven places and gone in nine, and the ground where the Water had been was white, and the ground where the Water had not been was white, and the difference between the two of them was that the new white had no dark inch on the top of it, and a man at a sluice who had scraped ground for forty years said the words about it that the county said about everything, in the plainest possible form, in a few words, to a stranger who had come to help.

“Same colour,” he said. “But it is not the same, and you can have both of them, and one of them will grow something in four years and the other one will grow a stone.”

And then he went back to work, and the eighth rolling went on, and the man who had gone into the water after a boy of fifteen was on the north approach at seven, and the man of sixty was in a yard with a book in it that had a line with nothing in the money column, and the woman of sixty-six was in a stone room with a foot of dried mud in it and a decision she had given herself a year to make and had now had four days to unmake, and the stranger from four hundred miles off was standing at the end of a staging in a county that had let him alone on purpose, with four words in his own book and no reason under them, waiting for the eighth rolling to bring him something he had not asked for.
