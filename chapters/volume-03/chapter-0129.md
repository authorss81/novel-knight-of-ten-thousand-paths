# Chapter 0129

The third rolling drew toward its close with the white ground spreading and the Water high. The council sat with the door open and the lamp unlit, because the light through the slot was enough. Thessa’s nine readings lay on the table beside the minute-book. The yard’s men sat below the window. The woman of sixty-six was not present, having declined to attend anything that might thank her. The woman of sixty on three hearths stood outside on the stair where she could hear.

Ordmar Gace had asked leave to put a proposal before the council, though he held no office and had never read a law. Munn granted it, because the chamber had grown used to hearing persons it had never heard before, and because the gauge woman’s nine sentences had taught the room that a question asked plainly did more work than a term debated.

Gace stood in the middle of the floor where Ferrow had once stood, hat in his hands, and spoke without notes.

He proposed an oath that could be broken. Not lifted by a bench or absolved by an office, but broken on purpose, in public, by the people who swore it, on a day named in advance, with a release in it and a consequence, and no office behind it.

The room was quiet. The minute-man’s pen hovered.

Gace laid it out as a tenant lays out a field. The men who renewed the stone would name a day a rolling ahead. On that day, in the field before the trough, they would speak the renewal and then speak the release: that any household might declare itself loosed from the boundary as sworn, and might work its ground to spare the soil, and might say what it would not plant without forfeit of lease, and that the consequence would be a list. The list would be households to be helped with seed and labour, entered by the council’s own hand, read aloud each sitting. Any person breaking the oath on the named day would stand on the list and take the consequence of being named on it: public knowledge, shared labour, no office to appeal to.

“A rule with no office behind it is a thing that can be argued with,” Gace said. “That is the reason for it. An oath nobody can break is a wall. An oath broken on a day named beforehand is a door. I have not read a law. I have read fields.”

Nobody spoke for a long while. Ferrow looked at his hands. Munn looked at Gace as if he had laid a stone on the table she had carried forty years.

The slate woman asked what would stop landlords taking the fields of men who declared. Gace said nothing would stop them except the public standing of the list, and that was little and was all there was. Thessa asked what would become of the stone. Gace said the stone would stand, because the stone was evidence that anybody had ever agreed to anything, and breaking an oath in public honoured the stone more than keeping it into ruin.

It was the first proposal in the rolling that was not Aren’s. He sat by the slot and felt the room turn toward it the way a field turns toward rain, slowly and without a single face changing. He had offered terms and riders and readings, and the county had refused his terms and taken its own. Now a man of forty-nine on twelve short acres had offered the county its own future in its own language, and the county was listening.

Munn said the council would not decide that day, because a promise kept a hundred and forty times was not answered in an afternoon. She asked the minute-man to enter the proposal as spoken, with the hour, and no opinion. He did.

After the sitting the stair was crowded. The woman on three hearths caught Aren’s sleeve and said she had heard every word through the boards, and that a list of households to be helped was a different thing from a list of persons to be struck, though she could not say how. He said Gace had said it best by not improving on it. She nodded and went down with her basket, already late for her own field.

Aren wrote that night that the rolling had turned, and not by his hand. A tenant who had never read a law had spoken the sentence a rule with no office behind it existed for, and the council had entered it as fact, and the seed keeper’s jars and the wall with two answers stood behind it though she had never entered the room.

He walked to the weir hut before sleep and found Thessa mending a net by lamplight. He told her what Gace proposed, word for word as the minute held it. She listened without setting down her work, and when he finished she said a door named beforehand was still a door, and she had waited eleven years for the county to speak of doors rather than walls. She asked whether the council would take it. He said Munn would carry it the way she carried the stone, heavily and faithfully, and Ferrow would measure it the way he measured water, and the sixty unasked would hear of it through stairs and lanes before any sitting decided.

She asked him, half smiling, whether the stranger would offer anything to match it. He said the stranger had offered Ash’s remedy once in his head and would likely offer it aloud and be refused, because a man with a bag offered what the bag held. She said then the bag would be lighter afterward, which was its own remedy. They parted laughing quietly, which in that rolling was a rare sound.
