# Chapter 0111

The fourth of Rainmonth, YR 313, was a Monday and it was not a market day, and the market in Merefen runs in one street with the water on the left of it, and a town on a Monday in Rainmonth has the particular quiet of a place where nothing has gone wrong yet and everybody is waiting to be told.

The sheet came up the hill at about the sixth hour in a bag with a bolt of hops and a letter for a toll-taker, and it had been sixteen days on the road, which is five days longer than the delegation itself took coming, and there is no post in this kingdom that can tell you whether a thing was sent on the day a waggon left or on the day after it.

It was one sheet, folded twice, and it was in a good hand, and it was not the hand of the woman who had put her hand flat on a table in a guild hall on the ninth of the month before and refused outside command before anybody had asked her a question.

Mara Vey read it standing up, because she was reading it at the window with the market noise not there, and then she sat down and read it again with a rule, because a keeper of records who reads a thing twice with a rule between the readings is doing the second reading properly.

---

**What the sheet said, in the order it said it, and the order is the sheet's own and not hers.**

*To any hand in the fen county that will take it. From the council of the Hearth Marches and from the first hand of its bridge guild, whose name is at the foot.*

*The line your road warden has asked about is not a road. We have said so and we will go on saying so and the six lines stand as they stood on the board.*

*What we have named is a day for four men of a yard. The day is the day those four men will be on our road, and the day is named by the fact of their walking and not by a number, because a number in your counting is not a day in ours and we have had that out with a man in a hall.*

*Four men of a yard of a fen county, on our road, on the day they arrive, with their own tools and their own keep and at their own rate, and the guild's terms stand as the guild's first hand stated them, and a stranger's labour taken at the guild's rate is paid in the county's coin at the crossing and not in goods, and we have had that argument already and lost it in a gateway in Weir Street and we have not come back to it.*

*And no day has been named for the line. There will not be one until the line is made.*

*A sheet is not a road. A road is a line and two hands and a date and a witness at each end. We have one of those four and we are not going to pretend to the other three, and we would be obliged if the county this came to would not print that we have agreed to a road.*

*And the woman of thirty-eight of Sallow Marsh says to say that she has not been asked for a line of the book and has not given one, and that if anybody in that county reads this sheet and finds a line of the book in it, it is not hers.*

*Coll Ferrow, first hand, and the council of the Hearth Marches, the nineteenth day of the rolling that ends with the sowing, which is a day this county cannot convert and has not tried.*

She read the last line four times, because a hand at the foot of a sheet is the only part of a sheet that anybody can be made to answer for, and the name was one she had not got on the ninth, and the man at the drawknife in Weir Street had a man from that county in his yard for a fortnight and had never once said what the man's name was.

And she wrote the sheet out entire, because a copy of a document that has come into a room and gone out of it again is a document that has never existed, and she has been beaten twice this year by a thing that was in a bag for three days and was not copied.

And while she was writing it out she came on the second line of the sheet, which is the one about the four men and their own keep and their own rate, and it is a copy of the guild's third term as that term was stated out loud in a hall in this county twenty-five days ago, and the sentence in the sheet and the sentence in the hall are the same words in the same order, and one of them was spoken by a man standing in the middle of a floor with his back to the bench and the other one is written down four hundred miles from him by somebody who took it down while he said it.

*That is the second time this year a real man's own words have come back from that office in a different hand and been relied on,* she thought, and did not think the rest of it, and the rest of it was that the office has never once asked a man whether the words were his.

Because that is what the foot of the form is for. Every form of that series carries, printed at the foot in a smaller type, the office's own worked example of how a county is to fill a column headed *Keeper* where no office of keeper is found in the county's schedule, and the example is a real name in a real guild, and a man in a fen county wrote *and no other office* in it four years ago and meant every word of it. A clerk in a border county read that example off the foot of a form on a wet day with a quill and put it in the third column of a return about a river, and a different man with the same printed sheet and a bag of string put a seed-carter's name in the fourth, and the fourth is the one the office relied on. A delegation came four hundred miles. And now a first hand of a bridge guild has put his own name under a sentence he said out loud in this county, in a good hand, at the foot of a sheet, and if anybody in that office ever wants to know what a *Keeper* column is for, that name is in a fair copy in this room, and it is the first name in this case that anybody has put anywhere on purpose.

She entered that. It took four lines and it was the entry of the morning.

And the other thing about the sheet, and it is nine words long, and it is the thing nobody in this county will ever be able to check.

The delegation got home. Or it did not.

Eleven days' road, one river crossing, they were in this county a fortnight, they came on the fourth of the month before and they went on the eighteenth on a calendar of their own, and the sheet that came up the hill this morning was written after that, because it answers a thing the delegation was here to ask, and it has been sixteen days on the road, and **there is no instrument in this kingdom that can tell this county whether eleven people got off a waggon, or whether four hundred miles of road in the wrong month is eleven days to a man who has been in it once.**

She wrote that down and then wrote, under it, the sentence she has written four times this year and does not intend to improve:

*Entered the fourth of Rainmonth, YR 313, sixth hour, against no person: that this office cannot establish the arrival of a delegation in a county that is not on any map in this kingdom, and that this is not an inconvenience of the post, it is a property of the county, and that a county with that property is a county in which a thing that happens cannot travel back out of it, and that this is the same property the sixth line of that county's paper is about, and I did not know that this morning and I know it now.*

And the sixth line had been sitting in a fair copy in her own press for eighteen days with a marginal note in her own hand against it, and the sheet had just taken the note away from her.

*Sixth. The line is not to be opened on a day the Marches have not named.*

She had written, in the margin, on the sixteenth of Longlight, in her own hand: *a condition of this office, and the office does not know what it is a condition of, and it is entered against nobody.*

It was not a condition of her office. It was a door, and it was a door with no handle on this side, and she had known that in the way a person knows a thing they have not been given the whole of: she had known it since the sheet came up the hill and she had got to it about eleven seconds after she had finished reading the third line, and the eleven seconds were spent at a window with a bag of hops going stale on the sill.

*It is not a condition of this office at all,* she wrote in the day-book, *and it is not a condition of a road. It is a condition of a calendar, and the calendar is four hundred miles off, and the two of us are the only people in this kingdom who are both old enough to know that a door which opens on somebody else's day is not a shut door.*

*Entered the fourth of Rainmonth, YR 313, sixth hour, against no person: that the sixth line has been read, in this room, in a sheet from a county that is not on any map in this kingdom, and that this office can now say what the sixth line is for, and that what it is for is that the county on the other end of it holds the only key to it, and that the key is a day, and that a day in that county is not a number in this one, and that there is not one of those in the other.*

*And entered: that this office has had that knowledge in the margin of a fair copy for three weeks and did not have it until a sheet came up the hill, and that a thing written in a margin in one's own hand is a claim and not a record, and that a claim about a person can be contradicted and a claim about a door cannot, and that this is the third time this year that this office has been wrong in the same direction and there is no procedure for being wrong in the same direction twice.*

She read that back and did not improve it, and the third rule does not improve sentences, and she signed nothing.

**And at about the ninth hour the coast mail was made up in the lane behind the weigh-house, and it goes on a Tuesday whether or not anybody has anything to say.**

There were two things in it that had not been in it the month before, and one of them was four hundred miles off and the other one was four hundred miles the other way, and neither of them was a letter.

The first was a fair copy of a column of eleven counties, made in her own hand on a sheet of this office's paper, with the date against it and no claim number, because there is nobody to serve: **eleven counties, eight with a boundary oath renewed in public on a stone in a year anybody there can name, three with nothing in the leaf at all, and one question at the foot of it, and the question is the head of the column.** It was going to a counting house in a city that does not admit to the Crown that it exists, to a man who had broken four of his own conditions on purpose and minuted the reason and signed the last line of it.

*You told me once that a thing I do arithmetic on is not a thing I have looked at, and you were right, and I have looked at it four times since and it is still a column.* And then the question, and the question is nine words long: *what is the head of this column, and whose hand is it in, and is it a head or a rule.*

It is not a message. A message is addressed to a person and a person can answer it or refuse it or lose it, and a message that is not answered is a thing somebody can be angry about. **A copy is addressed to a shelf.** She wrote that on the wrapper, because a copy that is sent to a person becomes a message by the act of sending it, and a copy that is sent to a shelf is a copy, and the whole of the difference is a line on a wrapper in a woman's hand and it is the only line in the bag.

**And the cost of it, in pence, because a keeper of records who sends a thing four hundred miles and does not know what it costs is a keeper who has sent a thing four hundred miles and is not a keeper.** A rider to that coast is a day and a half out and a day and a half back at the yard rate of eight pence a day, and there are two men because a letter bag goes with him and a man alone on that road does not come back, and thirty-two pence a day for three days is ninety-six, and a sheet, a stick of wax and the boy's copying at the office's own rate come to six more, and ninety-six and six is one hundred and two pence, **which this office has never spent on a letter and is spending on a column, and the entry is against the office and against no person, and the reason is that a column of eleven counties has been read four times in this room and has produced one question, and a question that has never left the building is not a question, it is a habit.**

The second was a fair copy of six lines of chalk off a board of lath and lime, in the county's own words, unaltered, which she made on the thirteenth of Longlight at the request of the man who keeps the minute and which has been in the press ever since with no use and no hurry on it.

She looked at it for a while.

"I am not sending that on a Tuesday because the post goes on a Tuesday," she said, to nobody, which is a thing she has done four times in four years and a half and has never entered.

And she put it in.

And then, because a copy that goes into a bag is a copy that somebody in that county can produce in a room, she read the third rule on the page over the bench-board where it has been since the sixteenth of the month before, in her own hand, and it says an authority is entered with the words that made it, and she went back and wrote the six lines at the foot of her copy in full, unaltered, with the date against them and the hour and the fact that they came off a board of lath and lime that is standing in the guild hall over a wharf in this county and has not been taken down or taken away.

Which means that for the first time in four years and a half, a thing written by a county that is not on any map in this kingdom is in a book in this county that anybody may come up two flights of a stair and read, and the words are the county's own and not hers, and the only thing she has done is to rule a page and copy them out, and a copyist is allowed to add nothing.

And he came up the two flights at about the tenth hour with a chalk stub in his hand and a tool bag left at the bottom of the stair because the stair is too tight for a tool bag and has been since the first year.

"Twenty-second," he said. "I am out of this county on the twenty-second at the fourth hour and I do not know how long for, and the sheet came up the hill this morning and named a day for four men of a yard and did not name one for me, and I want to tell you a number and nothing else tonight."

"Give me the number."

"The twenty-second, at the fourth hour, and it is a Friday and it is not a market day, and I have checked that twice because I have been out of this county on a market morning twice and lost a market both times and I am not doing it a third time by inattention."

She wrote it down and put the hour in the margin, because rule one is a rule about a book and not about a man.

"You said a number and nothing else," she said. "You also said the sheet named a day for four men of a yard and did not name one for you, and you said that in the same breath as the number and in the same eleven seconds, and those are two facts and one of them is a number and the other one is a question, and I am not going to ask you the question because you have not made it a question yet."

"I know."

"I have not made it a question," said Mara Vey, "and the ninth was entered on the sixteenth of the month before and I am not entering a tenth about this, because entering a tenth would be entering a question, and the rule is that I enter the name of the thing and not the name of a person, and **the thing is not a question. The thing is a sheet with a name at the foot of it that I did not have on the ninth of the month before, and there is a man in Weir Street who has had that name in his yard for a fortnight and did not say it, and I have no instrument that will make either of them tell me, and this office does not ask a tradesman for a name in his own yard.**"

He put the chalk stub on the table, which he has done perhaps forty times, and it rolled a quarter turn and stopped.

"The two marks and fourpence is still in the chest," he said. "And the bench has been asked four times."

"It has been asked four times," she said, "and it has said yes four times, and it is not a bench, and it knows it, and the minute carries that from the second time and I have never once asked that it be taken out."

They worked for another hour and a half and it was the first time in a month that the two of them had done a piece of work together that was not about the rules, and it was about a sheet and a column and a fair copy, and at about the last hour she said, without looking up, the thing she had been not-saying since the sixth hour, and it was not a question.

"A condition of this office," she said, "and it never was, and I have crossed it out in the margin in my own hand, and a keeper who alters her own fair copy enters the alteration with the date and the hour, and so I am entering it, and the entering is the whole of the evening."

*Entered the fourth of Rainmonth, YR 313, eleventh hour, by the keeper, against no person: the marginal note of the sixteenth of Longlight against the sixth line is withdrawn as wrong, in the keeper's own hand, on the ground that the sixth line is not a condition of this office, and the office has been wrong in the same direction three times this year and has no procedure for it, and the last of the three is entered above and is a fact about a door and not about an office.*

The two of them could not have said afterwards which of them had decided to do it, and it was done, and the pen was down, and the day went on being a Monday.

**On the Tuesday the mail went out of the lane behind the weigh-house with a fair copy in it and a column of eleven counties in it and no name in it, and the bag went south to a coast and east to a city that does not admit to the Crown that it exists, and the road warden's name was not on either of the two, and neither of the two was sent because of anything that happened in this town in the last ten days, and the seventh line of the six did not exist, because there are six.**

And a woman of about fifty-five in a county four hundred miles off put a hand flat on a board that had come in a wagon and told two men in a chamber over a tithe barn that the line is not a road, and one of the two men was sixty and had a spliced line across the back of his right hand, and neither of them said the word *gate* and the door has no handle on this side and there is not one of those in the other.
