# Chapter 0138

The keeper read the form back the way he read everything back, flat, with the hour, and then he read the reason above it back as well, because he had written it and had not heard anybody object to it, and then he shut the book and said one thing before anybody else spoke.

“I want the room to know what has just happened in this book, because I have been keeping it twenty-two years and I have never once had this in it. There is a form in here from nineteen years back that says a bench will decline a thing, and there is no form in here that says a bench will decline a thing and a man will explain why in the same sitting, and there is not one in here that says a stranger asked a bench to put its hand to nine days of the county's water and was refused, and there is not one that says the refusal was correct. All four of those are now in this book, and I put all four of them in, and the reason I put the fourth one in is that a book that records a refusal without recording that it was right is a book that invites the next man to try again, and I have not got the years left to keep a book that invites men.”

“Then enter that it is my judgement and not the council's,” said Munn.

“It is entered in your words and not in mine and the two of us know the difference and nobody in nine years will.”

Ferrow put a book on the table. It was the book that had been open in the guild room with the plan in it since the twelfth rolling of the year before, and it was open at a page with nineteen lines on it in a first hand's own writing, and the first line was a month, and the month was the twelfth rolling of the year before, and the seventh of this year is the rolling before the man with the bar came up the stair.

“Before he came,” said Ferrow. “Before the council agreed his route, and before his terms, and before the Water. It is in the twelfth rolling of the year before and it is the same plan with four stops in it, and I want the room to hear me say that I put four stops in a plan about water in a rolling in which the water was sitting where it always sits, and I want the room to hear that the reason is not that I am a careful man. The reason is that a man writing a plan in the twelfth rolling of the year before in this county has to put a stop in it, because the plan is about the Water and the Water in this county does the opposite of what a plan wants, and the only two ways of being wrong about it are to have no stop, in which case you find out at four in the morning, or to have a stop, in which case you find out at the stop.”

Then he read the nineteen lines, and Aren Kest stood at the bottom of the room and listened to a plan for nine days of water in a county he had been in for seven rollings, and it was better than his, and it was better in three places and he could name all three inside about four minutes and the fourth minute was the one that took him.

His sheet was keyed to the hour. It began at the second hour of the first morning and every task was at an hour, because a sequence of hours is what a bridgewright makes and a bridgewright's whole trade is that an hour is a promise you make to a man with a bar, and if the hour is wrong the man is standing about at the wrong hour on a wet day four hundred yards from a room.

Ferrow’s was keyed to the water. Nothing in it was at an hour. It said that the trench at the head would be worked until the water at the top of it was below a mark, and that the trough would be worked until the water in the trough was below another mark, and that the four villages’ ditches would be worked only while the pond against the low side of the bank was not rising, and that on the last three days of the nine nothing would be worked at all, whatever the hour said and whatever any stranger’s sheet said, and the reason for the last three days was in the nineteenth line and the reason was that the bank above the pond would be coming down and a man under it in the last three days of a rolling is a man who is working on the day the ground stops taking a barrow.

And the three places his sheet was better: it had a sequence, and Ferrow’s had a stop; it had the trough in the second week, and so did Ferrow’s; it had the four villages before the week the bank came down, and Ferrow’s had them after, which was the only one of the three that was plainly wrong and Ferrow had put it where he could see it.

“It is on the council’s account and not the guild’s, because the council takes the plan and the work is the guild’s and that is the arrangement in this county,” said Ferrow, “and I will cost it before anybody agrees to it, because a plan that has not been costed is a wish. Nineteen of mine at eight pence the day is a hundred and fifty-two pence the day, and the four men of the yard are on the council’s account now and at the guild’s rate, which is thirty-two pence the day between them, and a hundred and fifty-two and thirty-two is a hundred and eighty-four pence the day. Nine days is a week, and a week of that is six days, because the last three days are not worked, so: a hundred and eighty-four, and a hundred and eighty-four, and a hundred and eighty-four, and a hundred and eighty-four, and a hundred and eighty-four, and a hundred and eighty-four, which is a thousand one hundred and four pence. Twenty-three marks exactly, and it lands clean because the plan says stop, which is the only arithmetic joke I have got in nineteen years and it is also true.”

“Twenty-three marks,” said the bound, from the end of the bench.

“Twenty-three marks that the county has not got and will have by the fourth week of the ninth rolling or will not have at all, and I have said that out loud before the room so that nobody in nine years can say I did not,” said Ferrow.

He did not say anything for a while. The minute-man had his pen up and the room was watching a stranger with a bar decide whether to say a true thing about himself in public, and it was not a long wait, and everything the rest of that sitting turns on is whether he does.

“It is better than mine,” Aren said. “In three places, and I can name two of them, and the third is the stops, and the stops are all of it. A sheet keyed to the hour has no last day. You cannot foot a plan that has no last day, and I cannot cost mine, and that is not a complaint about my arithmetic. It is a complaint about my sheet, and I put four stops in it myself this morning off her board and I put them in as times, and a stop has to be a thing you can see, and a thing you can see is a mark on a board, and a mark on a board is a reading, and I had a reading and I made it into an hour, and an hour is not a reading, and it is a thing I learned four hundred miles off and forgot in the fourth week of the fifth rolling.”

“You did not forget it,” said Ferrow. “You had nine readings and you would not have the book and you were right not to, and the reading you could not have is the reading you would have needed at the stop, and that is not a forgetting, that is the cost of doing it properly, and I have paid it every year for nineteen years with about forty men who are on my account and not on the roll.”

“Enter what he said,” said Munn, to the minute-man, “and enter that he said it in this room and not in a yard, and enter the hour, and enter the three places he can name, because a book that says a man conceded a thing without saying which thing is a book that has thrown the concession away.”

The minute-man entered it. It is the longest single entry in that book and it is the only one in twenty-two years that contains a man's judgement of his own work in the third person and at his own request, and it ends: *and the stranger said that the guild's plan was better than the sheet he had brought up the stair, and named the three places, and the room heard him, and the hour.*

Then the hand-over, and it took four minutes, and it is the shortest thing in this account and the only one that happened in the county's own form with nobody raising his voice.

The council’s minute, in the hand of a man who keeps a minute, reads:

*Entered, in the second week of the eighth rolling: the council takes the guild's plan of the twelfth rolling of the year before, with the four stops, at the guild's terms and the guild's rate, and the work is the guild's work, and the council does not order it.*

*And it is entered that the causeway and the waterworks above the market town are the guild's, and that the stranger who brought the sheet is of no council and holds no office in this county and may not be given one by this council, and that he asked to be put under the council's hand and was not, and that the reason entered by the council is the reason entered above and is not shortened.*

Nobody argued about that last line and one person asked about it, which was the woman with the slate.

“Do you want that in the same hand as the rest of it,” she said to the minute-man, “or do you want it kept back on a separate leaf, because there are men in this county who will read the two places in a different order and get a different county out of them.”
“Kept in the same hand and the same book and the same run of the page,” said the minute-man, “because a separate leaf is a thing a person can take away, and this is not a thing anybody is taking away, it is a thing this county did and this county is to have it in one place in a good hand with the hour against it. And I will read it out to anybody who comes up two flights of that stair, and that is the use of a fair copy.”

So the county made a fair copy, and the fair copy went on the board under the bench where anybody coming up two flights could read it, and the minute-man read all of it aloud to the man at the drawknife and to the man of fifty-two from the fen yard and to a woman of about sixty on three hearths, who had come up out of the village, having heard the whole sitting through the boards, to hear it in the room, which she had not done in two rollings.

Nobody stopped her. The room made a space. That is a thing a room does and it is not a small thing and nobody in that room has ever been told that they did it.
And the cost of the eighth rolling, set out in one line, is this: a man from four hundred miles off came up a stair he had entered in his own book that he would not come up, with an instrument nobody asked him for, and it was refused in nine words, and the reason was minuted by a woman who gave it in a room and not in a yard, and the plan that replaced his was written before he arrived, and he said so out loud, and it went into a book in a good hand with the hour against it, and the book cannot be served, and the county has no office and no writ and no bell and no schedule, and there is no instrument in this kingdom that can make that book answer a man four hundred miles off, or a stranger, or a bench.

Which is exactly what the man who keeps it wanted, and exactly what the man who came up the stair did not want, and neither of them said so, and the sheet he had brought is folded in a notebook in a coat in a room over a lane in a county four hundred miles off with a bar in it that he is not to have, and the four words he wrote in the fourth week of the fifth rolling are in no book at all, and the withdrawal he entered is now a thing with nothing to withdraw to, and that is a cost, and it is the first one in this county that has come out of a room and gone into a page and stayed there.

The last hour of the eighth rolling was given to the forty ditchmen, who had been on the floor of that room since the third hour of the morning, and who were not in the minute at all, in any form, under any head, and who heard Ferrow read his nineteen lines aloud to the room twice and who asked him four questions and got four straight answers, and who then went out and dug.

The four men of the fen yard did not go out, and the man at the drawknife said why, and he said it to Ferrow and not to the bench.

“Our ninety days ran out in the fourth week of your seventh rolling and we are on your account and the account is not paid,” he said. “There are four of us and there is a bank coming down in a mile and there is nobody in this county who can set a sill on a mud bottom who has not set one on a mud bottom before, and we are going to be here, and I am not asking you to find the money, because I know there is none, and I am telling you so that the four of us are a decision and not a convenience.”

“Whose decision.”

“Ours,” said the man at the drawknife. “All four. It was made in about nine minutes on the north approach in the second week of your seventh rolling and we did not tell you and I am telling you now because the bench has just been told what a stranger is and I am not going to be a thing in this county that somebody finds out from a book.”

The man of fifty-two said one thing, and it was not about himself.

“We are not on your oath,” he said, to Ferrow, “and we are not on your roll, and we have not been asked what we swore, and we are not going to be, and I would like that put in whatever that is.” He put his thumb on the chalk board with the sums on it. “And I would like it put in the same place as the four of us, so that a man reading this in nine years finds the four men and the asking in the same four inches of page.”

Ferrow looked at him for a while. “Put it in the guild's book and not the council's,” he said, “because the council's minute is a bench and a bench can be asked to answer and a guild's book is a trade and a trade cannot, and I would rather the four of us were in a book that cannot be made to answer for us than in one that can. And put the date on it, and not the hour, because I have never in nineteen years entered an hour against a thing I did not want to be exact about.”

It is in the guild's book in the guild's hand in nineteen words with a date and no hour, and it is the second line in that book with nothing in a money column, and the four men of a fen county are the only persons in the history of the Hearth Marches bridge guild who have ever asked to be entered as not having sworn anything, and the guild's first hand wrote it without one word of comment, which is the only comment anybody in that county has ever made on it.
