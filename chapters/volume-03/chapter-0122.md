# Chapter 0122

The lowest field of Sallow Marsh lay flat under a thin rain. Eleven people from the room over the barn stood in the middle of it in their boots, with Coll Ferrow’s two alongside, and Thessa Wend of Sallow Marsh with a boy of fourteen who carried a pole, and Aren at the edge where a stranger stands when he has not been invited to stand anywhere else.

Round the headland and along the ditch stood perhaps sixty other people. Nobody had sent for them. A bench walking into a field draws a field the way a bell draws a lane.

The ground was the colour of bone. Wet, it gave under the boot and came up on the sole as a pale smear with grit in it, and the grit was angular, between a nail head and a pinhead, and it scored the skin if rubbed.

The boy put his hand down without being told and held it out. Half a dozen others did the same. It was the ordinary gesture of this county, and nobody commented on it.

“You scrape it,” Ferrow said to Aren, because Aren was looking. “Flat iron. It comes up in flakes thick as a coin. Underneath there is dark ground for a year, and then it pales again. The year it holds is the year you are living in.”

The minute-man knelt, touched his finger to his tongue, frowned, and said it was salt. Thessa said it had been salt since before the oldest man in the field was born, and that it was nobody’s doing, and that she wanted that said before anything else, because blame would find a home here within a year if the room let it.

The woman with the slate stood in a furrow with her boots gone and asked how Thessa knew it for salt and not ash or lime off a drain. She asked it the way a person asks when the form in her head is the wrong shape.

Thessa answered in the language of a person who has read a post twice a day for eleven years. She said the salt lay a foot and a half down and had lain there since the ice, and did not travel on its own, and did not climb, and came up with water and would go down again if the water were taken away. She said the water could not be taken away, because the water was the boundary.

Then she turned and held her hand out toward the ditch, eleven yards off the trough at the head of the field, where the bank on the far side had gone over on one line.

“That ditch has moved,” she said. “It stands eleven yards off the trough and it did not always. The bank has gone over on the same line, in the same part of the season, at the same stage of the water, for as long as I have walked it. A bank worn back that way goes a hand’s width in a year and no more. I have walked that line twice a year for eleven years and I can put a year to four stretches of it from the top of the bank.”

Delya Munn watched her without moving. “You have not said a hundred and forty.”

“No,” Thessa said. “I have said eleven. I have read water since I was twenty-seven and the ditch was moving long before I could walk to it. I have never seen a year of it beyond my own eleven, and I will not stand here and swear the rest. But I will say that a ditch moved a hand’s width in a year for as long as this county has kept a stone will have moved about a hand’s width for as long as the stone has been kept, and that every one of those years men were asked what they swore and gave the same answer, and the answer is on the stone and not in the water.”

Aren said nothing. He had stood in that ditch nine days earlier with a pole against a bank that gave, and he had written seen, not told in his book with the hour against it, and the third of the six terms forbade the north sending anybody who could be asked to come and look. A man with a bag in a field in the first rolling was the man that line was written about. He kept his hands at his sides and his mouth shut, and the rain ran off his coat onto ground the colour of bone.

Ferrow stood eleven feet away with his hands behind him and looked at Aren once and did not speak. The look was not unkind. It was a first hand deciding that a thing he had carried since the ninth day belonged in this field to the woman of thirty-eight who had lived here all her life, and letting it belong to her.

Afterward at the trough, with rain coming off the low roof behind them, Ferrow said quietly that he had watched Aren not speak, and would not tell the council what he knew Aren had not said, and would not speak of it aloud either, because the third of the six and the guild’s own terms were better reasons than any of his. Aren said he would not have let him speak of it anyway. Ferrow said he knew.

The council climbed back up the two flights and sat at the door leaves and had to find a position, and the position took the afternoon. Munn put it in her own sentence. Nothing could be done to the salt, not in this rolling and not in this year and not by anybody in the room. A man would tell them the salt was in the ground and be right, and tell them the water taken away would sink it in four years and be right, and neither truth was a thing a council could do in a morning.

The salt scrape went on below them while they talked, barrows to the boats on the first rise, weighed at nine pence the bushel by the county’s own figure. The four lowest villages had produced more each year for nine years than anyone could remember, and the county had not been so well supplied in living memory, and the money and the ruin were the same money, and nobody in the lamp-lit room said so until the minute-man entered the ditch is not at the trough and the nine days and and no wage, and left the reason out because his book held facts and the reason was a hand’s width of clay laid down once a year for longer than the book went back.

Late in the sitting Munn asked that the minute carry that the stranger had stood an hour and a half in the field and cost the council nothing. Aren inclined his head. Munn said she was not being kind but accurate, and that he knew the difference because he had not corrected her.

That night he wrote in his notebook that two parties in this county understood the work better than he did, one fifty-five who would not have the stone lifted and was right about the history, one thirty-eight who had said in nine sentences what he had carried since the ninth day, and that neither knew they held the same fact, and that the only reason the fact still lived in this county and not in a book four hundred miles off was that he had kept his mouth shut in a wet field, and that keeping still was not a skill.

Beyond the barn, round the headland, the sixty had stood all morning and had not been asked a single question. Among them, eleven feet from him in the rain, had stood a woman of about sixty on three hearths, watching the bench watch the ground.

He found her afterward by the ditch, wringing rain from her shawl. She said the bench had looked at the ground the way men look at a debt, and had she been asked she would have told them the field had been white since her daughters were girls, and no one asked, and so she told him instead, because he was the only person present who had not decided in advance what the field meant.

They talked of beans and barley, of what the white ground would still carry. She grew beans on the higher of her three hearths and let the lowest lie, and the middle she sowed to barley when the season allowed. She spoke of yields without figures, in bushels remembered as weight in the hand, and he understood that her knowledge was a gauge column of its own, kept nowhere and asked for never.

Ferrow joined them with mud to his knees, and asked her outright what she would do with the ditch if the ditch were hers to order. She said she would leave it to men who understood water, which was both an answer and a refusal to answer, and Ferrow accepted it as such, because he knew the difference.

On the walk back the boy with the pole fell into step beside Aren and asked whether the north had white ground. Aren said the north had its own ruins, peat and flood and fever, and every county thought its own the only kind until it stood in another’s field. The boy considered this with the gravity of fourteen and said the Sallow foot read higher every year, and his mistress wrote it down, and nobody read what she wrote, and did Aren think reading would help. Aren said being read by someone who did not own the weir would help, and the boy nodded as if that were obvious, which to the boy it was.

That night the council’s room buzzed like a hive poked with a stick. The minute-man read the entry thrice over to get the wording right. Munn sat silent a long while before she spoke of wages and weeks and the far side that would not yield four inches. No one proposed anything. The salt and the mechanism lay on the table between them, said in trade terms by the gauge woman and not by any stranger, and the question nobody asked hung over the lamp smoke: how to keep a promise renewed a hundred and forty times when the keeping was ending the keepers.
