# Chapter 0114

The twelfth of Rainmonth, YR 313, was a Tuesday and it was not a market day, and the guild hall over the wharf holds about forty people standing and eleven sitting when a thing is important enough, and on a Tuesday in the middle of a month eleven people came, and nine of them were the bench, and the tenth was a reeve, and the eleventh was the clerk who keeps the roll.

The board is against the end wall.

It is four feet by two and a half, of lath and lime, and the second line had been gone over twice, and four men of a council of a county four hundred miles off had carried it four hundred miles in a waggon with one river crossing in it, on a road that is not this county's road, in a month when the waggon was carrying a delegation and a return and a salt bill, and it is standing in a hall over a wharf in a fen county leaning at about four degrees because the wall behind it is older than the board.

**And it is still here because the man who keeps the minute of that council said one sentence before he got back on the waggon, and the sentence was in the roll of this hall on the ninth of the month before, and it is: *it is no use to us, a board is no use to a court, and the copy in your book is the use.***

That took the whole of it, and the reason it took the whole of it is the difference between a board and a book, and Aldis Fenn has been a reeve for nine years and had it in front of her for about as long as it takes to read one line, and it is this: **a board cannot be copied out of.** There is no proceeding by which a court takes a board. Nobody can serve a notice on lime and lath. What the board has is the thing it was in the room for, and the thing a board has that a book has not is that nobody at any remove can come and say *I have read your terms* and put it down as read, because there is nothing on it to read out of except the person standing in front of it.

And **a copy in a book is a thing a court can have.** A fair copy in a book in a room over a weigh-house is evidence, and evidence can be produced, and a thing that can be produced can be compared, and once a thing can be compared it is not the county's own hand any more, it is a text, and a text can be held up against a person.

So the county brought a board, and left it, and went home, and sent a copy in a book to a woman who makes them for a living, and between those two facts is the whole of what this hall did this morning.

And on the twelfth of Rainmonth, at about the fifth hour, a man in that hall found out that the first of those two sentences is not quite true, and he found it out with a stick of whitechalk and a yard of linen, and he meant well, and it is the only correction this county has made to anything that came out of that wagon.

He is a schoolmaster's son who keeps a press for print and does the notices for three societies in this town, and he wanted a rubbing of the six lines so that the town could have one of its own, and he had the linen on the second line and chalk on about nine inches of it when the man at the drawknife said one word.

The word was *no*.

The man at the drawknife is twenty-nine and has been in that yard since he was eleven and does all the arithmetic, and what he said was this: "Put that down and do it again in chalk on your own paper, and take it to the woman over the weigh-house, and pay her, and I will tell you why in one sentence and then I will not say it again."

"Because a board cannot be copied out of, and you have just copied out of it, and that is a thing a hall can produce."

"It is a rubbing."

"It is a copy of six lines off a board of lath and lime, in your hand, made in a hall, and in nine years it is a piece of paper in this town saying what a county four hundred miles off requires, and it was made by a man with no office in it and no one to be asked whether it is right. **The whole reason that board was worth four hundred miles of waggon to that council is that a rubbing can be taken off it and a copy cannot be taken off a thing that is only itself.**"

They stood in front of the wall for about a minute. Then the schoolmaster's son wiped the linen round with his sleeve, which took the chalk off and left the lime a shade paler where it had been, and asked whether the board would want cleaning, and was told by the bench-clerk to be still and to keep off the wall.

And the bench-clerk entered the whole of it, with the hour, on the ground that a thing a man does in a room to a document that came from a county that is not on any map in this kingdom is a fact about the room and not a fact about the county, and the roll of that hall now carries in four lines and in a copyist's hand the first recorded instance in this county of somebody trying to take a thing off a document that had come from a place that could not be asked about it.

"Enter the schoolmaster's son and the linen," said the toll-holder.

"Enter the linen and the hour," said the bench-clerk, "and enter that the schoolmaster's son is not named, because rule two is entered against this hall in the same book and on the same page as the entry about a man paid six pence twice out of a box with no name on it, and I am not going to be the second one."

---

What the bench was asked, and the asking took about four minutes, and it was asked by a road warden who is on the roll and has a vote and does not hold the crossing and did not vote.

"Put the six lines to the bench," he said, "on the copy, not on the board, and I want it read twice, and I want the reading of the second one to be a different person than the reading of the first one, and I want it in the roll in full with the hour."

And that was the first thing anybody in this hall understood about the six lines, which is that they are not the north's terms and they are not the county's terms, they are the price of a thing nobody has agreed to buy yet, and a price is a thing you can be asked to pay.

The bench-clerk read them off a fair copy made in a room over a weigh-house on the sixteenth of the month before, and the words are the county's words and were not altered:

*First. The road is not to be made to carry a person.*
*Second. No name is to be read out on the far side of the line by anybody from the north, and no name is to be written in the line's book at the north end.*
*Third. The north is not to send anybody who can be asked to come and look.*
*Fourth. The stone is not to be renewed in the north's presence.*
*Fifth. The road is kept by a hand at each end and a witness at each end, and the witness at the Marches' end is chosen by the Marches.*
*Sixth. The line is not to be opened on a day the Marches have not named.*

The second reading was by the toll-holder's clerk and he read the sixth one slower than the other five, because it is the shortest of the six and because he is a careful man.

**And then somebody said the word price, and the hall found out that a reeve of nine years and a bench of nine had been talking about the same six lines for three weeks and had arrived at three different numbers.**

The man at the drawknife said the fourth. The stone is not to be renewed in the north's presence, and that is the price, and a man who is asked not to be in a field is a man who is being asked to be somewhere else, and there is no other reason anybody has ever had for it.

The toll-holder's clerk said the sixth. A door. You do not build a door, you agree when to open it, and a thing you agree when to open is a thing somebody else has the key to.

Aren Kest, from the back, where he stands because he does not hold the crossing:

"**The fourth is the price and the sixth is the door,** and they are not competing and the reason is about nine seconds long if anybody wants it, and I have been carrying it about for three weeks and I have not said it out loud, and I will say it now and I will be wrong in about half the room."

He was asked for the nine seconds and gave them, and they are the length of nine seconds.

"A man from the north stands in a field and watches four men lay a stone, and in nine years there is a room in which a stranger stood in a field. The stranger is a document. The document is then the only evidence anybody has about the stone. And the stone is the only thing in that county that has lasted a hundred and forty years, and it has lasted it because men who are still alive can be asked what they swore. So the fourth line is the price of keeping that, and it is not a small price, and they have not asked us for anything else as large."

"And the sixth," said the reeve.

"And the sixth is the door, and I have had that in a marginal note in a book in this town for three weeks with the note in the keeper's own hand saying *a condition of this office and the office does not know what it is a condition of*, and on Monday she found out what it is a condition of, and it is not a condition of her office, and I am not going to pretend to know why she has not told me that."

Nobody in the hall asked him how he knew what was in a book in a room over a weigh-house, and that not asking is in the roll, because the bench-clerk entered it, and the bench-clerk has entered four things this year that he was not asked to enter and two of them were against himself.

And the fifth line is the witness line, and a witness at each end is the one thing in that hall that a bench of nine could have put its name to in about four seconds, and it did not, and here is why, and it is the same reason as the ninth of Embermonth of the year before, word for word, and she had been asked and had said no and had said no in a gateway on Weir Street on the ninth of Embermonth at eleven o'clock at night with the market audible four streets off, and Tovan Kest had written it down, and the man at the drawknife had said it was the best thing anybody in the town had said in a year, and it has not been improved on since and it is not going to be improved on now.

"I am not going to sign the fifth line," said Reeve Aldis Fenn, and put her hand flat on the copy, which is not what she did last time, and last time she put the rule down, and a reeve who has changed the gesture has been thinking about it for three weeks.

"I want it minuted that I was asked to and that I said no, and I want the reason minuted in the words I am about to use and not in better ones, because if it is minuted in better ones then in nine years a man will read that a reeve of Merefen had a reason, and it will not be my reason."

**"I am the reeve of this town. A reeve is the town's witness. That is what the word means and I have been it for nine years and I have wanted to vote on this guild's own matter for nine years and I have not, and I will not stand on that line today, because a road this town does not own cannot have this town as its witness, and if it has, then in four years somebody will say the town stood there, and the town will be right and the road will be shut, and the road will have been shut by a signature and not by an argument, and an argument is a thing that can be had again on a different day by a different bench with a different temper in it."**

The hall did not say anything, and a hall that does not say anything on the second reading of a thing is a hall that has heard it once.

"And the difference this morning," she said, "is that the fifth line has a slot in it for me and the fifth line is the only one of the six with a slot in it for anybody in this room, and I want it minuted that I noticed the slot."

**"Enter the whole," said the bench-clerk.**

"Enter the whole, and enter that I voted on it this morning with my hand, and that I am not going to stand on a line about a thing I have voted on, and that I have thought about that for five months and I have got to the bottom of it and the bottom of it is that a man may do one of the two and not the other and it took me nine years to find out which of the two I would rather do and it is not the one I thought when I started."

And the clerk entered it, and what he entered in the place where a roll carries a thing is what the roll has carried since the ninth of Greenmonth, and it is the shortest possible way of saying that a reeve voted and declined to witness and the roll cannot tell the difference and the bench can:

*The reeve voted.*

**And the bench voted on the six lines as six lines, which is a thing the roll of this hall has never had before, and it was seven for and two against, and the two against are the toll-holder and a man who keeps eels, and the toll-holder's reason is that the sixth line puts a county's calendar in charge of a road and a man cannot get a road when the answer to a letter is a season, and the man who keeps eels said nothing and voted against because the third line is the north's protection and not ours and he is against any protection that is not his.**

And the vote was carried and it is in the roll and the six lines stand in a copy in this county's hands, and a line made of those six conditions can be made by this town, and the making of it is three days of work and about two marks and twelve pence, and there is no money in the room, and the reeve asked that the minute carry that too, and the clerk entered that too.
