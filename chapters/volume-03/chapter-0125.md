# Chapter 0125

The day after the naming, Aren walked the Sallow foot with the boy of fourteen and the pole, and did not touch the water. The boy read the post and Aren read after him, and the boy got it a finger out and then got it right, the way Ferrow had taught him. They parted at the weir hut without much talk. The boy had learned to be looked at, and a stranger looking was still a weight.

In the market town Aren found the roll. It lived in a chest in the room under the chamber, in the minute-man’s keeping, on leaves sewn with string. The minute-man let him look because Aren had asked in the right way, for nothing to copy and nothing to carry away, only to understand what the council meant when it spoke of itself.

The roll was a list of households. Against each stood a mark for the oath, or no mark. He ran his finger down the four white villages and counted under his breath, and stopped, and counted again, because the number mattered and he did not want to carry a wrong one.

About sixty households stood on the roll with no mark against them. Alive, on the roll, paying in salt and in labour when labour was asked, and not on the oath, because the oath was renewed by the men who held the water and they did not hold water.

He closed the chest and thanked the minute-man, and went out into the lane with the number sitting in him like a stone.

The woman of about sixty on three hearths was waiting at the corner with a basket of beans. She had lived forty-one years on ground that went white, had buried two men, and had told him weeks earlier that nobody had ever asked her what she swore because she had never sworn anything. She looked at him now as at a man carrying something heavy.

“You have seen it,” she said.

“I have seen the roll,” he said.

“And nobody in that room has asked us what we swore.”

“No.”

She shifted the basket. “My hearths were in the water last year, four rows under. The men who renew the stone walk past my door to the field. They are good men. They have never asked, because the stone is renewed by men who hold the ditch, and I do not hold a ditch, and there is no form anywhere that says a woman may.”

He wanted to ask her what she would swear if asked, and did not, because asking in a lane would be a promise he could not keep. Instead he asked whether she had been in the chamber for the naming. She said she had stood outside on the stair and heard it through the boards, and had heard his answer and had not understood one word of it, and had been glad he gave no more.

“A refusal with a date in it is still a refusal,” she said. “My grandmother used to say a thing lost can be looked for and a thing refused cannot. We had no word for the second until lately. Now the young barge hand uses it as if he grew it.”

They walked a little way together. She told him the sixty were not a party and not a league and not a grievance, only households that paid and were not asked, and that the not asking had gone on so long it had become a kind of weather. He told her the council had entered the rider charge so it did not fall on them, and she said Munn had done that, and Munn was right about the stone, and left the rest unsaid.

At the turning she stopped. “You will write us down,” she said, not unkindly. “A woman of sixty on three hearths, not on the oath. In nine years a man will read it and think he knows something.”

“I have written nothing that goes north,” he said.

“You will,” she said. “And I am not asking you not to. I am telling you what it will be.”

He went back to his lodging and wrote two lines in his notebook with the hour: the roll holds about sixty households of the four white villages with no oath mark, living and paying and never asked; and the woman on three hearths heard the refusal through the boards and understood none of it and was glad of the silence after. Then he sat a long while with his hands open on the table, thinking of Ash, where to be struck was to be removed from a count, and of this county, where to be unmarked was to be present and unasked, and of how one was a wound and the other a promise, and how neither could be mended by answering.

The minute-man came up in the evening with a request. Would the stranger look at the roll again and say whether the sixty was sixty or nearer seventy, because the slate woman and he differed, and the book deserved an exact reading. Aren went down and counted a third time, leaf by leaf, marking with a bean from the woman’s basket lest his finger slip. Fifty-eight certain, two doubtful where the mark might be a smudge. About sixty, he said, and the minute-man entered about sixty with the hour, because exactness in that book meant admitting doubt.

They drank small beer below the chamber while the rain started again. The minute-man spoke of his twenty-two years keeping facts and never decisions, and how the book had never held a decision about a person until the rider entry. Aren asked whether that troubled him. The minute-man said a book that held only facts was a narrow river, and narrow rivers ran deep, and he would keep it narrow as long as the council let him.

Aren walked home through wet lanes thinking that the not asking had become visible at last, and visibility was the beginning of either mending or breaking, and no one could yet say which.
