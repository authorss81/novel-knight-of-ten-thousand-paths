# Chapter 0125

The day after the naming, Aren walked the Sallow foot with the boy of fourteen and the pole, and did not touch the water. The boy read the post and Aren read after him, and the boy got it a finger out and then got it right, the way Ferrow had taught him. They parted at the weir hut without much talk. The boy had learned to be looked at, and a stranger looking was still a weight.

In the market town Aren found the roll. It lived in a chest in the room under the chamber, in the minute-man’s keeping, on leaves sewn with string. The minute-man let him look because Aren had asked in the right way, for nothing to copy and nothing to carry away, only to understand what the council meant when it spoke of itself.

The roll was a list of households. Against each stood a mark for the oath, or no mark. He ran his finger down the four white villages and counted under his breath, and stopped, and counted again, because the number mattered and he did not want to carry a wrong one.

About sixty households stood on the roll with no mark against them. Alive, on the roll, paying in salt and in labour when labour was asked, and not on the oath, because the oath was renewed by the men who held the water and they did not hold water.

He closed the chest and thanked the minute-man, and went out into the lane with the number sitting in him like a stone.

The woman of about sixty on three hearths was waiting at the corner with a basket of beans. She had told him in a doorway before he had ever been in that chamber, that nobody had ever asked her what she swore, and had told him what would be done with it afterwards, and he had written neither sentence down because he had understood both of them at once. She looked at him now as at a man carrying something heavy.

“You have seen it,” she said.

“I have seen the roll,” he said.

“And nobody in that room has asked us what we swore.”

“No.”

She shifted the basket. “My hearths were in the water last year, four rows under. The men who renew the stone walk past my door to the field. They are good men. They have never asked, because the stone is renewed by men who hold the ditch, and I do not hold a ditch, and there is no form anywhere that says a woman may.”

He wanted to ask her what she would swear if asked, and did not, because asking in a lane would be a promise he could not keep. Instead he asked whether she had been in the chamber for the naming. She said she had stood outside on the stair and heard it through the boards, and had heard his answer and had not understood one word of it, and had been glad he gave no more.

“A refusal with a date in it is still a refusal,” she said. “My grandmother used to say a thing lost can be looked for and a thing refused cannot. We had no word for the second until lately. Now the young barge hand uses it as if he grew it.”

They walked a little way together. She told him the sixty were not a party and not a league and not a grievance, only households that paid and were not asked, and that the not asking had gone on so long it had become a kind of weather. He told her the council had entered the rider charge so it did not fall on them, and she said Munn had done that, and Munn was right about the stone, and left the rest unsaid.

At the turning she stopped. “You will write us down,” she said, not unkindly. “A woman of sixty on three hearths, not on the oath. In nine years a man will read it and think he knows something.”

“I have written nothing that goes north,” he said.

“You will,” she said. “And I am not asking you not to. I am telling you what it will be.”

He went back to his lodging and wrote two lines in his notebook with the hour: the roll holds about sixty households of the four white villages with no oath mark, living and paying and never asked; and the woman on three hearths heard the refusal through the boards and understood none of it and was glad of the silence after. Then he sat with his hands open on the table and did arithmetic that was not sums. The roll had the sixty in it once. His book had the same sixty in it a second time, and the two were now one fact in two places, which is the only shape a fact takes in a county with no office in it, and one of the two places was his and could be contradicted and the other was sewn with string and could not. A number that travels is a person who has stopped being a person. She had told him that in a doorway before he had ever seen the roll, and here was the roll, and here was the number, and here was the doorway.

The minute-man came up in the evening with a request. Would the stranger look at the roll again and say whether the sixty was sixty or nearer seventy, because the slate woman and he differed, and the book deserved an exact reading. Aren went down and counted a third time, leaf by leaf, marking with a bean from the woman’s basket lest his finger slip. Fifty-eight certain, two doubtful where the mark might be a smudge. About sixty, he said, and the minute-man entered about sixty with the hour, because exactness in that book meant admitting doubt.

They drank small beer below the chamber while the rain started again. The minute-man spoke of his twenty-two years keeping facts and never decisions, and how the book had never held a decision about a person until the rider entry. Aren asked whether that troubled him. The minute-man said a book that held only facts was a narrow river, and narrow rivers ran deep, and he would keep it narrow as long as the council let him. Then he said that a narrow river had one fault, which was that it went where the ground let it and not where anybody wanted it, and that a question was not a fact and could not be entered, and that this was a trouble he had never had in twenty-two years because nobody in that chamber had ever asked him to enter one.

Aren said he would go and look at the two doubtful, since his finger had made the doubt and the least he owed the book was to find out what was under the smudge. The minute-man said the second was a house with a door he would know and the first was four fields off the lane and might be a lock-up, and that he had kept off both for nineteen years on the ground that a roll is not a reason to walk into a man's yard.

The first was a house with a dog and a boy of nine who told him the old man was at the ditch and would be back by dark. The old man was sixty-eight and had been on that ground fifty-one years, and he had dug water in the years when the county dug water, and had not dug it in eleven of the last fourteen. He agreed that his mark was not a mark. He said it was a thumb and a wet thumb, in a bad year, and that the year had been a flood year and the book had been written on a table in a barn with the water under the door.

“Then there is a mistake in it,” Aren said. “I will say so.”

“Do not.” The old man was quick about it, and then he was slow, the way a man is when he has found the shape of what he is about to say and is surprised that it is a shape. “I will tell you why, and then you will do as you like with it and I shall not ask you. I have been on that roll fifty-one years and I have never once been asked what I swore, and I swore nothing, and there is no oath on me and there never was. And I have never said that to a living soul, because a man who says it out loud has said it in order to be given something, and the day after he has said it he is a man who wants something, and then it is a matter of whether he gets it. I have wanted nothing off this county since I was nine. That is my whole holding and it is not in the book.”

Aren asked what the man would say if he were asked, in a room, in front of eleven people, and the minute-man standing there with the pen.

“The truth, and it is four words long. I am on the roll. I hold no water. Nobody has ever asked me. That is all of it and it is the truth and it is nothing.” He turned the words over. “And then the next morning there would be a line in the book with my name at the end of it, and the line would be true, and the line would be the reason anybody knew anything about me at all, and in nine years a man would read that line and think he knew a thing about this village that he does not know. I have been nothing on a roll for fifty-one years. Nothing does not get read.”

“So you would rather be asked and stay silent.”

“I would rather be asked and stay silent, and be asked by somebody who has to write down that I stayed silent.” The old man looked at the ditch, which was four fields off and full to the shoulder in the new rain. “Ask us and we will say nothing, and then it will be in a book that we said nothing, and a book that says a man said nothing is a book that says a man agreed. You will have made us agree by asking, and you will have done it with the best intentions in the world, and that is the part I would want written down, if anybody is writing anything down tonight.”

Aren stood in the wet yard a while with the rain coming off the ditch into his boots.

He had come four hundred miles to bring this county a way of being asked, and he had found out in one evening that the way of being asked was the thing that would take the not asking away from them and put it in a book instead. It was not going to be refused. Nobody in that chamber had any intention of refusing anybody anything, and a question put to a household that answered nothing would be answered by the pen.

He did not write it in his notebook. He stood in the yard and made the decision the way a man makes one at a plank end, once, and then it was made: the four true words would go in his own hand or in nobody's, and he would not hand them to a book that had not asked for them, and if he was wrong about that then the county would be the last place in four hundred miles where a man had been careful for nothing.

The walk back took him past the chamber steps, and the minute-man was on them with the lamp unlit, waiting to go in and lock the book. Aren told him he had found the two doubtful, and that one of them was a smudge in a flood year and the other a house with a lock-up door.

The minute-man had his pen out before Aren had finished, which was a thing he had never once done for a figure.

“And what did the two say?” he asked.

“One of them said nothing at all,” Aren said, “which is the answer.”

The minute-man wrote it, and the hour, and closed the book, and it was the first entry that man had made in twenty-two years that was a silence.
