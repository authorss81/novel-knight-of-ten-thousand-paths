# Chapter 0661

**Rainmonth the twentieth, YR 321, a Wednesday and not a market morning, the second hour at the boards in the market street with the room over the weigh-house shut behind its own door, and a copyist of about twenty-six reporting out loud a thing a woman told her on Tuesday who had come two days down a made road on foot to ask it.**

The room over the weigh-house was shut behind its own door, which it is on every day that is not a market morning, and the boards in the market street had the man with the bar going about them, and there were about four carts in the street and a Thursday cart going through with a load of sacking on it and neither the cart nor the load stopped.

Aren Kest had nothing to do. He had been at the boards since about the second hour with his stick in his left hand and his good leg on the stones and about nine lanes' worth of walking still in his legs from a place two days off, and he had been standing there for about half an hour with nothing to do, and it was the first time in about a year.

The errand had been nine gates and not nine hundred, and it was finished on the fourth of Longlight, and since then nobody had sent him anywhere, and he had gone on walking because walking is what he does with a morning and because there was nothing else to put the morning in.

He had thought about that on the road, twice, and had arrived at the same place both times, which is that about four years of this county had taught him one thing and the thing was how to be found, and there is no way of being found that does not also have you standing in a street at the second hour on a Wednesday with your hands empty while about nine people wait for a man with a stick to say something.

He said nothing. He had been saying nothing in this street for about a year and he had got used to the cost of it and had stopped counting, and the count had gone from nine times a day to four, and about four was as low as it went without being a different man.

Then the copyist of about twenty-six came down the lane with a sheet under her arm and ink on the thumb of her right hand, which is new, and about nine people were at the boards by then because the boards is where things happen on a day when there is nothing else.

She said the room was shut and she said why, in about nine words, and then she said the thing she had come down for, and she said it out loud to about nine people in a market street on a Wednesday, which is not where a keeper of a room usually says a thing.

“**I am going to say this once and I would like the nine of you to know that I have never said a thing in this street and that I am saying it because the room is shut and the register is upstairs and there is nowhere else for it to go.**”

Nobody stopped her.

What she had been told was this. On the Tuesday, in the rain, a woman of about thirty-nine who keeps a house in a county two days off this one came into the market town on foot and came up the lane and asked the copyist one question, and the question was whether a Crown officer had the right to ask a person at a gate which place or road a paper meant.

“**And I said that I did not know,**” the copyist said. “**I said it as a person and not as a keeper, and I told her the truth, which is that I have a quarter of a sheet on the boards in this street and that I have read it about nine times and that I cannot tell her the answer to her question out of that, and that there is nobody in nine counties I can put her in front of, and she said she had worked that out in about four days and had come to see whether I would say it to her face.**”

Then the copyist said the part that made the street go quiet, and nine people in it had about nine things to do and five of them did them.

“**She said that the man came to her gate on the Monday at the fourth hour. That he was about thirty-one and he had a small book. That he asked her which place or road was meant, and she said she did not know and had never been told, and he said that was all right and wrote it down. And she said that he read it back to her before he shut the book, and that he asked her whether she wanted to hear it, and she said yes, and that he said that it was very good of her, and that she has not been able to get that out of her head since Monday.**”

Nobody said anything and four people at the boards looked at five other people at the boards and five of them looked at the ground.

Then the badge-man said the true thing, and he said it in about as many words as it takes and he did not soften it and nine people heard it.

“**Then that is what the sheet is for,**” he said. “**And I am going to say the rest of it in this street because I have been saying the first half of it in this street for a month and nobody has made me say the second half and I would rather be the one who says it. A paper that asks nothing gets an answer anyway. That is the whole of what has happened in that county and it has happened in four days and nobody has sent anybody to make it happen.**”

Four people in that market street understood the whole of what he had said and five understood half of it.

“**Say the half that is missing,**” the copyist said, and she said it in the way she asks things, which is that a figure is a figure until somebody makes it an argument.

A man of about fifty-two who was in the street and not at the boards asked it directly, in the ordinary way, and he asked the copyist and not the badge-man.

“**Did he say what county he was from?**”

“**He did not,**” the copyist said. “**She did not ask him either and she said that afterwards, and she has not been able to get at herself about whether that was laziness or sense.**”

“**The second half is that a man with a small book can stand at a gate and ask a question that has no right in it and no wrong answer in it, and a person at a gate will answer him,**” the badge-man said, “**because a gate is a bad place to be asked anything and because a person standing in her own gateway at about the fourth hour in the rain is not going to stand there and argue about a thing she has never been told about. And I have been doing this for about thirty-one years and I will tell you what happens at a gate. They answer. And the answer goes in the book. And in about four years there will be a man in a livery this county has not seen who will read the book out in a room with a table in it, and there will be about nine people in that county who will say that is not what we said, and the book will say it is what they said, and the book will have been written by a man who was not lying.**”

Then he said the part that was the finding of that morning, and he said it to about nine people and not to the copyist.

“**And I am not going to read the three clauses out in this street this morning. A man with a badge read them out in this street on the twelfth of Longlight and about nine people have been carrying them since, and I am not going to hand a woman of about thirty-nine three more clauses in a lane when what she has come to ask is whether she has to answer a question.**”

“**Then what do we do with it,**” the copyist said.

“**Nothing,**” the badge-man said. “**Which is the answer to about half of what gets asked in this street, and it is the correct half, and it is the half nobody here wants, and I would rather give a woman of about thirty-nine a true nothing than a rule that does not reach her by a mile.**”

Then he said the part he had actually come to say, and he said it slowly, and it took him about four seconds, and four people at the boards heard all of it and five heard half of it.

“**I have got nothing, and I have had it since the seventh of Rainmonth, and I would rather a woman of about thirty-nine walked two days in the wet and went home on a true thing than on a rule. A rule in this street is worth nothing to her until somebody in her own county has been asked something, and nobody in her county has been asked anything, and she is the first person from that county up this road in about nine months and she came on her own account and nobody sent her.**”

Nobody thanked him and four people at the boards said nothing and five said something at once and then stopped.

Aren Kest had the finding and had had it for six weeks, and the six weeks were the eighth of Longlight to the second of a month, and he had been carrying it since about the fourth hour on a Wednesday morning at the low end of a woman's road in the dark, and it was three sentences long and one of the three was the only one anybody in nine counties could have argued with, and he had said one true small thing about a ford in the rain on the nineteenth of Rainmonth and had been looked at by about nine people while he said it, and the rest of it had stayed where it was.

He was the man a thing arrives for. That had been true for four years and it had cost him a road once and it had not been offered to him since, and the badge-man had said out loud in this street on the twelfth of Longlight that he was not going to ask him to put his name at the top of a thing, and that in about a month nine people were going to ask whether he had offered it and he would have to say that he had.

About five weeks had gone by since that and nobody had asked him anything, and the reason nobody had asked him anything was not manners and was not the separation and was that every time a room had asked him for something in four years the thing it had wanted was a man to put a name on it, and he had found the true thing in nine seconds each time and had said it and had been given something to hold.

He stood at the high end of the boards and listened to a copyist of about twenty-six say out loud in a market street that a man of about thirty-one had written down what a woman of about thirty-nine said at her own gate and had read it back to her and had said that it was very good of her, and he thought about nine days of a column on four miles of made road and about a false baulm with a stone on the end of it and four marks of lime at the height of a thumb in four stones at the low end of a woman's road, and he thought that a rule about shutting a road cannot be used against a man who never shuts one, and that the man who was going to be difficult in this county was not a man with a paper.

The man who was going to be difficult in this county was a man with a small book and nine words, and he would be at a gate, and he would be polite, and nobody in nine counties had any rule about being asked a question.

Then he asked the copyist one question, and it was the only thing he said in that street, and about four people heard it and about five did not.

“**Did she say where she had been told to go?**”

The copyist looked at him for about a second and a half, which is short for her, and then she gave him the answer in the order she gave answers in.

“**No,**” she said. “**She said that nobody sent her and nobody told her to go and nobody paid her, and that she walked two days in the wet because on the Friday before the sheet went up on the boards in her county a man had asked her husband a question at a gate and had written the answer down, and that she wanted to know whether that was what the sheet was for, and that she worked out on the Tuesday of last week, on her own, with nobody, that the answer was probably that it was, and that she would rather be told than go home on probably.**”

Nobody thanked her for that either.

So a woman of about thirty-nine had walked two days in the wet on her own account, and had not been sent, and about nine people in nine counties had spent a year building a rule about a thing that nobody is going to do, and the first thing that has happened in the rule's direction in six weeks is a person on foot.

About four people in that market street understood the whole of that and about five understood that a woman had walked somewhere.

Then the badge-man said the last of it, and he said it to nobody in particular and he said it at about the second hour of a Wednesday in a market street that was not a market, and there was nobody in that street who had asked him for it.

“**The delegation asked me on the first of Longlight whether we were to be an army, and I told her the truth, and the truth was that nobody had asked her anything and that the Crown had not asked her anything either, and I have been standing in this street since and I have thought about that answer about nine times a day and it is the truest thing I have said in about a year and it is not an answer.**”

The copyist said nothing and about nine people waited, and he went on.

“**It is not an answer because it is a true thing about what has not happened, and a woman who has come eleven days on a cart cannot go home and say to about nine hundred people that nothing has happened. She can say that, and she will say it, and in about four months somebody will ask her what happens when something does, and she will not have an answer and she will have said in this county that she did not have one.**”

“**And the second answer came on the twentieth of Longlight,**” he said, “**off a crossing in a city that was never severed, and it was no. We will not come. We will not be moved. Do not ask us again this year. And it is a good answer and it is the plainest thing anybody in nine counties has had for a year and it is not hers to carry and it is not about her. It is about them. And what she came eleven days to ask was about her, and whether about nine hundred people on a coast are to be asked for their aid by a piece of paper that has not yet asked them for anything.**”

Nine people in that market street had heard both answers and about four of them could have said out loud, at that moment, in about nine words, what the question was, and nobody did, and the copyist of about twenty-six had ink on the thumb of her right hand and did not look at it.

Then the badge-man said that the copyist should write the woman's question down on her own sheet with no head over it, in her own hand, and that she should not put it in the register, and that he was telling her so in a street so that about nine people would know that a question in that county can arrive on a person's own paper and go nowhere.

“**And I would like the nine of you to think about one thing between now and the next time any of you is in this street,**” he said. “**There is going to be a person in this county inside about four days from a county that has read a sheet out loud in a market, and he will be asked whether the nine of us have read ours, and about nine of us will have read ours, and four of us will not have understood it, and one of us will be asked a question at a gate about a thing we have never been asked about, and I would like somebody in this street to have thought about what a county says at a gate.**”

Nobody said anything and four people went away and five stayed, and the man with the bar came about the boards at the third hour and did not stop, and the cart with the sacking on it had gone by.

**And the cost of that Wednesday is a column and it is nobody's, and a woman of about thirty-nine who walked two days in the wet on her own account and a man of about thirty-one with a small book who said that it was very good of her at a gate in a county two days off this one are not in it and neither of them has a price.**

**a tally stick at 2, a cake of soap at 6, a sheet of oiled paper at 15, a boot brush at 16, a purse string at 17, a horn button at 18, a skin of glue at 21, a cooper's hoop at 25, a ream of blotting paper at 37, and a side of leather at 39.** Two, six, fifteen, sixteen, seventeen, eighteen, twenty-one, twenty-five, thirty-seven and thirty-nine is one hundred and ninety-six pence. **A mark is forty-eight pence and one hundred and ninety-six pence is four marks and four over.** The copyist went up the thirteen stairs with the register under her arm and did not open it, and put the woman's question on a leaf of her own with no head over it, and the leaf went into the drawer about eleven inches deep with about four hundred returns in it and nine other leaves, and the drawer is not far enough for the drawer and never has been, and nobody in nine counties has ever taken it out and counted what was in it, and nobody in nine counties is going to.

Then Aren Kest walked down the lane to the gate and stood at the low end of the boards where the four feet of a market morning's stone was lying open and grey, and a woman of about thirty-eight had been in this street on the first of a month and had asked him which of two things to be ready for, and he had said that he would not tell her, and it was the fourth time in his life he had been asked what he wanted and the first time he had got the shape of the answer out before the person asking had finished the question.

He had got a sixth one on that day and he had not noticed it, and it was a woman of about thirty-nine in a county two days off, and she had asked nothing at all, and that was the difference between the two questions, and he stood at a gate in the rain for about as long as it takes a carter to get a load round working out that he had been asked a question by somebody in a county he had never walked in, about whether anybody in nine counties had a rule for a man who was being polite.
