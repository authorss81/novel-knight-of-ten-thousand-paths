# Chapter 0666

**Harvestmonth the fourteenth, YR 321, a Saturday and a market morning, the fourth hour at a gate on a made road in the second county, two days off the market town, about nine people standing in a lane who do not know each other, and a man of about thirty-one with a stick of chalk in his hand putting a board on the inside of somebody's gate in about four minutes.**

There was a thing the badge-man was not talking about that morning, and four people at that gate knew what it was and five did not. The reason he was not talking about it was that he had said out loud in a market street on the first of Harvestmonth that he was not going to ask anybody anything for a month, and thirteen days of that month had gone by, and he was keeping to it in a way that was costing him the whole of his standing in his own opinion and nothing whatever in anybody else's.

The party had come down the made road on the Friday evening and had slept in a barn two miles on, where the road is still made, and had come on again at the fourth hour because there was nowhere to sit. The reason they had come was the copyist of about twenty-six, and the reason had been given to her out loud in a lane at the market town's own gate on the second of Fallowmonth, on the made road, by a woman of about fifty-six with a bag on the floor at her feet who had been on her way home and had stopped, and the copyist had written nothing down and had not asked the woman to repeat it.

What the woman of about fifty-six had said to her, at a gate, in about nine words, was that she had asked four people in this county where a county keeps a paper and that all four had given her a different answer and that she was going home with a bag of nine pages she could not put down. The copyist had said out loud that she would find out, and the woman of about fifty-six had said that nobody had asked her to, and the copyist had come down the made road on the Friday, and the badge-man had come with her because he had a badge and no head and nine pence a week and there was nothing in this county for him to be at and a man in a livery was writing on gates, and a man of about thirty-four with a sheet in his coat had come because he goes where the road is.

The woman of about thirty-six had come because she had been in a cart for three weeks and had not slept properly since, and wanted to be in a lane with a bar in it.

Aren Kest had come because he was thirty-two and had a stick and there was a gate.

The man at the gate was a man of about thirty-one with a livery this county had not seen and a small book in his coat and about nine feet of deal in his other hand, and a woman of about fifty-eight who kept that gate came out into the lane, stood in front of it, and asked him, before he had said a word, whether he was going to shut it.

“**No,**” he said. “**I am going to write something on a board and peg it inside your gate and peg it on the inside so that you can take it down whenever you like, and I am going to write down the day and this gate and my name and the fact that I asked you a question and what you said to me, and that is all there is.**”

That was nine words longer than she needed and she took it.

He did it in four minutes, which was fast, and he did it correctly, and the correctness was the thing four people in that lane noticed and five did not. She watched all four minutes of it without moving and without asking him anything, and when he was finished she said that her husband had held that gate for about thirty-one years and that nobody had ever put anything on it, and that there was a nail in the top rail about a hand's breadth from the hinge that was not holding anything and had not held anything since before she came.

He measured nothing and brought nothing to a level. He took the small book out, found a page, and wrote a line on it, and then took the deal and a stick of chalk and wrote on the board in thirty words, and he wrote it without stopping and without going back, and the writing was upright and even and the letters were the same size as each other, and at the top of it he put the day, because a person coming back in nine years needs the day first.

Then he burned the ends of the chalk on the board, which is a thing about thirty-one year old men do to keep the weather off, and he read it out loud.

It said the county, and then the gate, which he described by the long field on the high side and the lower pasture and the two thorn trees, because a gate has no name anybody uses and the description is the name. Then the day, the fourteenth of Harvestmonth, and then his own name in full, and then one line, which was that he asked at that gate which place or road was meant, and that the person at the gate said that she had not been told, and that he had said that it was very good of her, and that the answer so given is entered here.

He read all of that out loud in a lane in about thirty seconds and about nine people heard it and four of them understood all of it and five of them understood that a man in a livery had written something on a board.

Then the man of about thirty-four with a sheet in his coat, who had come down with them, said the obvious thing.

“**You are not the man who marked the ford.**”

“**No,**” the man of about thirty-one said. “**I have not been to the ford. He is at the third marker in the waterworks country and I have not seen him since Thawmonth. We do the same work and we are taught it the same way and I do not mind being asked.**”

“**I am not accusing you of anything,**” the man of about thirty-four said, and then stopped, and put his hand into his coat and about four people in that lane watched him do it, because a hand going into a coat in this county has meant one thing for four and a half years.

He took out a sheet and a stub of pencil, and he held them, and he did not write.

“**Say the line,**” the badge-man said to him, from the low side of the lane, in the voice a man of fifty-eight uses when he has said a thing twice already and does not intend to say it a third time.

“**Which line?**”

“**The one you have been carrying since Friday.**”

The man of about thirty-four said that he was not going to write it down, and he said why, and he said it in nine words, and four people in that lane understood all of it and five did not.

“**If I write it down then there are two of them. If there are two of them then in about nine years there is a piece of paper in a coat and a board on a gate and about nine people who were standing here, and the one that is easier to get at is the one on the gate, and in about four years the one in the coat will be in a drawer about eleven inches deep in a town two days off and the one on the gate will still be on the gate.**”

Nobody thanked him for that and the copyist of about twenty-six said the true thing about it out loud, which is that he was right, and that she had thought about it for about two days and had got to the same place and had come down the made road on the Friday to see whether a county could hold a paper.

Then the badge-man did not say the thing.

About four people in that lane were waiting for him to say it, and about four of them were waiting for it because they had spent a year in a market street listening to a man of about fifty-eight say every true thing out loud as it came into his head, and the true thing was in the lane and it was nine words long, and it was the thing all four of them had been carrying since the eighth of Longlight, and the thing nine people in nine counties were carrying, and four seconds went by.

The badge-man took the badge out and turned it over in his fingers the way a man turns a thing he has carried too long, and he put it back inside his coat, and he said:

“**I have said it about nine times in a market street and I am not going to say it in a lane, and I would like the four of you who were waiting to notice that a man of fifty-eight stood in a lane and did not say a thing, and that I have not done that since Thawmonth of last year, and that I have not got a good reason and I am not going to make one up in front of these people.**”

Four people in that lane understood what had just happened and five did not, and four of the five who did not understand it understood that the man with the badge had not said anything, which is a different thing and is most of it.

The woman of about thirty-six said what the board was, out loud, in the lane, to about nine people, and she said it as the person who has said hard true things in market streets nine times and has never once been asked for anything.

“**It is not a record of a road,**” she said. “**It is a record of a gate. And a gate with a date on it is a thing that a man can come back to in about nine years and read, and he will not have been here, and nobody will be able to get at it afterwards to find out what was said, and that is the whole of the arrangement we have got and I have said it in four counties and I have never once said it about a board with my name on it at the top of it.**”

“**It is not your name at the top of it,**” the copyist of about twenty-six said. “**It is his. That is what I came down here to find out and I have found out in about a minute and a half at a gate and I would like the nine of you to understand that what I came two days for is a thing that has been happening in this county for about four weeks and that nobody in the market town knew, and I did not know, and I keep a book.**”

“**What is the thing, then?**” the woman of about fifty-eight at the gate said, which is a question that nine people in this county have not asked this month and which she asked in the ordinary way.

“**You want a county where a thing is written down and there is nobody to keep it,**” the copyist said, “**and a county is not a place where a person keeps a thing. A place where a person keeps a thing is a room with a door on it, and there is one of those in a town two days off this one, and it has been open with no date anybody will give for about four and a half years, and the woman who keeps it has said out loud that she is not the person that things like that end up with.**”

Nobody thanked her and the woman of about thirty-six said the badge-man was not going to say it, and that he had said so in a lane, and that she was going to say it instead because she was not the man with a badge and did not have thirty-one years of anybody expecting him to. **And then she did not say the hole, and about four people in that lane noticed that she did not say it, and about five noticed nothing at all.**

“**You heard what I said on the first of this month,**” she said, “**and you know what I am going to say and so do I and I am not going to say it in a lane to about nine people who keep no road. I have said it in about nine streets and four counties and I am not going to say it twice in nine days to the same nine people, and if you want it said here then somebody will have to say to me why here, and nobody has, and I have been waiting since the first of the month.**”

About four people at that gate understood that the man with the badge had been offered a sentence out loud and had not taken it and had been told why, and about five understood that a woman had declined to say something.

The man of about thirty-one was patient through all of it. He had put the board on and read it out and he had not said one word about any of it, and when the woman of about thirty-six finished, he said one thing to her and he said it in the ordinary way, and about nine people in that lane heard it.

“**I was told to enter what was said and not what was meant,**” he said, “**and I have entered what was said. If I had entered what was meant then it would have been a thing I had made up, and I have been taught the difference about nine times and I have got it right about nine times out of nine, and I would like somebody in this lane to tell me that a book with what was meant in it is a different book, because I have been in this for about four weeks and nobody has told me and I would rather be told by a woman who says things in market streets than find out in four years.**”

Nobody said anything and four people at that gate looked at the woman of about thirty-six and five looked at the board.

Aren Kest had been standing about nine paces off with his stick in his left hand for the whole of it, and he had said nothing, and the reason he had said nothing was not the separation and was not that about nine people were waiting for him. It was that he had walked four miles in the dark on the twentieth of Longlight to look at four marks of lime at the height of a thumb at a ford, and had understood in about nine seconds that they were where the water stops and that they are put there to be read in four years by another man, and that a Crown officer who wants to shut a ford on a particular day needs four marks and a book and not a column.

He had carried that for about seven weeks as a discovery. It was not a discovery. It was a craft, and it was taught, and about nine men in nine counties were at that moment doing it at about nine gates, and the man at this gate had said so out loud in a lane and had not minded being asked and had got two figures in it right out of two, and there was no version of that in which a county could say no to it, because a county could say no to a man and not to a craft.

That was the whole of what he had learned between the eighth of Longlight and the fourteenth of Harvestmonth, and it took him about nine seconds, and nine people in a lane did not see it happen, and four of them saw a man with a stick put his weight off his right leg and take the stick in his right hand instead, which takes longer than it should.

Then he asked the man of about thirty-one one question, and it was the only thing he said in that lane, and the man answered it in four seconds and correctly.

“**How many gates?**”

“**I have a page for each one and I have been to nine gates in this county in four weeks, and there are forty-one gates on this made road, and I will have them all by the middle of Frostmonth,**” the man said, and then he said the other half of it, unasked, in the way of a man who has been doing a thing for about nine years and has never had anybody ask him a question about it. “**And that is not mine. There will be another man in another county with a page for each gate and he will not know about this page, and the two pages will not be the same size, because I write about forty words to a gate and he writes about eleven, and four years from now a man will be asked to compare them.**”

Nobody thanked him and four people at that gate understood the whole of what he had said and five understood that a man in a livery had a book with a page for every gate.

**And the cost of that Saturday is a column, and it is nobody's, and forty-one gates on a made road and forty words to a gate and nine gates in four weeks are not in it and none of them has a price.**

**a pound of chalk at 10, a billhook at 23, a padlock at 24, a bundle of pegs at 25, a hoof pick at 27, a horse collar at 28, a lantern at 31, a wicket latch at 32, a mail bag at 36, and a tally board at 40.** Ten, twenty-three, twenty-four, twenty-five, twenty-seven, twenty-eight, thirty-one, thirty-two, thirty-six and forty is two hundred and seventy-six pence. **A mark is forty-eight pence and two hundred and seventy-six pence is five marks and thirty-six over.** The board went on the inside of the gate about a hand's breadth below the top rail, where it is under the drip line and out of the way of a bar, and the man of about thirty-one showed the woman of about fifty-eight how to lift it off its two pegs, and she lifted it off and put it back twice, and nine people in that lane watched her do it, and four of them understood that a board on a gate is held on by two pegs and five did not.

Then the copyist of about twenty-six put her hand flat on the gate and said one sentence to about nine people, and it was nine words, and four of them heard it and five heard a woman say something at a gate.

“**A county is not a place a paper is kept,**” she said, and “**I have come two days to find that out and I would have found it out in a market in nine seconds if I had asked anybody.**”
