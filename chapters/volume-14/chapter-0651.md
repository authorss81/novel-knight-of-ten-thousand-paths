# Chapter 0651

**Greenmonth the twenty-sixth, YR 321, a Friday and not a market morning, the second hour in the market street and then four lanes, a man with a stick saying a sentence out loud at four gates, and nine people at the fourth gate who did not come for the sentence.**

The room over the weigh-house was shut behind its own door, which it is on every day that is not a market morning, and the boards in the market street had a piece of chalk on them because the man who swept them had swept them on the Saturday before and had come back on the Saturday after and had decided that a market street did not need sweeping twice in four days.

Aren Kest was in the street at about the second hour with his stick in his left hand and his right leg on the boards and the weight off it, and he had nine lanes to do and had done three.

The errand was two days old and had no name at the top of it. That had been deliberate, and the man who had given it had said so in a room over a saddler's in about nine words, and the reason he had given it in that form was that a man with a badge and thirty-one years behind him had spent the whole of a year finding out what happens to a thing that has a name at the top of it.

Nobody thanked him for that, and nobody thanked the man carrying the errand either, and the errand was this. Say the words at a gate, in the open, to whoever opens it. Say that the hearing stands on the first of every month. Say that it has no bench in it. Say that it is a hearing that cannot say a sheet is false. Say it nine times and not nine hundred, and if somebody asks what it is for, say that it is a place where a question can be asked out loud in front of about nine people and that nobody has to answer it.

That was the whole of it, and it took him about forty seconds at a gate, and he had been at it for two days.

He thought about the forty seconds more than he thought about the rest of it, which was a gate, a dog, a lane, a woman with a bar of iron who did not open the gate and did not shut it either and stood in the gap while he said it. The dog was the only one of the three that barked. The woman listened with her head on one side the way people do when they have decided in advance not to agree with anything, and he had said it to her the same as he had said it to the dog, and he had said it to the dog first, and neither of them took it well.

The first lane was the worst lane in the town to stand in, because the wall on the far side took the sound and gave it back about two seconds late, and by the time his own words came back to him he had finished them and was standing in a lane that appeared to be repeating itself.

The second gate belonged to a man of about sixty who asked him what the hearing was for before he had got to the end of it, and Aren Kest gave him the answer he had brought, and the man said that was a thing you say to a man who is asking so that he stops asking, and Aren Kest said it was, and the man said right, and shut the gate, and it was not clear whether he had been right about the man or wrong about the dog.

The third gate was a gate to a yard with nothing in it and he said the words into the gap and a girl of about nine who was sitting on the wall with her boots off heard all of it and asked him afterwards whether the hearing had a roof, and he said it did not, and she said that was better, and he did not know what she meant and thought about it for two lanes afterwards.

Nobody has to answer it. That was the part he kept coming back to, and it was not modesty and it was not a story he told about himself. It was the only part of the errand he believed, and he believed it because of about nine months of a room over a weigh-house that had stood open with no date anybody would give and had a register in it with nine questions in it and nothing under any of them, and because of a room in Thawmonth, where a woman had stood up in front of about nine people and said in four sentences that a name in a room is a head at the top of a thing.

He was the man who could be sent for. Nobody had made him anything and the badge-man had said in a room with the door open that he was not being given a post by a man with a badge and would stand in a street and say so if anybody said otherwise. But there is a difference between having a post and being the person a thing arrives for, and the difference is that a post can be declined and this cannot, and about four hundred people in that town had been waiting eleven months for him to be given something with a name at the top of it so that they could hand it back to him.

He had not asked for it. He had also not refused it, because there was nothing on the table to refuse, and he had noticed that about four days ago and had not been able to do anything about it since.

The fourth lane was not made for a mile and a half out of the town and then was, and at the place where it stopped being made there was a gate and a cart standing in the mud at the low side of it with its off-side wheel off and lying about four paces away in the wet, and the hub was out of the cart and lying in the same place, and about nine people were standing about it not helping.

The woman of about thirty-six who keeps a road in the county where a list of names was burned was one of the nine and had come out about four days on a different errand and was in no mood. The copyist of about twenty-six was another and had a sheet on a board under her arm and no ink on her hands, which meant she had come to say a sentence at a gate and not to write anything down. A carter in a wet coat was holding a bar of oak and had been holding it for some time.

The wheel had come off on the last rise before the gate, which is the one place on that lane where the made road gives, and the cart had not gone over because the carter had a bar and about nine years of doing this. The hub was out because somebody had put it out to see. That was the carter of about fifty, and Aren Kest had seen him work at a sill in the market town in Mudmonth and knew that he had put the hub out to see because that was how he looked at a wheel, and not because he thought it was broken.

Aren Kest got down off his high side, which took him about four seconds longer than getting down off a low one, and put his stick against the gatepost, and went and knelt in the wet and put his hand into the nave of the hub and turned it, and the carter of about fifty watched him do it and did not say anything.

That is what nobody outside this county understands a wheel. It is not broken. It has been through something and it is telling you about it in the only way a thing that has been through something can, by being heavy in one place and light in another, and a man who puts his hand in it and turns it reads the road the wheel has just been on and does not need to be told.

“**You are not going to fix it,**” the carter said, after a minute and a half.

“**I am going to tell you what happened to it,**” Aren Kest said. “**It came off the cart on the rise and it went down on the mud side and there is stone in the nave on the off side and none on the near side, and it came off because the off side of that cart is heavier than the off side of the road, and that is not the wheel's fault and it is not the cart's fault either, and if you put that hub back in and grease it you will have it off again in about nine miles and you will have it off again at the same rise, and the second time it will be at the gate instead of at the rise, and there will be a gate open behind you when it happens.**”

Nobody thanked him for it and about four of the nine people said something at once and the carter of about fifty put the bar down in the mud and said that he had a new hub in the cart and that the new hub was for the other wheel.

That is the other half of what nobody outside this county understands a wheel, which is that the far side of a cart goes first and has always gone first and there is no arguing with a wheel about fairness.

Then he put the hub back in, and two of the nine got the wheel up, and Aren Kest stood back on his good side and watched, and the copyist of about twenty-six was looking at the cart and not at the wheel.

The cart was loaded with about nine hundred pounds of nothing that anybody could name from where she stood, which is to say sacks, a chest, two bales, a basket, a bundle of something long under a tarpaulin, and a quantity of people.

There were three people sitting on the load. A woman of about thirty-eight with her back against the chest and a hood down and her hands in her sleeves, who had been in that cart for about eleven days by the look of her and who had the particular stillness of a person who has been told not to move. A woman of about sixty on the far side with her feet up on the bale, who was asleep and had been asleep for most of the last mile, and who had one boot on and one boot off and the boot that was off was on her lap. And a boy of about nine between the two of them with his arms round a bundle the shape of a person's arm and a leg.

“**Where are they from?**” the carter said, to nobody, in the way a carter says a thing.

“**The coast road,**” the copyist said. “**They came in on the Friday. There is nobody on this lane that keeps that road and there is not supposed to be anybody on this lane past the gate.**”

“**I came off the coast road,**” the woman of about thirty-eight said. Her voice was not hoarse and it was not loud and she said it to the carter and then she said it again to the nine people, because she wanted it to be a fact in the lane and not a remark in a lane. “**I came off the coast road and it is eleven days and there is a ford on it that is wet in Longlight and it took us two days at that ford, and there is a woman of about sixty with us who has not been out of a cart in nine years except to do what she had to do, and there is a boy. I am not asking you for anything. I am saying where we are from, because it is about to be somebody else's business and I would rather it was ours first.**”

The boy of about nine looked up at that and then looked at Aren Kest, and Aren Kest looked at the road and not at him.

Then the copyist asked the carter, in the ordinary way a person asks a thing in a lane, whose sheet the cart was on.

“**There is no sheet,**” the carter said. “**They paid for the loading and they paid for the ford and they paid for nine days and they have not asked anybody for anything and there is nowhere to put a name.**”

“**There is a name,**” the copyist of about twenty-six said. She said it flatly, the way she said figures, and she did not ask anybody's permission and she did not look at anybody while she said it. “**The name is Mara Vey. She keeps the room over the weigh-house and the room is shut behind its own door this morning because it is a Friday and it is not a market morning, and she is the person a return goes to and a sheet is put in front of, and that is what she is for.**”

About nine people heard a name in a lane at about the third hour on a Friday morning, and four of them did not know it and five of them had heard it before and did not use it again.

Aren Kest was two paces away with his stick in his hand and he did not say anything, and the reason he did not say anything was not manners and was not the separation and was that a name in a room is a head at the top of a thing and she had said so in a room on the twenty-fifth of Greenmonth in four sentences, and about nine people had heard her say it, and he was not going to be the one who found out whether a name also works in a lane before anybody had tested it.

What he thought, standing in the mud at the low side of a gate with about nine people in it, was that a carter asking whose sheet a cart was on is the whole of what a name does, and that it had taken her two years to find that out and about nine months to say it.

Then the carter said the thing that made the morning turn over.

“**If there is a name,**” he said, “**then there is somebody who keeps it, and can I ask her what happens to us at the gate? Because I have been held at a gate on this road twice since I was a boy and I do not know whether that is this gate or a different one and I would rather know now than on the road.**”

Nobody answered him, and Aren Kest said the standing notice at the fourth gate, which he had not done yet, because there were nine people and it was a gate and the man who had given him the errand had said to whoever opens it and the carter had his hand on the gate.

He said it in forty seconds and it took ninety, because he was in the mud and had to turn round twice to include the nine people who were behind him at the wheel.

“**The hearing stands in the street on the first of every month. It has no bench in it. It is a hearing that cannot say a sheet is false. It is a place where a question can be asked out loud in front of about nine people, and nobody has to answer it.**”

The carter took his hand off the gate and wiped it on his coat and said that he would come on a market morning, and that was the end of it, and it was not agreed with and it was not argued with.

The copyist went back down the lane with her sheet still under her arm and still with no ink on her hands.

Nobody thanked anybody.

Then the woman of about thirty-eight got down off the load, which took her a while, and stood at the low side of the gate with her hands still in her sleeves, and said one more thing, and said it to Aren Kest and not to the nine people, and said it as though she had decided which of the two she wanted it said to.

“**You are the man who has a road to somebody,**” she said. “**I have known that for about four days and I have not said it to anybody. I have come eleven days to ask one question and it is not about the gate. If somebody comes up that road with a paper, are we to fight, or are we to stand there and let them take it? I am not asking you to promise me anything. I am asking you to tell me which of the two I should be ready for, so that I can go back and tell them.**”

Aren Kest stood in the mud with his stick in his left hand and had four more lanes to do and about two hours of light and about nine things in his head and none of them an answer to that.

He said, what he said, and he said it because she had come eleven days and because the boy of about nine was looking at him again, and it is put down here in his own words because it is the fourth time in his life he has been asked what he wants and it is the first time he has got the shape of the answer out before the person asking him had finished the question.

He said that he did not know, and that he would not tell her which of the two to be ready for, and that the reason he would not was that a person who is told which of two things to be ready for will be ready for it, and that being ready for the wrong one is the same as being caught by the right one, and that the only thing he could tell her honestly was that nobody had asked her anything at all yet and that she had come eleven days on the strength of a question she had made up herself, and that if a paper did come up that road it would come up with nobody having thought about what it would be met with, and that that was not a comfort and he was not going to give it to her as one.

She said that was the answer to a different question and she said it once, without any temper in it at all, and got back on the cart.

**And the cost of that Friday is a column and it is nobody's, and nine lanes and four of them done and a name said out loud in a lane by a person who keeps a room and did not ask anybody's permission and a woman who came eleven days on a cart with a boot off and a boy with a person's arm under a tarpaulin are not in it and none of them has a price.**

**a wicker basket at 3, a sheaf of withies at 4, a rope of chalk line at 6, a whetstone at 13, a hank of yarn at 21, a hoop of iron at 26, a bundle of laths at 32, a sheet of oiled paper at 35, a water can at 39, and a hanglock at 41.** Three, four, six, thirteen, twenty-one, twenty-six, thirty-two, thirty-five, thirty-nine, and forty-one is two hundred and twenty pence. **A mark is forty-eight pence and two hundred and twenty pence is four marks and twenty-eight over.** The wheel went back on the cart at about the fifth hour with grease out of the woman's basket and not out of anybody's pocket, and the cart went up through the gate and stopped on the rise about nine minutes later, and stood there, and the carter got off it and looked at the off side of it for a while and then got back on it and went on up the lane, and about nine people who had watched him do that said nothing to each other about it afterwards, and about five of them understood the whole of what the morning had been for and the other four did not, and that was the correct proportion and nobody in the lane had arranged it.

Then the woman of about thirty-eight got off the cart again on the far side of the rise, and stood in the road while the cart went on without her, because she had one boot on and one boot off and she was not going up the lane in front of nine people in that state, and the woman of about sixty woke up and put her boot on without being told and got off on the other side and stood beside her, and the boy of about nine was already off the load and standing in the middle of the road with his arms still round the bundle and did not say where he was going to sleep.

Nobody said anything about that either. It was about the fourth hour and there were about nine people standing at a gate in Longlight light with the water coming off four miles of made road somewhere behind them, and about four of them went back into the town and about five did not, and the man with the stick and the road-marked left palm went on to the fifth lane with the sun coming up on the side of it that he could not see.
