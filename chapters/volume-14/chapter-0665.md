# Chapter 0665

**Harvestmonth the seventh, YR 321, a Saturday and a market morning, the sixth hour on a mile and a half of lane that stops being made, about nine people and a boy at a drain in white frost, a Crown crew at work on the made road a quarter of a mile above them, and a barrow that goes up the lane at about the ninth hour with a man of about twenty-two on it.**

The lane runs about a mile from the made road and then it is a lane, and the last half mile of it is not made at all, and nine people keep it because there is nobody else to keep it and because the man of about fifty who laid three courses of sill in lime at the town's own gate in Greenmonth has said out loud more than once that a road is not kept by anybody being paid for it, it is kept by the households at the two ends.

At the point where the lane takes the water off the made road above it there is a cut about four feet wide and about three feet deep running across, and under the lane there is a barrel of lime-washed brick about four feet long under a stone lintel, and three feet of silt and weed had gone into it in six weeks, and four inches of that was a lime crust gone hard, and it had to come off in pieces.

The water that goes through that barrel is the water off about four miles of made road, and when the barrel is choked it does not run, and when it does not run it comes over the top of the cut at the low side, and it has been coming over the top of the cut at the low side since the middle of Rainmonth, and there is a channel nine inches deep and nine feet long in the lane where it goes, and a man of about fifty-one who owns the field on the low side of it has been putting a board across the bottom of that channel since the middle of Rainmonth and lifting it every morning, and this was the seventh morning.

He was not asked to do that. About nine people knew about it and nobody in this county had arranged it.

That is the whole of the work on that lane on that morning, and it took nine people and a boy of about thirteen and most of a day, and it was not in anybody's order and it was not a contract and it was not the market town's business, and it was the fourth time in about four years that the people at the two ends of that mile and a half had turned out for something nobody had ordered.

The frost was about an inch in the ditch and the ground on the high side of the cut was hard as a table and the ground on the low side was soft, and that was the whole difficulty, and four people understood it in nine seconds and five did not, which is the correct proportion and nobody in that lane had arranged it.

“**It will not come off the top,**” the woman of about thirty-eight said to the boy of about thirteen, and she was talking about the lime crust. “**Get under the low end and come up. If you break it in the middle there will be three days of it in a drain and I will not be here on the third day.**”

“**I will bring the short bar,**” the boy said.

“**Bring the long one,**” she said. “**And do not carry it on your shoulder, because I have watched a boy carry a bar on a shoulder over a drain and that is how the fourth finger goes.**”

“**It is my own bar,**” the boy said.

“**Then your own fourth finger,**” she said, and he took the long one.

A man of about thirty-eight at the top end of the lane put his head over the cut and said, out loud, to nobody in particular, that if the crust came off in one piece they could have two feet of it out before noon, and the man of about fifty-one said that they would not, and the man of about thirty-eight said that he had been at this nine years and would know, and the man of about fifty-one said that he had been at it nineteen and that in nineteen years it had never once come off in one piece and that the crust breaks along the lime and not along the joint, and the man of about thirty-eight said that that was one thing said two ways and went away, and four people in the cut thought he was right and five did not.

Aren Kest came down the lane at about the fifth hour with his stick and got into the cut at the low end and worked with a shovel for the first hour before anybody asked him who he was, which is what happens on a lane, and the man of about fifty-one did not ask him and the boy did not ask him and a woman of about thirty-eight with a bar did not ask him, and four people at the two ends of the lane knew who he was and five did not, and it made no difference to the work at all.

Then the cart of the man of about fifty-one went over.

The near wheel had come off on the rise at the top of the field, and the ground at the top of that field has been giving for about nine years and everybody who uses it knows so, and there is a cart's off-side wheel lying in four days of mud on a mile and a half of made road four days in the other direction with a new hub in the cart that is for the other wheel, and this is not that wheel and this is not that cart, and nobody in that lane has ever seen the other one.

The hub was out and lying in the channel in nine inches of water and the cart had gone over onto its side and the load of about nine hundred pounds had gone with it, and the load was about four hundred and a half pounds of stone and nine sacks, and the man of about fifty-one was standing in the channel with the water up to his knees and had his hands on the axle and was not calling for anything.

He did not want to be helped. That was visible from about nine paces and four people in that lane could see it and about five could not, and the way of it was that he was a man of about fifty-one who has raised a cart on his own about four times a year for about nine years and does it in about twenty minutes without saying anything to anybody, and there were ten other people within calling distance on a Saturday morning and every one of them knew where he did not want them.

The woman of about thirty-eight with the bar said one thing, which was the name of the hub and the direction of the wall, and she said it once and did not say it twice, and he said no.

That is what not wanting to be helped looks like at the low end of a lane in Harvestmonth. It does not look like refusing. It looks like doing.

He got the load off in nineteen minutes by himself, sack by sack, a third of a load at a time, and the sacks went up on the wall and the stone went up in pieces he could lift, and at the eighth minute he had a barrow to put them in, and the barrow was on the high side of the lane and belonged to nine households, and he got it and did not ask.

He got it in the fourth minute, having been under the cart twice, and he was helped at the eleventh minute, and the help was not asked for and did not arrive as an offer. A boy of about thirteen came down the low side of the cut with a sack on his shoulder that he had not been told to bring, and put it on the wall, and went back up. A woman of about thirty-eight put her bar under the axle and lifted and did not count, and the man of about fifty-one did not stop her doing it and did not look at her while she did it, which is the whole of what consent looks like between two people who have done this before.

Then it was up, and the wheel was on, and he got his cart out of the channel and onto the lane, and he stood in nine inches of water with his hands shaking, and he said, to nobody, that the ground at the top of that field was going and had been going for about nine years, and nobody said that they had said so before.

Nobody thanked him and nobody thanked the boy of about thirteen.

A Crown crew was working on the made road a quarter of a mile above them, and it had been there four days, and it was a bridge crew and not the column, and it was about nine men with a waggon and a good deal of timber, and the yard that held the crews in this county had taken a contract from the advance party and was working ahead of everybody, which is a thing a captain says out loud at a board because he has said he will answer any person being asked.

Aren Kest had known that the yard would be on that road and had walked down the lane at about the fifth hour anyway, and he had not gone up to it, and at about the seventh hour he came out of the cut and went up to the made road with his stick and stood at the tail of a waggon for about two minutes and then went back down.

Tovan Kest was about four hundred yards up the made road with a length of board and a bar and about four men and a fire that had gone out an hour ago, and he looked at his boots and then at the cut below and worked out the rest of it, and neither of them said anything about the cart.

What Tovan Kest said was this, and he said it while he was driving a wedge and did not stop driving it.

“**I asked my own yard for a barrow twice this week,**” he said. “**And I paid for both, and I paid the man who brought it and not the man who owns it, because that is the way it is done and it has been done in four counties for about nine years, and if you take the second part out of it then the first part does not work.**”

“**The second part is not his,**” Aren Kest said.

“**The second part is not his and the second part is the only reason anybody brings a barrow back,**” Tovan Kest said, and drove the wedge, and the timber came down about an inch and the fire wanted relighting. “**That is not an opinion. That is nine years of a yard. You can have an honest county and you can have a lane that gets worked and you do not get both out of one of them, and I have never met the man who has worked that out with a barrow in his hand.**”

Nobody said anything to that and four men of that crew heard it and five did not, and two of the four who heard it looked at the two of them and then at the cut below and worked out what was going on a quarter of a mile away on a lane.

Then the thing that the whole of that morning turned on happened, and it took nine seconds, and it was not said by anybody in authority and it was not written down anywhere and nine people in that lane saw all of it.

A man came down the lane on foot from the made road at about the eighth hour.

He was in a livery this county had not seen since the nineteenth of Rainmonth, and he was thirty, and he had come off the made road where the waggon was, and he walked nine hundred yards of lane at the low side because the high side had the timber on it, and he came down to the cut and stopped at the top of it and looked at nine people in a barrel drain in white frost and did not go down.

“**Is this the lane's barrow?**” he said.

Nobody answered it at once, and then four people said things at once, which is what happens in a lane.

“**He means the barrow,**” the woman of about thirty-eight said.

“**I want a barrow,**” the man said. “**For about two hours, to move stone off a sill four hundred yards up. My man has a barrow with a cracked cheek on it and there is another barrow in this lane lying on its side, and I would like to use it and have it back inside a day.**”

Nobody said anything about money. That is what happened and it is the whole of it, and four people in that lane understood it inside four seconds and five did not understand it at all, and the man of about fifty-one who had had a cart in a channel said it out loud afterwards in a market street four days later and nobody has been able to establish since whether he understood it on the morning or only afterwards.

The man of about fifty-one said, from the low side of the cut, that the barrow was the lane's, and he said it flatly and without heat, and he said it the way a man says a thing that is true and that he is not going to argue about.

The man in the livery said that he was sure it was the lane's, and that he was not asking the lane's leave in front of about nine people because he was on somebody's business and could not stand in a lane arguing, and that he would have it back inside a day and that he would leave the barrow against the wall at the top of the cut and not against the house.

Then he said that it was very good of them and went up the lane at four miles an hour, and the barrow went up the lane behind him about nine minutes later, and a young man of about twenty-two from the market town went with it, because a barrow weighs forty pounds empty and the lane is a mile and a half and the man in the livery had nine men and four hundred yards of sill and no time.

About nine people in that lane saw the barrow go up. About four of them had understood what had happened in the first nine seconds and about five of them had not, and the five of them went back to the cut and worked the silt out of the barrel of lime-washed brick until about the ninth hour, and the four of them stood about nine paces off and looked up the lane at the place where it comes out on the made road, and the four of them said nothing at all to anybody, and four of the other five said it was a decent man, and the fifth said it was a liberty and was told by about four people to shut up about it.

Nobody said the thing that any of the four of them had worked out, and the thing was not a complaint and was not a fear and was a finding, and it was this. That a county which will not shut a road can still be taken a barrow, and that it will hand the barrow over in about nine seconds, and that nobody in that lane had any standing to keep it, and that about nine people in a market street four miles away had been working for four and a half years on the standing and were not on that lane and were not going to be.

Aren Kest was one of the four who understood and he was the only one of the four who could have said it out loud in a room and he said nothing, and it was not because of the finding he had been carrying for eight weeks and it was because the four people who understood on that lane were the four people who keep a mile and a half of it, and about nine people in a market street were the arrangement, and he had known for about four years that those were two groups of people and he had never once seen it in the same lane at the same time in the same weather.

**And the cost of that Saturday is a column and it is nobody's, and about four inches of a lime crust and a raised cart and a barrow that weighs about forty pounds empty are not in it and none of them has a price.**

**a bar of iron at 5, a bag of shingle at 16, a pick handle at 17, a scythe stone at 19, a hurdle at 30, a barrow at 32, a rope of eighteen feet at 34, a pair of knee pads at 35, a hand lantern at 37, and a boat's fender at 39.** Five, sixteen, seventeen, nineteen, thirty, thirty-two, thirty-four, thirty-five, thirty-seven and thirty-nine is two hundred and sixty-four pence. **A mark is forty-eight pence and two hundred and sixty-four pence is five marks and twenty-four over.** The barrel came clear at about the seventh hour and the water went through it in about a minute and a half and went down the ditch and away, and the cut stopped running over the top, and the man of about fifty-one took the board out of the bottom of the channel for the first time since the middle of Rainmonth and stood it against the wall of the field and did not put it back in the morning, and about nine people in that lane saw that and about five of them understood what it meant and about four of them did not, and four hundred yards above them a waggon got a sill moved a foot and no more.

Then the frost lifted, as it does in Harvestmonth about two hours after it has begun, and the water in the ditch went from white to the ordinary colour of water, and the boy of about thirteen put the barrow that was left in its place against the wall at the top of the cut and not against the house, exactly as the man in the livery had said he would, and nobody had told him to, and nine people in a market town four miles off did not know any of it.
