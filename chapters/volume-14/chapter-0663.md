# Chapter 0663

**Rainmonth the twenty-eighth, YR 321, a Thursday and a market morning, the second hour in a market town of about nine hundred people two days off by made road, a woman of about fifty-six at a table in a counting house with nine pages in a bag, and a name given once in a cold room to one witness.**

The made road from the market town ends in this one and does not stop, and the difference between the two places is on the first morning and it stays on every morning after that.

In the market town there are about nine people at the boards and a man with a bar and a room with a door on a lane up thirteen stairs, and there is a thing in the street on a market morning that nine people know how to do. In this town there are about nine people at the boards on a Thursday because there is a market on Thursdays, and about four of them have a reason to be there, and nobody is standing about waiting for anybody.

Aren Kest had come up on the Wednesday evening, four miles an hour at the start of the made road and two at the end of it, and had slept in a barn with about nine other men who were on their way somewhere, and none of them had asked him anything and he had not asked them anything, which is the ordinary arrangement on a made road.

The sheet was on the boards. It was a quarter of it, folded in four, with the same three breaks in the same three places that every copy in nine counties has, and it had been up about eleven days, and it had come down once and gone up again on the Saturday, and nobody had read it out loud in this market street.

That last fact was the one that told Aren Kest he was two days into the right county. In the market town a sheet went up and got read within a week, badly, by somebody standing in front of it, and four people understood it and five told it wrong. Here it had been up about eleven days and about nine people in this town had walked past it and looked at the top of it and had not asked anybody what it was, because there was nobody here to ask.

He asked, at about the second hour, one question of a man of about thirty-three who sells cheese at the low end of the boards, and the man said that he had read it in about nine days ago and that he had not understood one word of it and that it was not his business and that he had a cart to load.

The woman was at a table in the counting house, which is a room with a counter in it and a door that is shut, and nobody in the market had any idea she was in the town, and Aren Kest found her because the counting house is the only building in this street with a fire lit in it at the second hour on a market morning.

She was about fifty-six and she had grey in the hair at the front and none at the back, and a bag on the floor at her feet with the strap over the corner of the table, and nine pages of writing in front of her in a hand better than any hand in nine counties, and she was writing the fourth page when he came in and she did not stop and she did not look up.

“**Sit down if you are going to,**” she said. “**And if you are the road office you are a day late and I have not been idle, and if you are not the road office then you are the reason I am here, and either way you may sit down.**”

He sat down.

She wrote the fourth page out and then the fifth, and the room was cold and the fire was small and there was a candle on the table that had been burning about an hour, and he understood in about four seconds that she had been at that table since before the market and had a bag she had not unpacked.

“**Nine pages,**” Aren Kest said.

“**Nine pages,**” Maud Trenchard said, without looking up, and then she stopped writing, and she put the pen down on the edge of the paper and turned her right hand over and looked at it, and that was the first thing she had done that was not writing.

That hand was stiff. It was stiff the way a hand is stiff when it has been writing for about an hour and a half and has been writing for six years before that, and she worked it open and shut twice and did not make a face about it, and he noticed, because a man who is good at loads is good at noticing what a thing is carrying.

“**You are the man who walks,**” she said. “**I have been told about a man who walks. I have been told that a man who walks came to a board in the town two days off with a stick and said one true thing about a ford and did not say the other three, and that a man with a badge said out loud in a street that the nine of you were looking at him and that he was not going to ask him, and that is the whole of what I have been given about you and it is more than I usually get, and I would like you to know that I was told it as a warning and not as a compliment.**”

“**You have not asked me who I am,**” she said, “**which is the first decent thing anybody has done in about nine years, and I am going to tell you anyway, because I have said my own name out loud in a room about nine hundred times and I would like it said in this one in front of a witness. My name is Maud Trenchard. I am about fifty-six. I have been a clerk for about six years and I drafted the instrument that is on the boards in your market street, and the practice in the district is that where a county is written back about, the person who wrote the words goes down.**”

“**The man who sells cheese will have it by the fifth hour, and four people in this market will have it by the second hour on Saturday, and by the following week nine counties will know it,**” Aren Kest said. “**None of that is your doing and all of it is a name at the top of a thing, and I am telling you in a room with nobody in it but the two of us, which is the only place I have ever been able to say it.**”

“**I did not know you were coming,**” Aren Kest said.

“**Nobody knew I was coming,**” Maud Trenchard said. “**I am here because a man in a livery wrote a line to a room in the district and the line said that this county has put up the sheet and has not complied and does not appear to intend to. That is the whole of what he wrote and it is about nine words and it is a true thing, and it is not a punishment, and it is not an honour either.**”

“**What is it, then?**” Aren Kest said.

“**It is that you will be asked what it was for by a person in the street,**” she said. “**And I have not decided what I am going to say, and I have had two days of a made road to decide in, and I have not decided, which has not happened to me in about nine years.**”

Then she picked the pen up and went on writing, and she wrote the sixth page in about four minutes, and he watched her do it, and it was the first time in about four years that he had watched somebody do the thing he did.

The hand was on the paper the whole time and it did not hurry and it did not stop, and every line was even, and the downstrokes were heavier than the upstrokes because of how she held the pen. At the top of the eighth page her right hand began to go and she moved the pen to her left without a word and carried on, and the left was worse, and she did not change the size of her writing and did not slow down.

“**You have written that before,**” Aren Kest said.

“**I have written that page four times,**” she said. “**Nine pages is about nine hundred words, and I wrote the first one in a room in the district in about nine days and have made it fair twice for two counties, and I have the fair copy of the first making in a bag on this floor, and I am going to tell you now that it is the whole of it and that your sheet on your boards is not.**”

“**The captain had nine pages on the nineteenth of Rainmonth at a board in the rain in the town two days off,**” Aren Kest said, “**and he said out loud that a man with a badge had said in that street a week before that it was not nine pages and that he was right, and that nobody in nine counties had said a word to him about it.**”

“**Then that is the third time,**” Maud Trenchard said, “**and I would like it in a street if you can manage it, and I cannot, because I have a bag on this floor with a fair copy in it and nowhere to put it. That is the thing I have not decided about. You may have guessed it, so I will say it.**”

“**Say it,**” Aren Kest said.

“**I cannot leave it,**” she said. “**I have been in this market a quarter of an hour and I have asked four people where a county keeps a paper. The smith keeps his bills in a hat. A woman of about sixty at the end of the street has a chest with three locks on it and she offered me all three. A man of about thirty-three who sells cheese said that a county keeps its papers in the county, which is a true sentence. There is no place in nine counties for nine pages, and the room with the press and the drawer is in a town about two days off and belongs to one woman, and I am not going to arrive at that room with a fair copy of the whole of this and put it on her table and make her the keeper of the Marshal's hand.**”

She said the last four words without any weight on them, the way a person says the name of a thing they have written out four times.

Nobody thanked her for that, because she had not said it for anybody.

Then she put both hands flat on the table, which she had not done, and said the thing she had come down to say, to a man she had met a quarter of an hour ago and not to a county.

“**I want nothing I write ever to be obeyed by anybody who has not chosen to obey it,**” she said. “**That is my want and it is not a modest one and I have never said it out loud to a living person before today, and I would like that noticed and I would like it let stand.**”

“**Why is that,**” Aren Kest said.

“**Because I have drafted about four hundred instruments in six years,**” she said, “**and every one of them asked somebody to do a thing, and asking is the whole of what an instrument is, and I have never once had a difficulty with that, and about four hundred people in nine counties have obeyed things I wrote and nine of them are alive. And there is one sentence in the nine pages on this table that does not ask. It instructs. It is the ninth line of the second page, and I have not said it out loud to a living person until about a minute ago, and I have known for five weeks that it is the sentence the whole thing runs on.**”

“**Say the sentence,**” Aren Kest said.

“**And stand taken from the hour of this sheet, which hour is the hour of the sealing of it.**”

The fire moved. The candle had about two inches of it left and neither of them looked at it.

“**I wrote that,**” she said. “**I was told it was a matter of form. It is not. It is the difference between a thing a person agrees to and a thing a person is already inside of, and five weeks ago every person in nine counties was already inside this, and nobody asked them, and I put them there, and I did it in four days and I have not been right since.**”

“**Who told you it was a matter of form,**” Aren Kest said.

“**A man whose name is on it,**” she said, and she did not say the name and he did not ask her for it, and about four hundred paces of lane in a town two days off there is a woman of about twenty-nine who keeps a room and had said out loud in a market street that a name in a room is a head at the top of a thing, and the two of those facts sat on the same table about four hundred paces apart and neither of them was on a page.

“**You are the fifth person to ask me about that sentence,**” she said. “**And the four before you were all Crown, and all four wanted to know if I would say it out loud in a market, and I have said it out loud in a market exactly once in six years, in a room with two men in it, and I have thought about it every week since. I am going to say it in this street in about two hours if these nine people will stand still long enough, and I have not decided that either, and I am telling you so that you cannot tell me afterwards that I did not know what I was doing.**”

“**Then say the rest of it,**” Maud Trenchard said. “**You have been nine seconds looking at my hand and I have been watching you do it. You walked eight miles in the dark to look at four marks of lime in a ford and came back with something you have not said, and I have been told about that too. I would like the thing you have not said, because there is nothing in nine hundred words on this floor that you have not already worked out. And if you are not going to say it, I would like to know that in words, so that I can stop waiting.**”

“**You will be asked why you are not safe,**” Aren Kest said, and he said it because he had worked it out in nine seconds, and it was a thing that had to be said out loud before the market filled, and not because he was kind.

Maud Trenchard wrote the ninth page while he said it and did not look up.

“**Because I drafted it and I would draft the next one,**” she said. “**I have thought about that for two days on a made road and I have arrived at a place. I am not going to do anything clever, because clever is what drafters do and it changes nothing and it makes me feel better. The next one will be on a desk in a room in the district in nine days and the woman who asks me for it will not be a man with a stick two days off on a bad road. I am not going to refuse and I am not going to pretend I have, and the two of those are the only honest things available to me, and I am going to say one of them out loud in a market street in about two hours, and I have not decided which, and that is not a trick. It is a woman deciding, in a cold room, with a stiff hand, about nine feet from a fire that is not big enough.**”

Nobody thanked her and Aren Kest did not tell her she was not safe and did not tell her she was safe, and he thought about a rule nine people held in nine hands and about the whole of the rest of his finding sitting in him.

Then he said the one true small thing he had.

“**You have written that sentence in four hands and told me it is the sentence the whole thing runs on, and I have known about a mark in a ford for six weeks and have not said it. Between the two of us there are two true things and nine people in this county who could carry either one and neither of us has handed over,**” he said. “**I am not saying that to be fair. I am saying it because I have been the man a thing arrives for for four years and the two of us have just found out that neither of us has arrived for anybody in a year.**”

**And the cost of that Thursday is a column, and the column belongs to nobody, and nine pages of a fair copy in a bag that cannot be put down and a stiff hand that writes the last three pages with the wrong one are not in it and neither of them has a price.**

**a candlestick at 7, a slice of bread at 9, a bundle of tape at 13, a seal box at 19, a bundle of laths at 23, a hobnail at 29, a wicket at 30, a pound of nails at 31, a shutter hinge at 35, and a press handle at 39.** Seven, nine, thirteen, nineteen, twenty-three, twenty-nine, thirty, thirty-one, thirty-five and thirty-nine is two hundred and thirty-five pence. **A mark is forty-eight pence and two hundred and thirty-five pence is four marks and forty-three over.** The nine pages were finished at about the fourth hour, and Maud Trenchard folded them in four, and put the bag on the floor, and did not unpack it, and had not unpacked it when she came down two days before either, and the candle went out about nine minutes later because the counting house has a fire in it at the second hour and not after the fourth.

Then she went out into the market and asked four more people the question she had asked four people inside, which is where a county keeps a paper, and about nine people heard her ask it and about four answered and none of the four was the same, and a man of about forty-four who keeps a road in a county four days further off said, out loud, to about nine people, that there was a room with a door on a lane in a town two days off with a press in it and a drawer and a woman of about twenty-nine who would not put a paper in a drawer for tidiness.

He said it because he had come to this market to see for himself whether the quarter sheet on the boards said the same thing as the half of one a man in a livery had read out at a gate in his own county, and because it did not, and because he has spent nine days working out what to do with that, and because he has a barrow wheel to get home by and a road four in ten of whose households cannot go round in the worst week, and he is not a man who thinks a woman keeping a drawer fixes that, and he has said so out loud twice in two markets.

She asked him whether she might write it down, and he said yes, and she wrote it on the back of the ninth page, which is the page the seal is folded into, and then she stopped, and looked at what she had written, and asked him again whether she might write it down, and he said yes, and she wrote it out again on the back of the eighth, and the bag went back on the floor with nine pages in it and two lines of a stranger's road on the outside.

The man of about thirty-three who sells cheese came back through the market at about the fifth hour with his cart and stopped, and he said, in the ordinary way a cheese seller says a thing, that he had heard a woman in the counting house talking about a hand that was not hers, and asked her, in front of about nine people, whether she was the Crown.

“**Are you the Crown?**”

“**I am the person who wrote it,**” Maud Trenchard said, from the step of the counting house door, with the bag in her hand.

“**Well,**” the man said, and got on his cart, and went nine paces, and turned round and said that if it was the same hand then the whole of it had been in a room two days off for five weeks before any of them had seen a word of it, and that he crossed at the ferry stone twice a day with a load on and it had not been shut once in all the time he had been running the cart, and that he was going to have to explain that to somebody as well.

Nobody thanked anybody, and about nine people in that market had heard a man in a cart say in nine seconds the whole of what the rest of that morning had been about, and about four of them had understood that the five weeks had started in a room and not at a gate.
