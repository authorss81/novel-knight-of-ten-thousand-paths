# Chapter 0652

**Longlight the first, YR 321, a Wednesday and not a market morning, the second hour at the counter boards in the market street with the room over the weigh-house shut behind its own door, a hearing standing on the first of a month with about nine people in it, and a woman of about thirty-eight who came eleven days with one question.**

The first of a month is the only day in the year on which the hearing stands by any arrangement at all, and it stands in the street, and it has no bench in it, and about nine people came.

Aren Kest had done nine lanes in six days and had finished them at about the fourth hour of the day before, at a gate belonging to a man of about forty-four who had listened to the whole of it with the gate open and had then said that he would think about it, which is more than most of them did. He came down the lane at about the second hour on the first of Longlight with his stick in his left hand and about four days of nothing in front of him, and the market was not on, because the market is on Saturdays and Thursdays and today was a Wednesday, and the street had about four carts in it and a man with a bar going about the boards.

Nine people came by about the third hour and they were not the same nine as any other nine, and the badge-man said out loud at the low end of the boards that he had never once in about thirty-one years seen the same nine, and that he had stopped expecting to, and that a thing which depends on the same nine arriving is a thing that has already ended.

The copyist of about twenty-six was there with the register in her apron, because the register goes to the street on the first of a month and has gone to the street on the first of a month since the hearing started, and it stands at nine questions with no finding under any of them and nothing has gone under any of them in about four years and a half.

The woman of about thirty-eight came at about the third hour with the woman of about sixty and the boy of about nine and she had slept in a barn and not in a house and she said so, and she said it to nobody in particular and in a way that was not complaint.

The badge-man asked her who she was, in the open, in about the ordinary words.

“**Salla Dunnock,**” she said. “**And I am not the one who holds the post.**”

“**That is a strange thing to open with,**” the badge-man said.

“**It is the first true thing about me in this county and I would rather you had it before the question,**” Salla Dunnock said.

Then she told them, in nine sentences and in the order it had happened to her, that the coast of returning roads had written in Mudmonth that it would send nobody to any meeting, and that the reason given was that the only person among them who could be spared for the length of an argument was the one who holds the post, and that a post does not get left. She said that the letter was on a file in the room over a weigh-house and had not been thrown away and that nobody had argued with it.

“**They changed their mind,**” she said. “**In Hearthmonth they changed their mind and they told me and gave me the reason, which I am going to say because it is the reason I am standing here and not the reason I am on a cart. They changed their mind because a letter can be read twice and put down, and a person cannot be put down, and there is about to be something that needs a person to be told in a room and not in a letter, and they could not tell what it was, and they would not go without knowing.**” She stopped there and looked at the boards. “**That is all of it. I am not important. I am the person who could be got away.**”

Nobody thanked the coast for sending her and the woman of about sixty said out loud, from the wall she was sitting on, that the post had been held by nobody at all for about nine days and that this was the answer to that and she had said it to nobody in the street and she was saying it here because here was where she was.

Then the badge-man read the standing of the hearing out loud, and nobody stopped him and nobody helped him.

“**The hearing stands in the street on the first of every month and it has no bench in it and it cannot say that a sheet is false. The book has nine questions in it and no finding under any of them. The fourth line of the outgoing sheet is ruled and it is empty and it is not going to be filled and the person who keeps the sheet has said that out loud about four times in a year. Nine people have each got a piece of paper with three clauses on it and there is no copy of anything. There is no head in nine counties with which to call a meeting and the man who says that most often has a badge and no head, and that is not a fault in him.**”

Nobody said anything at that, and about four people in that street had heard the fourth line of the outgoing sheet and about five had not.

Then Salla Dunnock asked her question, and she asked it in the street, in about nine counties' hearing, with about nine people in front of her, which she had not wanted to do and had been told by the copyist that it was the only place in this county where a thing could be asked out loud and be written down.

“**There is a man on this road,**” she said, “**and I want to know what my road is for.**”

Nobody answered that for a while.

“**Say the question,**” the badge-man said. “**You have said a sentence and the sentence has about nine meanings in it and I am thirty-one years in this work and I want the one you mean.**”

“**Are we to be an army,**” Salla Dunnock said.

The street was very quiet in the way a market street is quiet at about the fourth hour when the market is not on, which is not silent and is the sound of a place that is about four people and a bar going about.

“**Because I have been in this town five days,**” she said, “**and I have been asked one question and it was whether I had a bed, and I have asked one and it is whether we are to be an army, and I would like to go back and be able to tell nine hundred people which one of the two it was that I came for. And I am not asking anybody here to promise me anything. I am asking whether there is anybody in nine counties who has been asked what a road is for, and whether anybody has been asked whether it will be used, and if there has not then I would like to go home and say so.**”

The copyist put her hand flat on the register and did not open it, and did not write anything, which for the copyist of about twenty-six was a thing that about four people in that street understood and about five did not.

The badge-man said the true thing, and he said it slowly, and he turned the badge over in his fingers the way a man turns a thing he has carried too long.

“**Nobody has asked you,**” he said. “**I have not asked you. The Crown has not asked you, so far as I know, and I have been in this work for about thirty-one years and I would be prepared to say out loud in a street that if the Crown had asked you I should have heard of it inside a month, because a Crown that asks a road anything writes it down, and we would get the writing.**” He put the badge back inside his coat. “**And I am going to tell you the other half of it, which is that I have been standing in this street for a year with about nine people in it, and about four of them keep a road, and not one of us has ever been asked what a road is for. I have a rule in nine hands in front of me that is worth more than anything in this county and it is a rule a man going to the end of a road and saying it out loud before he shuts it, and there is no rule anywhere in nine counties a man arriving at the end of a road and taking it, and I have thought about that for about two days since the last time anybody said it out loud and I have not got round to it.**”

“**Then it is too late,**” Salla Dunnock said.

“**It is not too late,**” the badge-man said. “**It is early, and there is a difference, and I will not let it be confused in a street.**” He looked down the lane towards the gate and then he said the rest of it, and he said it to her and not to the nine people, which she noticed. “**It is early because nothing has come up that road. I have watched that road every day since I said this and nothing has come up it. You came up it eleven days in the other direction and I heard about you from a carter a week ago and I have not sent anybody to find you, because there is nothing to find. If something comes up that road, it will not come up to a rule. It will come up to a lane with about nine people in it and a cart at the gate.**”

She said that she understood and that it was not an answer, and he said that it was not one.

“**Then what would you do, if it came?**” she said. “**You have been in that street about a year and you have a badge and no head and about nine people who come, and I have been in this town two days and I have asked one question and got a true thing. If it comes up that road on a Thursday, what does the man with the badge do?**”

The badge-man took about as long to answer it as it takes a carter to get a load round, and about four people in that street watched him not answer it, and about five watched him decide not to.

“**I have been asking myself that since the last time a person said it out loud in this street,**” he said, “**and the honest answer is that I have got nothing, and I am not going to give you something in a street on a Wednesday to keep you on a cart another day, and I would rather you went home and told nine hundred people that a man of fifty-eight in nine counties had nothing.**”

Then the badge-man asked her whether anybody on the coast road had come up past her in the last nine days, and she said no, and he said good, in the manner of a man who has been given an answer he did not much want, and turned to somebody else.

Aren Kest had not been part of any of it and had been standing about four paces off with his stick against the boards and his good leg on the stones, and about nine people in the street had looked at him at some point in the last half hour and nobody had asked him anything, and he had got used to that about four months ago and had stopped noticing it, which he noticed on about the fifth of Longlight and which turned out to be the shape of the whole winter.

He thought about the eleven days and about the fact that he had a woman of about thirty-eight in a lane because the coast had decided that a letter can be put down and a person cannot, and about the fact that a person of about sixty had a post held by nobody for about nine days, and a barn in Longlight.

He also thought, because he could not stop it and had not been able to stop it since about the fourth hour on the second of a month, about the other thing, which was that he had been asked four times in his life whether he wanted the thing he was doing and had answered it once, in a lane, to about four people who could hear, and that none of those four had been in this street this morning, and that the woman who had asked him the last time had crossed four miles of made road and two days of ford to ask him a different question and had been given an answer to it that she had told him was the answer to a different question.

That is the whole of what happened to him in that street on the first of a month, and he did not speak in it, and nobody asked him anything in it, and about four of the nine people at the boards could have said afterwards that he had been present and about five could not have.

**And the cost of that Wednesday is a column and it is nobody's, and nine questions with no finding under any of them and a register carried down nine stairs and back up to a street and a post on a coast held by nobody for about nine days are not in it and none of them has a price.**

**a hoop of osier at 3, a pad of blotting paper at 4, a boot brush at 7, a coil of new cord at 14, a sack of lime at 22, a trestle at 28, a hand shovel at 33, a rushlight holder at 37, a leather strap at 39, and a candlestick at 41.** Three, four, seven, fourteen, twenty-two, twenty-eight, thirty-three, thirty-seven, thirty-nine, and forty-one is two hundred and twenty-eight pence. **A mark is forty-eight pence and two hundred and twenty-eight pence is four marks and thirty-six over.** The boy of about nine slept on the cart that night and the cart was outside the weigh-house because the cart belonged to a carter who owed the weigh-house nothing and was asked to put it there by a woman of about sixty who told him it was for the good of his soul, which the carter said was not a thing his soul understood and then put it there anyway, and the weigh-house man moved his own cart out of the yard to the lane, without being asked and without saying anything about it, and about nine people in the market street knew about it inside two days and about five of them told it and about four did not.

Then the badge-man said the standing notice on the first of a month, which is the only notice that has ever had a day attached to it, and the nine people who had come stayed about half an hour past it, and Salla Dunnock did not ask her question again, and the copyist went down the lane with the register against her chest and did not open it on the way, and about four paces from the low end of the boards she stopped and turned round and said one sentence back to the street, out loud, and it was the only sentence she said all morning that was not about herself.

“**There is nobody here who has been asked what a road is for,**” she said, “**and I would like it written down that I asked it.**”

And the copyist said that it was not going in the register, and gave the reason, which was that the register held nine questions that had been asked in this county by people who lived in it and that a question asked by a person who had come eleven days to ask it was not one of theirs, and that if it went in there it would be a tenth question with nobody able to say who had standing to have asked it, and that the last thing that room needed was a question with no name at the top of it.

And Salla Dunnock said that she had a name at the top of it and it was Salla Dunnock and she had said it in a street in front of about nine people, and the copyist said that was correct and that it was not the point, and then she took the register down to the room over the weigh-house and shut the door behind it, which it was shut behind already, and nobody followed her, and the street went back to being about four carts and a bar.
