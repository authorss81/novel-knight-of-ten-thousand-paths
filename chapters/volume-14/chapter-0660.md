# Chapter 0660

**Rainmonth the nineteenth, YR 321, a Tuesday and not a market morning, the second hour at the low end of the counter boards at the town's own gate, a captain of about forty-four with a sealed warrant who has come up four days' road with about nine hundred men and asks for the name of a road, and is told there is no head in nine counties with which to give him one.**

The gate of the market town is where a drain takes the water off about four miles of made road and goes under the road into the channel, and there is a culvert in it with three courses of sill in lime and the middle course gone between two clean joints, and there is four feet of silt and weed across the mouth of it, and the man of about fifty who laid the courses in Greenmonth has been down twice since and looked at them and put his hand on the top course and said that it would do.

The stone for a fourth course is in a line on the bank with the mallet on top of it, where anybody coming off the road can see it.

Nobody picked the mallet up. Not that morning, and not for about a fortnight, and four people who came out to look at the column said that there was a line of stone on the bank with a mallet on it and asked what it was, and about nine people told them, and about five of them asked why nobody had laid it, and nobody had an answer that was better than the one the man of about fifty had given about the lime and a winter.

The board was at the low end of the same gate, which is where the boards always are when the market is not on, and there were about nine people at it at about the second hour on a Tuesday in Rainmonth because there were nine men in a livery this county had not seen since anybody could remember at the low end of the lane and they had stopped.

The captain came up to the boards.

He did not come to them the way a man comes to a counter. He came up the lane at a walk, stopped nine feet off them, and stood there and let about nine people look at him, and about four people who had heard about him for four days looked at him and about five had not.

He was about forty-four and he was not tired in the way a man who has come up four days of road is tired, which is a thing that shows in the legs. He had come up the last mile on foot and the boots were good and the coat was a coat that has been worn nine hundred miles and has been brushed since.

“**I am going to ask one question and I would like it answered in a way I can put in a book,**” he said. “**My name is Halvard Rees and I have a warrant under the seal of the marshal's office and I have come up this road with about nine hundred men and four days of forage behind them, and I am not going to put a hand on anybody or take anything off anybody until I have asked this and been answered.**”

Nobody said anything and about nine people were standing at a board in a wet street.

“**The paper I carry says the works, anchors and roads of Bellweather and of every road standing in connection with them. I have been four days on that road. I have not been to any works. I have not seen any anchor. I have a map made nine years ago of about nine counties and on that map there is a mark at a place called the ninth milestone and a mark at a place called the ferry stone, and between them four miles, and seven other marks that are not roads at all but are places where somebody once put something in the ground and wrote it down.**”

“**I have been asking since Tuesday what those places are,**” Halvard Rees said, “**and I have been told by nine people that nobody knows and one man who told me that nobody would know and that this was not a Crown matter, which is correct and is not much use to me.**”

“**What is the name of the road?**”

He said it plainly, with no threat in it at all, and four people in that street noticed that there was no threat in it, and about five did not.

The badge-man was at the low end of the boards. He had been there since about the second hour and he had said nothing while the whole of it was being said and nobody had asked him anything, and he had a badge and no head and about thirty-one years.

“**There is no head in nine counties with which to give you the name of a road,**” the badge-man said, “**and I am not being difficult, and I have said that sentence in this street nine times in a year and I have said it every time a meeting has been asked for and not been called, and I have never once had to say it about a road, and I would like it noticed that this is the first time in thirty-one years that anybody has wanted something out of this that is a name.**”

“**I am not asking for a document,**” Halvard Rees said. “**I am asking for a name. I have nine hundred men on a road four days long and about eleven places marked on a map and I need to know which of them the works stand behind. I am not going into a village and taking a bell off a bridge because a map has a mark on it. Give me the name of one road and I will take my nine hundred men and stand them on it and we will all be in the same county and everybody will know what is happening.**”

“**Then there is no road to give you a name for,**” the badge-man said.

“**Say that again?**”

“**There is no road in nine counties with a head over it,**” the badge-man said, “**and about three of them kept by people who will tell you their own road's name and about four of them kept by people who keep a road and do not and have never been asked, and there is no head in nine counties, and I will not make one, and if I made one this morning you would be able to walk up this road with nine hundred men and put a name under nine counties' feet and it would be a name I made in a street at about the second hour on a Tuesday.**”

“**You have refused to make a head,**” Halvard Rees said, “**which is not the same as having refused me, because I did not ask you to make one. I asked for a name.**”

“**And I have said that there is none, and that is the true answer and I have given it to you at about the second hour and about nine people have heard it, and I am not going to soften it.**”

Then Halvard Rees took out of the inside of his coat the sheet, and the sheet that had been on the boards in this market street since the twelfth of Longlight was a quarter of it, and his copy had nine pages and a seal on the fold, and he broke the seal in a market street in front of about nine people because he had decided that he was not going to do it in an office.

“**Before I read it,**” he said, “**a man with a badge stood at these boards on the twelfth of Longlight and said out loud that this was not nine pages of it, and he was right, and he has been right about it in public for a week and I have been in this county four days and nobody has said a word to me about it, which tells me the county does not think it matters.**”

**It matters,** the badge-man said, from about nine paces off, and nobody had asked him.

“**I know it matters,**” Halvard Rees said. “**That is why I am reading it at a board.**”

“**I have carried the whole of it for about four days and the quarter for about nine, and I have wanted to open it in a room,**” he said, “**and I have opened it at a board in the rain, and I am going to read it out because I have been in this county four days and every person I have spoken to has told me the truth, and about four of them have told me the same truth twice, and the least I can do is read this where they can hear it.**”

He read it out.

“**That the works and anchors and roads aforesaid are taken into the keeping of the marshal's officers, and that for the purpose of the taking and the keeping of the same, wherever in the said counties the works, anchors or roads stand, be they on a made road, on a lane, on a ford, on a track, on a bridge or on the ground, whether or not the said works, anchors or roads are named in any register, book, roll, return or sheet, the said counties and every place within them are and shall be within the taking, and that any person, being asked by an officer of the Crown, which place or road is meant, shall answer.**”

Nobody in that street said anything, and the rain started again about nine minutes later and went on for the rest of the morning.

“**It does not say a road,**” Halvard Rees said, “**and I noticed that on the first day and I have said nothing about it for four days because I thought it was a piece of legal wording and about nine men in my own office could not tell me what it meant, and this morning a woman of about thirty-eight told me what it meant and did not tell me nicely.**” He turned the paper about. “**Any person being asked which place or road is meant shall answer. You do not have to be able to name it. You have to answer. And that is why I wanted a name from the man with the badge and it is not because I wanted a road. It is because I wanted to see whether a place in nine counties would give me something I could put in a book, and if it would, then this county knows what is happening to it.**”

Nobody thanked him for reading it out and about four people in that street understood the whole of what had just happened and about five understood about half of it.

“**And it is the right instrument,**” Halvard Rees said, and he said it flatly and in the middle of everything else, and about nine people heard it. “**I want that said in a street and I want it said by me and not by somebody else. It is the right instrument because I have nine hundred men on a road four days long and if I come to a ford in spate with nine hundred men behind me I will put about nine hundred men in the water and forty of them will drown, and this is not a thing I say to be easy with myself, it is a thing I have been told is what happens and I have believed the man who told me because I have seen the ford. That paper says a place is in the taking. Without it, a ford in a county is not in the taking and I have nine hundred men on a road with no legal standing on nine miles of it and the first man with a grievance is a man who is owed a wage, and he is owed a wage, and I would be the man taking it.**”

He put the paper back inside his coat and did not close it, and about nine people in that street found it difficult to look at him after that and about five found it easy.

Then he asked the fourth question and he asked it in the ordinary way and he asked about nine people and not Aren Kest in particular.

“**Somebody has to answer, then. Not a name. Somebody in this county who can say yes to a place. Has anybody thought about that?**”

Nobody answered that for about as long as a man counts a horse.

Aren Kest was at the high end of the boards with his stick in his left hand and his good leg on the stones and about eleven inches of mud on the right boot, and he had been in the water at a ford for eleven minutes on the second of Rainmonth, and he had carried eleven barrows, and he had been in the room over the weigh-house on the second of Rainmonth and had told one person what he had found and had not told anybody else.

He had the answer. He had had it for three weeks and two days. A measurement is not a march, and the second reading of a road is not the same instrument, and there are about nine hundred men behind a man with a book who has read the second clause of a warrant out loud at a board in the rain.

And about nine people in that street turned round and looked at him at about the same moment, which is a thing that happens, and which had happened four times in four years in four rooms, and which he had never once been asked about and had never once said anything about.

“**They are looking at you,**” the badge-man said, out loud, at the low end of the boards, to nobody in particular, in the manner of a man who has been saying things out loud in streets for thirty-one years. “**I am not going to ask you. I am going to say that they are.**”

Nobody in that street argued with him about it and about nine people kept looking and about four of them looked away and about five did not.

Aren Kest put his weight off his right leg and took his stick in his right hand instead of his left, which takes longer than it should, and said one sentence.

“**There is a ford at the low end of a woman's road about four miles down this lane and it has four marks in it at the height of a thumb and the marks are not about the road.**”

Halvard Rees looked at him for about a second and wrote it down, and wrote it down at once, because he had said he would answer any person being asked, and because a man who is handed a useful fact by a stranger on a wet road writes it down in front of about nine people and does not stop to be anything else about it.

He wrote it in the small book, and about four people in that street watched a Crown officer write down a fact about a ford in this county in a book with a column in it, and about five looked at the badge-man.

“**And that was the whole of what he said, and it was the right fact, and it was about nine feet of standing water on about four hundred yards of a road, and about nine hundred men who had been at a gate for half an hour could not have used it, and the argument that the rest of the town would need had been in his head for three weeks and two days and had stayed there while a man read out a warrant at a board and asked a direct question and got one true small thing.**”

Nobody said he was wrong, and that is what happened, and it is the fourth time, and the badge-man noticed it.

The badge-man noticed it in the way he notices things, which is by not saying anything, and he said afterwards, in a lane, to nobody, that he had been going to ask a man of about thirty-four with a sheet in his coat to write it down and had not done it, and that the reason he had not done it was that he had wanted to see what a man would say when nine people turned round, and that he had seen what the man would say, and that it was the only true small thing available to him at about the third hour on a Tuesday in the rain in a town of about two thousand people.

Nobody heard him say that except the man of about thirty-four, who heard most of it.

Then Halvard Rees said the last thing of that morning and he said it to the badge-man and to about nine people.

“**I am going to go back down this road tonight,**” he said, “**and I am going to send about four men forward and I am going to put them at the ford you have just told me about, because that ford is in the taking under a paper I have read out loud at a board, and if I do not take it now then somebody else will take it in about four days and will not have nine hundred men to notice doing it, and I have thought about that for about nine days and I have decided that I do not care, because the paper is the paper.**”

Nobody thanked him and nobody told him he was wrong and about nine people in that street understood that a Crown captain had voluntarily made a thing easier for himself on the grounds that it was easier for him and worse for nobody but a road.

Then the small book came out again, on the officer's hip, in the officer's hand, and Halvard Rees said one more thing that was about the crew and not about the road.

“**There are four bridge crews working forward on this lane. There is one in this county with a yard four hundred yards up a road in another town, and it has taken a contract from my advance party, and it is working ahead of me and I have not had to ask it twice.**” He shut the book. “**I am not telling you that because it is any use to you. I am telling you because I said in this street that I would answer any person being asked and about nine of you asked me something in nine days and I have not had to lie to one of you, and I would rather somebody in this county knew that its own people are on this road and did not find it out at about the seventh hour from a man in a different livery.**”

Then he put his hat on and went down the lane at about the same walk, and about nine men in a livery this county had never seen went down the lane behind him, and about nine people stood at a board in a market street in the rain and watched about nine men in a livery go down the lane and did not follow them.

Nobody thanked anybody and nobody said what any of it meant.

**And the cost of that Tuesday is a column and it is nobody's, and a contract taken by a yard in this county and four marks of lime at the height of a thumb are not in it and neither of them has a price.**

**a handbill at 16, a printed proclamation at 21, a sheet of ruled paper at 27, a stick of red chalk at 30, a pair of gloves at 34, a pad of blotting paper at 35, a sealed warrant at 37, a seal matrix at 39, a leather bag at 40, and a rack of vellum at 41.** Sixteen, twenty-one, twenty-seven, thirty, thirty-four, thirty-five, thirty-seven, thirty-nine, forty, and forty-one is three hundred and twenty pence. **A mark is forty-eight pence and three hundred and twenty pence is six marks and thirty-two over.** The mallet was still on the stone on the bank at about the ninth hour that night and it was still there four days later and about nine people who went out to look at it in the four days did not pick it up and about five of them could not say afterwards why they had gone out to look at it, and the silting over the three courses went from about four feet to about three and a half in about four days and the man of about fifty came down on the Thursday, looked at it, put his hand on the top course, and said that it would do.

Then about four days after that, a man of about thirty-four with a sheet in his coat stood at the low end of the boards in about the second hour on a market morning and said out loud, to about nine people, a question that he had written on his own sheet with no head over it and had carried four days in his coat and had not shown it to anybody, and nobody copied it.

“**The paper says anybody asked which place is meant shall answer. Who in this county answers when nobody has been asked anything yet?**”
