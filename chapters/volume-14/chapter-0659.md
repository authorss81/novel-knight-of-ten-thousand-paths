# Chapter 0659

**Rainmonth the second, YR 321, a Saturday and a market morning, a wagon coming back empty off four days of road with a boy of about nineteen on it, two boards and a wedge at a ford that has been in spate for about a month, and about nine hundred men counted from four miles off in a wet light by people who have never seen a column.**

The wagon came in at about the sixth hour on the Saturday, empty, with the boy of about nineteen on the tailboard and nine inches of mud on the wheels and a nose on him that had gone the colour of the ford.

He came down the lane to the market town's gate at four miles an hour because the near side of the road was under three inches and he was not going to break an axle for four pence, and he came in through the gate at about the seventh hour and about nine people were in the street, because it was a market morning and about four hundred of them were, and four of the four hundred were watching him come down the lane and had been for some time.

“**She got in about seven days ago,**” he said, to anybody. “**Wet from the ford both ways. The old one was awake about eight days out of it. The boy has not been sick.**”

“**Where is the wagon going?**”

“**Empty to the ford and back up the coast road with nine sacks of oats in it,**” the boy said, “**and I am going home at the seventh hour on the Monday with a week's wage and my father is going to say I should have taken the four days.**”

Nobody thanked him and about nine people in that market street had about nine things to do and went and did them.

Then the boy of about nineteen said the thing he had come back to say, which is that he had counted something on the way up and had counted it twice because he did not believe it the first time, and that he had not said it to anybody on the road and had said it to a carter at about the top of the made lane and that the carter had said that he should not have counted it at all.

“**I counted them on the Friday, going up,**” the boy said, “**a quarter of a mile back where the lane comes out on the made road, and they came up past the wagon all morning while four women sat in it and one of them was asleep. I counted the cartwheels, not the carts, because you cannot see the carts in a lane, and I counted the wheels coming the other way as well because there was nothing else to do with my eyes and because it seemed like a thing to count at the time.**” He stopped and worked it out. “**And I got four hundred and something the first time and six hundred and something the second time and I do not know which it was and I am not going to tell anybody either of them, because I counted on a road with wet on it and I did not count it twice the same way twice.**”

“**A wagon's wheel is a wagon's wheel,**” the badge-man said, “**and a horse in a line of horses is a near wheel and a far wheel and the third horse has two near wheels and two far ones and a man on a road in the wet at a quarter of a mile is not a surveyor. You did right and I would not want a figure off you.**”

Nobody had asked for one and nobody got one.

**We had them all morning,** the boy said. **Coming up past us the whole way from the ford, and at the ford they were already in the water putting men across who were not ours, and I counted the ones that got past and I counted the ones that did not go by, and the number of the ones that did not go by is the number I am not going to say.**

Nobody asked him for it and about nine people in that market street understood that a boy of about nineteen had said the thing he had come back to say, and about five of them understood the rest of it.

About four people in that market street went to about four gates over the following two hours and looked up the lane at the place where it comes out on the made road, and about five of them did not go, and about nine hundred men had come up that lane over the four days before that, and a mile and a half of it is not made, and nobody in nine counties has a figure for the column and nobody in nine counties is going to be given one.

The work at the ford was begun on the Saturday afternoon and took four days, which everybody in this market town knows exactly the length of, and the only interesting thing about it is who did it.

The false baulm had held. That is what a false baulm is for and it had held a wagon in spate and about nine carts and about forty feet of water going over the top of it in the worst hour, and the hard ground under the near end had printed a quarter of an inch into itself, which is about what Tovan Kest said it would do on about the eighth of Longlight at the low end of a woman's road with a bar in his hand, and the end of the baulm had come down a quarter of an inch, which is what he said it would do, and there was a stone on the end of it the size of a fist and a man could find it in four seconds.

Nobody charged Tovan Kest for the baulm and nobody paid Tovan Kest for the baulm, because it was built out of nine feet of board and one wedge that came out of a wagon and not out of anybody's money, and he had put a stone on the end of it so that anybody could see where a mistake had been, and there had not been one.

What was left to do was the packing. The near nine feet of the approach had gone down about three inches in about nine days of spate and two hundred yards of it had gone down four, and packing it back meant barrow work, and it meant stone, and stone is not free and neither is a barrow, and it took four days.

Six men, a boy of about seventeen and a cart came off their own road at the second hour on the Saturday afternoon and did not stop to explain themselves to anybody, which is a thing that happens in this county about twice a year and which nobody has ever written a rule about.

The woman of about fifty whose road it was was in the market town on the Saturday and came down at about the fifth hour to look at it and stood on the bank for a minute and a half and then said a sentence a quarter of an inch, out loud, to about nine people, and then went back up the lane to her own work.

“**A quarter of an inch,**” she said. “**That is what he said it would do. He said it at the low end of my road with a bar in his hand and I wrote it on the slate in the apron in a column I have never shown anybody and I did not show it to him and I am showing it to about nine people now because about nine people came off their own road at about the second hour and none of them was sent.**”

Nobody thanked her.

Then the boy of about seventeen said, in about nine words, why the crew had come, and it was the same reason as the last time and it was not the same reason, because the last time it was a drain on about four miles of made road and this time it was a ford on about four hundred yards of a road that about nine hundred people had used in about nine days.

“**The ford takes the crossing off the lane,**” he said. “**And the lane is the only made thing between here and the coast, and about nine carts a day are on it now instead of four, and in a week there will be more, and if the ford goes the lane goes, and then four hundred yards of made road at the low end is under water at about nine inches and nobody is measuring how deep.**”

Nobody thanked him and nobody argued and four of the six men had been on that crew in Mudmonth and about two had not.

Aren Kest went down the lane at about the fourth hour with his stick and worked the packing from about the fifth until about the ninth, which is four hours, on the off side, on his good side, with a barrow, and two of the six men watched him do it for the first ten minutes and one of them said that a man with a stick could not hold a barrow on the off side of a wet ford and would be in the water in nine minutes.

He was in the water in eleven minutes and he got out of it and did not say anything about it and carried the next four barrows.

Nobody thanked him and nobody asked him why he was doing it and the woman of about fifty did not thank him and the woman of about thirty-eight was seven days gone up the coast road.

Then he went up the lane and did not go into the market, and he went up the thirteen stairs to the room over a weigh-house, which he had not been in since about the twenty-fifth of Greenmonth, and which was open because it was a market morning.

Mara Vey was at the table with a sheet of returns in front of her and a hand going across it, and she did not get up, and she said that if he was going to stand in the doorway for the length of a conversation she would put the returns down.

“**I will put them down,**” she said, and she put them down.

He stood with his stick against the frame and half of his trousers wet to the knee and his boots making a mark on the boards of a room that had stood open with no date anybody would give for four and a half years, and he told her.

He told her in nine sentences and he did not soften them and he did not put it in the right order to make himself look better. He said that a Crown officer at a ford had put four marks in four stones at the height of a thumb and that the marks were not about the road. He said that the four marks were where the water stops, and that they are put there to be read in four years by a man who will want to know how high it came on a day somebody told him to shut a ford, and that a Crown officer who knows the day in advance does not need a column or a road at all, he needs four marks and a book.

“**So it is not a march,**” he said. “**It is a measurement, and the march is the thing that comes after the measurement, and by the time it comes every ford on this coast has been marked by a man who did not have to be told what the marks were for.**”

Then he stopped, and he had not said any of the rest of it, and the rest of it was the three clauses and the fact that not one of them was touched, and the fact that the rule nine people built in four and a half years is a rule a man going to the end of a road and saying it out loud before he shuts it.

“**You know the rest of it,**” Mara Vey said.

“**I know the rest of it.**”

“**Then why did you tell me the four marks and not the rest of it.**”

“**Because the four marks are mine,**” Aren Kest said, “**and I walked eight miles to find them and I have not walked eight miles for anything in a year.**”

Mara Vey looked at him for about as long as it takes a person to decide something, and then she asked one question, and it was the only question, and she did not ask it kindly and she did not ask it as a way of making a point.

“**How long have you had the rest of it?**”

“**Since about the eighth of Longlight.**”

“**That is three weeks and two days.**”

“**That is three weeks and two days.**”

“**And you have not been in this room since the twenty-fifth of Greenmonth and I have been in this room every day and you have not said it,**” Mara Vey said, “**and I am not asking you why and I am not going to help you with it and I am going to say one sentence and then I am going to pick the returns up again.**”

She picked the returns up.

“**You have found the load-bearing thing and you have not said it, and I have noticed, and I am not going to pretend to you that I have not, and I am not going to be the person who says it for you in a room with about nine people in it, because if I say it then nine counties have it out of a woman who keeps a room and not out of the man who found it, and in a year that will be the version, and I have seen the version about four times in this county and it is never the version that was true.**”

Nobody thanked her and Aren Kest said nothing at all, which is the thing he is good at, and four hundred paces of lane separated the room over a weigh-house from the two rooms over a saddler's and both of them were shut behind their own doors by about the ninth hour, and nobody in nine counties had any of it in a document.

**And the cost of that Saturday is a column and it is nobody's, and about nine days of spate and a quarter of an inch printed into hard ground and eleven barrows are not in it and none of them has a price.**

**a horseshoe at 11, a boot brush at 16, a whetstone at 23, a string of twine at 29, a bundle of hay at 33, a barrow wheel at 35, a sack of sand at 38, a mattock at 39, a spike bar at 40, and a padlock at 41.** Eleven, sixteen, twenty-three, twenty-nine, thirty-three, thirty-five, thirty-eight, thirty-nine, forty, and forty-one is three hundred and five pence. **A mark is forty-eight pence and three hundred and five pence is six marks and seventeen over.** The packing crew finished the near nine feet at about the sixth hour on the Wednesday and the barrow wheel came off in the wet about the fourth hour on the Tuesday, and it came off because the pin in it had gone, and the pin had gone because it was put in in Mudmonth by a different man in a hurry, and the crew put it back in with the pin they had, and the wheel came off again about an hour later, and the boy of about seventeen said that they had one barrow with two wheels on it and that this was the best that had happened all week and that somebody should make a rule about it and about nine people laughed.

Then on about the seventh hour of the Saturday, a woman of about forty-nine who keeps a house four doors up the market street came out of her door and said to the street in general that there were men on the made road, and about four hundred people in a market street looked up the lane at about the ninth hour in a rain that had stopped about an hour before, and about nine hundred men came up that lane over the following four days, and about nine people counted them, and one of them counted twice and got two figures and would not give either, and nobody in this county has a number for them to this day.
