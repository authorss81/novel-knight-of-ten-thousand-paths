# Chapter 0668

**Harvestmonth the twenty-sixth, YR 321, a Thursday and a market morning, from the fourth hour to the ninth at the market town's own gate, a channel about four feet wide with three courses of sill in lime and the middle course gone, nine households, a boy of about seventeen, a chain, a lamp, a counted sum of thirty-one pence, and a line of stone on the bank with a mallet on top of it that nobody picked up.**

The gate of the market town is where a drain takes the water off four miles of made road and goes under the road into the channel, and the culvert in it is three courses of sill laid in lime in Greenmonth by a man of about fifty who has been laying stone at that gate since he was about twenty, and the middle course is gone between two clean joints.

About four feet of silt and weed had gone into the mouth of it by the middle of Longlight. It went to three and a half in about four days, and it went on going down through Rainmonth as the weather turned, and then it began to come back, and by the fourth hour on the twenty-sixth of Harvestmonth there were about two feet of it in there again and about nine inches of that was the last rain, and the standing part of the two feet had gone hard as a floor and had to come off in pieces.

The work is not the courses. The courses are laid and two of the three are holding and the middle one is not, and there is no way to put a middle one in without taking the top one out, and nobody in nine counties is going to do that in Harvestmonth.

The work is the two feet.

Nine households at that gate put in what they could and nine people turned out and the man of about fifty was in it from the fourth hour, and the work took until the ninth, which is five hours for nine people and a boy of seventeen, and it is not a day's work, and it is two days' work if anybody is paid, and nobody is paid and nobody said anything about being paid.

He had the heel of his hand burned about twice a year and did not mention it, and he burned it again on the twenty-sixth of Harvestmonth and still did not mention it, and four people at the culvert saw him take the hand out of the water, stand at the bank for a minute and a half, and go back in, and five did not.

The cold was the kind that gets into a wrist and stays there. There was a chain on a bar going through the mouth of the culvert and nine people took it in turns, and four of them could not lift it at the second pull and five could, and nobody arranged the turns and the turns arranged themselves in nine minutes the way they do.

A boy of about seventeen worked the bucket end and put the silt into a barrow, and the barrow went up the bank and about nine paces and tipped, and that is a hundred and forty times in an afternoon if the barrow holds, and the barrow held.

The woman of about fifty, whose four miles and whose ford are four miles down this same lane, came down at about the fifth hour and stood on the high bank and looked at it for a minute and a half and did not come down into it, and four people at the culvert said good morning and five said nothing.

“**Two feet,**” she said, to nine people, and she said it the way she says figures, which is that a figure is a figure. “**In the middle of Longlight it was four, and at the gate it went to three and a half in four days, and now it is two again and it has not stopped raining since Friday. That is three lots of silt in nine weeks and this is the first time anybody has been in this culvert since it was laid, and I am not blaming anybody and I want it said that I am not.**”

“**It is a drain,**” the man of about fifty said, from the water, without turning round. “**It takes the water off four miles of made road and puts it in a channel. A drain is not a cistern. It is going to fill and it is going to be emptied, and if it is full when the water comes then the water is on the road, and that is the whole of what it is for.**”

“**Then how often does somebody clear it?**” the boy of about seventeen said, from the bucket end with his arms full.

The man of about fifty stopped, and thought about it, and four people at the culvert watched a mason work out a figure he could not work out, and five did not.

“**Every year,**” he said. “**Twice in a bad one. And there is nobody in this county whose job it is, and there is nobody in this county I could tell, and I have been laying stone at that gate since I was about twenty and I have cleared it nine times in forty years and every one of those nine was out of my own pocket in about four hours and I told nobody, and I am telling nine people now because there is a boy of about seventeen on the bucket end and he is not going to be forty when this road floods.**”

Nobody thanked him and four people at the culvert looked at the boy of about seventeen and five looked at the water.

Then the chain came out with the last of it, at about the sixth hour, and the man of about fifty got down into the mouth of the culvert on his hands and knees with a lamp in his other hand and went in about four feet, and came out again, and wiped his face, and said the thing that nine people had been waiting nine weeks to hear and one of whom had a question about.

“**The middle course is gone. It is not cracked and it is not shifted. It is not there, between two clean joints, and the joint above it and the joint below it are the best two joints I have ever laid and I have laid nine thousand feet and I know what a clean joint looks like at a year old.**”

“**Then somebody took it out,**” a man of about thirty-eight said, from the bank.

“**Nobody took it out,**” the man of about fifty said. “**There is no tool in this county that lifts a sill course clean out of a bed in four feet without taking the top one with it, and I have watched four men try it and I would not let any of them. A sill is bedded on lime and lime does not let go of a stone in nine months, it lets go of it in about four years, and in four years this thing is under four feet of made road and nobody will ever see it again.**”

“**So what was it, then?**” the boy of about seventeen said.

“**It is a course of stone laid in lime by a man in a hurry in a wet month, about nine weeks ago,**” the man of about fifty said, “**and the lime under it was green and the ground came up under it and the whole course lifted about half an inch, all of it, together, and then the water got under it. And that is not a fault. That is a wet month. I laid it and I knew the month and I laid it because there was a hole in the gate road and a hole in the gate road in a wet month is a hole that drowns a cart.**”

Nobody said anything, and four people at the culvert understood the whole of that and five understood that a man had said a thing about himself in a market-street voice at the bottom of a drain.

“**Then lay it again,**” the man of about thirty-eight said. “**You have got nine people here and a chain and a lamp.**”

“**I have got nine people, a chain, a lamp, and no lime that has been through a winter,**” the man of about fifty said. “**And there is a line of stone on the bank outside this gate with a mallet on top of it, and it is the stone for the fourth course, and it is the fourth course because I laid three and then stopped, and I stopped because three courses and a bed is what a lime bed will take in that ground. A fourth would go on lime that has not been through a winter and it would come up in about four months and take the third with it, and then there is a hole in the gate road that nobody laid and the hole is mine, because I put a course in on the strength of a morning.**”

“**So the line of stone is for nothing,**” the man of about thirty-eight said.

“**The line of stone is a course and not a lie,**” the man of about fifty said, and got back into the water. “**I have said that about four times this year and I will say it four times next year, and in four years one of us in this county will be old enough to want to put it in, and if I am not here then somebody will have to have told him why it is there, and that is why it is on the bank and not in a barrow, and that is the whole of my business and it is not a small one and I have nobody to hand it to.**”

Nobody thanked him and the boy of about seventeen looked at the line of stone on the bank for a while and then went back to the barrow without saying anything, and four people at the culvert noticed that he had looked at it and five did not.

The badge-man turned up at about the fifth hour and did not say why, and got a bucket at about the sixth, and carried silt for about an hour, and was not any good at it, and four people at the culvert worked out in nine seconds that a man of fifty-eight with a badge and a chain of office and nine pence a week was standing in a drain on a Thursday morning, and five people at the culvert had known it on sight and four of those five had not said anything about it either.

He filled the bucket and carried it up the bank and tipped it and came back for the next one and did it eleven times, and on the ninth one he put about half of it down his coat, and a woman of about forty-nine who keeps a house four doors up the market street said, in the ordinary way, that it was not his job.

“**It is not my job,**” the badge-man said, and filled the bucket again. “**I have a badge and no head and nine pence a week and thirty-one years of standing in streets, and this is the first physical thing I have done in a year, and I am not going to be told about my job by a woman with a barrow wheel, and I am not going to be thanked for it either, so you had better not start.**”

Nobody said thank you and four people at the culvert said the other half of what the badge-man had said, which is that he was right, and five said nothing.

Then the sum was counted, at the boards, in the open, at about the seventh hour, in about four minutes, and the man of about fifty counted it because counting is what he does and because anybody else would have made a figure of it.

Nine households at that gate. **The two at the low end of the lane gave elevenpence each,** and the man of about fifty said out loud that those were the two whose ground was under the water every time the culvert choked, and that anybody who wanted to argue with the figure could come and stand in the channel. The three behind the weigh-house gave twopence each. The four that keep the two yards either side of the gate gave a penny each, and one of those four gave nothing and said so, out loud, in front of nine people, at the boards, in the middle of the counting.

“**I have had a bad summer and I am not going to pretend otherwise, and I would rather say it here than have nine people think it,**” he said. He was about thirty-one. “**And I have been at this gate every Friday since the middle of Rainmonth for about nine weeks and nobody has asked me for anything, and I am not going to start by handing over a penny.**”

Nobody argued with him and four people at the boards said nothing and five said something, and nine people watched a man of about thirty-one say in a market street that he would not give a penny, and nobody thanked him for saying it either, and the man of about fifty counted it as nothing and wrote nothing beside it and moved on, and four people at the boards noticed that he had not written anything beside it and five did not.

**Twenty-two, six and three is thirty-one pence.** It cost about three days of a skilled man's work at eight pence a day, and it bought four inches of depth in a culvert at the ninth hour, and the man of about fifty said that out loud at the boards in nine words and said that he was not complaining and that it was the truth, and that nine people would find it useful in about a year.

Nobody thanked him.

Then a man of about thirty-four with a sheet in his coat asked the badge-man a question, in the open, at about the eighth hour, and it was a question that four people at the boards had wanted to ask since Greenmonth and five had not known they wanted to ask it.

“**There is a line of stone on the bank with a mallet on top of it.**”

“**There is,**” the badge-man said.

“**It has been there since Greenmonth.**”

“**It has.**”

“**You are the man with the badge in this county.**”

“**I am,**” the badge-man said, and then he said the rest of it in nine words, and nine people at the boards heard it, four understood it, and five heard a man decline to do something.

“**I asked the man of about fifty about that line of stone once, in Greenmonth, in a lane, and he gave me a reason about lime and about a winter, and the reason was good, and I did not understand half of it and I have not asked him since, and I am not going to ask him in a market street in front of nine people, because a man who asks a tradesman to explain his trade in a street is doing it to be seen asking, and four of the nine of you would be watching me do it.**”

Nobody said anything to that and four people at the boards looked at the line of stone and five looked at the water.

Aren Kest was on the bucket end from about the fifth hour, and the bucket end is the worst end and everybody in that market town knows it, and he was on it because the boy of about seventeen had been on it since the fourth hour and had the look of a person about to be sick of a thing.

He had not been the man anybody could be sent for for about two hours, and he had had ninety barrows, and the thing he turned over for those two hours was not the instrument and was not the ford and was not a boy of about twenty-two, and it was a sentence the man of about fifty had said about a boy not going to be forty when the road floods.

He had learned that in nine seconds, standing in nine inches of water at the fourth hour of a Thursday, and it was the first thing anybody in nine counties had put in front of him in about a year that he had not already had.

At about the seventh hour the woman of about fifty came down the bank and stood at the water's edge and said one thing to him, and nine people were within hearing and four of them were listening.

“**Tovan Kest put a stone on the end of that baulm on the eighth of Longlight so that anybody could see where a mistake had been,**” she said, “**and there has not been one, and the baulm is still there under a foot of water at the low end of my road, and I have walked past it nine times since and I have not once thought about the stone. And there is a line of stone on the bank at that gate with a mallet on it, and the difference is that the man who laid that line down is standing in the water now, and I would like that said somewhere, and I would like it said by somebody who will not get a piece of paper out of a drawer in four years and read it.**”

Nobody thanked her and Aren Kest said, in nine words, that he would not have a piece of paper, and that he would say it out loud in that market street on a first of a month, which is a thing a hearing can do, and that it would be about four weeks, and that a hearing cannot say a sheet is false and cannot make anybody do anything, and that the whole of what it could do was put a sentence into nine people's mouths once a month, and that he had thought about it for four seconds and had decided to do it anyway.

She said that four weeks was a long time and that he should say it sooner if he could, and he said that he would not be able to, and she said that she had not asked, and he said that he knew she had not.

Nobody thanked anybody.

**And the cost of that Thursday is a column and it is nobody's, and about two feet of silt and thirty-one pence and a line of stone on a bank that nobody has picked up are not in it and none of them has a price.**

**a sill stone at 22, a bushel of lime at 25, a lantern at 27, a second chain at 28, a shovel at 29, a bucket at 30, a mason's rule at 31, a second bundle of laths at 32, a sack of sand at 36, and a hod at 40.** Twenty-two, twenty-five, twenty-seven, twenty-eight, twenty-nine, thirty, thirty-one, thirty-two, thirty-six and forty is three hundred pence. **A mark is forty-eight pence and three hundred pence is six marks and twelve over.** The water went under the gate road into the channel at about the ninth hour, in front of nine people, at four inches deep and moving, and four of them watched it and five watched a man of about fifty put his hand on the top course of three and say that it would do, which is the second time this year he has said that and the fourth time in nine years, and nine people had been in that market that morning and four had not said anything about it, and five of them said it was a shame about the stone.

Then the mallet was still on the stone on the bank at about the ninth hour, and it was still there on the following Thursday, and it was still there on the first of a month, and nine people who went out to look at it in those four days did not pick it up, and five of them could not say afterwards why they had gone out to look, and nobody in nine counties has ever written down what a line of stone on a bank is for, and four hundred yards up a made road a yard was taking timber off a waggon and nine men were loading a sill, and nobody in that yard had heard about the money or the silt or the boy on the bucket end.
