# Chapter 0658

**Longlight the twenty-fifth, YR 321, a Saturday and a market morning, a wagon going out of the market town at about the fifth hour with four people and nine hundredweight on it, a barrow from a yard two days off, and a man with a badge who is asked to send a rider and says no.**

It is about three weeks into the wet in a year that had been dry until Greenmonth, and the four miles between the market town and the ferry stone had stopped being a road in the ordinary sense and had become about nine hundred yards of standing water with a made surface under it somewhere, and the loaded carts on that lane had gone from about four a day to about one, and the one that was going was going empty.

The wagon was not empty. It was the delegation's, which had been a cart and was not a cart now, because the carter who had brought it eleven days up the coast road had taken it back to where it came from and there was another carter at the market town on the Saturday morning with a wagon and about four wheels and a boy of about nineteen who knew the lane and had come for the work.

A wagon on that road on that morning cost a figure and the boy had said the figure out loud in the street without being asked, because he was about nineteen and it was the only interesting thing about the morning.

“**Eleven marks for the four days and the ford,**” the boy said, “**and that is with the wagon back, and if the water comes up on Thursday it is fourteen, and if it comes up on Thursday and stands, then you are not getting it back at all and I have told my father so and my father has told the wagon's owner so.**”

Nobody asked the wagon's owner and the wagon's owner was not in the street, and about four people at the boards said out loud that eleven marks was about four months of a wage and about five said that it was less than that and both of those were correct.

Salla Dunnock did the arithmetic out loud in about nine seconds and it was not the arithmetic of the wagon.

“**About nine days of all four of our wages,**” she said, “**for four of us, going back. I have about two months of the delegation's money left and this takes a third of it, and I am going to say in this street that I have been in this town nine days and I have not spent anything on a bed and I have not spent anything on food except four days of it, and I am going back with a third of what I was sent with and the rest of it is because there was no need and there was no need because there is nowhere in this county for a woman with a bundle to sleep that is not a barn.**”

“**Then take less of it,**” the badge-man said. “**I have a badge and no head and thirty-one years and about nine pence a week for the work of standing in a street, and I am not going to tell a delegation how to spend the money of a coast of nine hundred people, and neither is anybody else in this street.**”

Nobody thanked him and Salla Dunnock said that she was not asking, and that she had said it out loud because a person who has said a figure out loud in a street cannot afterwards be told that somebody else thought of it.

Tovan Kest came down the lane at about the fourth hour with a barrow and a length of board and about four men and no wagon, and did it because the ford at the low end had a false baulm on it that a man of about forty-four had told him about on the Thursday, and because the ford was the one place on the four miles where a wagon in spate would be in trouble, and because Tovan Kest had been hired for nine days by a road office that does not pay for work it has not ordered and had now been on that job for about twenty-one days, which was twelve days longer than it should have been.

Nobody thanked him for the barrow and the boy of about nineteen looked at the barrow and then looked at the four men and did not say anything about the count, which is what a boy of about nineteen does when he has been beaten twice in a market street by being wrong in front of a carter.

The four days were ordinary and nobody has asked for them to be written down, and about nine people have lived them, and the only thing anybody kept of them is a boy of about nineteen with a nose the colour of a ford.

They went up at about the fifth hour. The woman of about sixty went in the wagon and was asleep inside it before it was a quarter of a mile up the lane. The boy of about nine went at the front of the wagon with his boots on, which he had not earned, and which the woman of about sixty said nothing about because she had said it once and the boy had heard her.

Aren Kest walked with them as far as the ford and no further and he did not offer to go further and he was not asked to.

At the ford the wagon took about nine minutes to cross in spate, and it would have taken half as long again if Tovan Kest had not put two boards across the approach on the hard side and because the wagon's off-side wheels were on the two boards and the near wheels were not, which is the whole of it.

The wagon went up the far side and stopped, and Salla Dunnock got down and came back about nine paces and said the thing she had come to say, which she said on the bank and not in a road, because there was nobody else in the road.

“**You still have not told me which of the two,**” she said, “**and I am going to tell you that it does not matter, and that I have worked out why, and I am going to tell you why in about nine words and then I am going to get back in the wagon.**”

He waited.

“**Because if you tell me to fight, then I am a person who has been told what to do about her own road by a man she met twenty-nine days ago, and if you tell me to stand still, then I am a person who has been told what to do about her own road by a man she met twenty-nine days ago,**” Salla Dunnock said. “**Either way it is a man and not a road. And I came eleven days to find out whether there was a third thing and there is, and it is that the road is not shut and it is not going to be shut by a piece of paper, and nobody has asked me anything and the not-asking is the part that is doing it, and I am going to go back and say so, and I am not going to say I learned it here.**”

“**Then what did you learn here,**” Aren Kest said.

“**That a woman of about twenty-nine keeps a room that has stood open for four and a half years with nothing in it and has not shut it,**” Salla Dunnock said, “**and that a man with a badge stood in a street for a year with about nine people and had no head and would not make one, and that a copyist of about twenty-six would not put a paper in a drawer to be tidy. That is all I learned here and it is worth the eleven days and I am not going to write any of it down because if I write it down it becomes an account of your county and it is not mine and I have nine hundred people who will want to know what it was and not what I thought about it.**”

She got back in the wagon. About nine people at the ford and about four at the market town behind them watched a wagon with four people in it go up four miles of bad road in spate, and about five of them understood the whole of what had been said on the bank and about four understood none of it.

Then the badge-man was asked to send a rider.

It was not Aren Kest who asked him, and the badge-man noticed that it was not, and said so out loud to nobody before anybody had asked him anything. “**It was the man of about forty-nine who keeps a road four days below, who had come up in the morning for the market and had been in the street at about the fourth hour.**”

“**Then send a rider with them,**” he said. “**A rider would go in a day instead of four. I have a boy who would do it and he is about sixteen and he would want four pence and I would pay the four pence myself, and I am not asking the badge. I am asking the man.**”

The badge-man said no, and he said it in about nine words, and he said it standing at the low end of the boards with about nine people in the street.

“**No, and I am not going to do it in a way that lets anybody put a better offer on the table, so I am going to say all of it now. If I send a rider on that road then I have put a boy of sixteen on a road that a Crown officer has been marking with four marks for about four days, and I have done it on the strength of nine people standing in a market street, and none of my three clauses lets anybody refuse that road and none of them lets anybody protect a person on it. A rule about shutting a road is no use to a boy on a wagon road and I will not pretend it is.**”

“**But you said nobody could go,**” the man said.

“**Nobody can go and that is what I am saying,**” the badge-man said, “**and I have been saying it for about nine days and I have been wrong about when it would matter and right about that it would. The three clauses are a man who goes to the end of a road before he shuts it, and a witness standing on it, and the burden being his. None of the three is a man who sends a boy along one. And I have no answer to the second and I am not going to build one in a street at about the sixth hour on a Saturday.**”

Nobody argued with him and about four people agreed with him and about five did not and none of them said so, and one of the five was a man of about forty-four who keeps a road in the waterworks country and who had stood his own road down for two days on the Monday and had said out loud that he did not know what else there was to do with a sheet in his hand, and who said now that the badge-man's no was the correct answer and that he would like it noted that the same man had stood down four men on a Monday without asking anybody, and that he did not think anybody ought to be told that by him and had said it anyway.

“**I would rather you said it,**” the badge-man said, “**than that you had worked it out and said nothing.**”

Nobody said anything about that.

Tovan Kest took the barrow back down the lane at about the sixth hour with the two boards still across the ford and his four men with him, and he said to Aren Kest, on the way, in about nine words and without stopping, that the false baulm had held a wagon in spate and had held about nine carts after it and that a baulm is a baulm and that somebody would have to come back and look at it in about nine days and that he was not going to be the one, because he had a job at the low end of the waterworks country that somebody had paid him for, and that he would leave the two boards and the wedge and a stone on the end of it, and that the stone was so that anybody could see where his mistake had been and that this time it would not be a mistake.

**And the cost of that Saturday is a column and it is nobody's, and about eleven marks for four days of a wagon and about two boards and a wedge and a stone on the end of them are not in it and none of them has a price.**

**a quill at 9, a box of chalk at 14, a coil of chalk line at 20, a string of twine at 27, a bundle of straw at 31, a hand shovel at 34, a tarpaulin at 37, a hurdle at 39, a water butt at 40, and a kettle at 41.** Nine, fourteen, twenty, twenty-seven, thirty-one, thirty-four, thirty-seven, thirty-nine, forty, and forty-one is two hundred and ninety-two pence. **A mark is forty-eight pence and two hundred and ninety-two pence is six marks and four over.** The wagon went up through the ferry stone at about the eighth hour with the water up nine inches on the ford and a foot and a half at the low side of the crossing, and about nine people at the market town watched it go until it was a shape at the top of the lane, and the badge-man stood at the low end of the boards with his hands behind his back for about as long as it took to lose it, and then a clerk of the Crown's road office came across the street with a small book and asked the badge-man whether he knew the names of the four people who had gone out on that road, and the badge-man said that he knew one of them and that she had given her name in a street about nine days ago and that it was Salla Dunnock, and that he was not going to give him the other three because the other three had not given their names to anybody, which is a thing that is entirely possible and had happened.

“**And why are you writing it down?**” the badge-man asked him, and about four people in that street turned round at the question, because in about nine counties nobody ever asks a Crown clerk that in a market street. The clerk said, in about nine words and without any harm in it at all, that he was writing it down because there was a column on the page headed with the date, and that in about four days there was going to be a person in this county who was going to be asked how many persons and how many carts had gone out of it on the coast road on the twenty-fifth of Longlight, and that he had been a clerk in this office for six years, and that he did not know who would be asked, and that a person who is asked a road he does not keep should be able to say what was on it.

“**Who asked you to keep it?**”

“**Nobody has asked me yet,**” the clerk said. “**I have a book with a column in it.**”

And then he shut the book and went off down the lane to get his cart, and the badge-man stood at the low end of the boards and watched him go and said nothing at all, and about four people in that street had heard the exchange and about nine had not, and three of the four went home and thought a book with a column in it and did not know what to do with the thinking and did not do anything with it.

Then the badge-man turned round to the boards and said the standing notice out loud at about the seventh hour of a Saturday market evening, to about nine people, most of whom had heard it about four times, and said that he was going to say it and that nobody had to stop him.
