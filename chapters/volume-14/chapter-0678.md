# Chapter 0678

**Embermonth the sixteenth, YR 321, a Friday and not a market morning with the room over the weigh-house shut behind its own door, a lane in a county two days off the market town at about the ninth hour in the rain, about nine households on it, and one woman saying no at her own gate before anybody has asked her.**

The question had been asked on the Tuesday of Rainmonth and it had never been answered, and both of those facts were on the page in a market street in a county two days off this one, in a market town four miles and a lane away from here.

The question was this. Whether a Crown officer has the right to ask a person at a gate which place or road a paper of the Crown is meant.

The copyist of about twenty-six had said she did not know, and she had said it as a person and not as a keeper, and she had told the truth, which was that she had a quarter of a sheet on a board in her town and about nine pages of nothing at all and that there was nobody in nine counties she could put a woman in front of.

The woman who asked it walked two days in the wet to ask it. That is on the page as well, and her name is not on anything and her age is about thirty-nine and she keeps a house about nine hundred yards down a lane in a county two days off the market town, and nine households are on that lane.

Aren Kest came down the made road on the Wednesday with his stick and got as far as the lane and then walked the whole of it on foot because there was no cart on it, and he got to the ninth gate at about the fourth hour on the Friday and stood at the low side of it as long as it takes a carter to get a load round.

There is no cart on that lane and there has not been a cart on it since about the year his mother was at it, which he did not say to anybody and which was the sort of thing that came out of a lane like that once every four or five years and never twice in the same county.

Nine households on nine gates. A house at the first that keeps a dog. A house at the third where nine people have come and gone since Longlight and nobody knows why and nobody has asked, which is the ordinary arrangement on a lane. A house at the fourth with a woman of about sixty in it who has been at the boards in the market town four times in a year and has never once said a hard true thing, and four people on that lane have worked out why, and five have not.

Nobody at the ninth gate knew that the man with the stick was there at all, and nine people in that lane went on with their Friday while he stood at the low side of it, and four of them knew what he was standing for and five thought he was resting, which is what a man with a limp is to nine people out of ten.

What he was doing was not waiting for anybody. He had worked out on the Wednesday, four miles back, that the only thing he could usefully do with a Friday on a lane like that was to be on it, and that being on it was worth nothing and that he was going to do it anyway, and that a man who has written nine hundred words that a woman of about thirty-four walked nine days to take apart does not get to go and find somebody else to be wrong in front of.

Nobody in that county knew he was there and four of the nine households on that lane had worked out that a man with a stick who was not asking anybody anything was on the lane, and about five had not.

The house at the ninth gate belonged to the woman of about thirty-nine and there were four people in it, and the gate is a five-barred thing about four feet high with a latch that lifts, and it has been lifted and put down about nine thousand times.

Four hundred yards down the lane, at the second gate, a man of about thirty-one in a livery this county had not seen since the middle of Rainmonth was standing with about nine feet of deal in his hand and a stick of chalk in the other, and about nine people in that lane could see him, and about four of those nine had seen him before.

He was not the man who marked the ford. He said so at the second gate before anybody asked him, in the ordinary way, and four people in that lane took that in and about five did not.

He put a board on the inside of the second gate in about four minutes and read out what he had written, which was the county, the gate described by the two elms and the pond and the field, the day, his own name in full, and one line saying that he asked which place or road was meant and that the person at the gate said she had not been told.

Then he read it back to the woman of about thirty-eight who keeps that gate, and she said it was right, and he said that it was very good of her, and nine people on that lane heard the whole of it and four of them understood what the second clause of a sheet does to a gate and about five heard a man being pleasant to a woman.

He stopped at the fifth gate a good deal longer than he stopped at the others, and four people on that lane noticed that and five did not, and the reason was that the person at the fifth gate was a man of about seventy who took about four minutes to get to the gate and about four minutes to get back from it, and the man of about thirty-one stood at the high side of that gate and waited for all of it and did not knock twice.

The man of about seventy said the only thing anybody said to him at any gate on that lane that morning, and he said it to the man and not to the lane, and it was nine words.

“**I have kept this gate since before your father and I have never been told one thing by anybody in a coat and I am not going to start at seventy.**”

The man of about thirty-one said that he was sorry and that he would read it back and that he had taught in about nine counties that the difference between what was said and what was meant was the whole of the trade, and four people at the fifth gate heard both halves of that and about five heard a man be polite.

Then he went on down the lane.

Nobody thanked him and four people in that lane had watched a man put a board on the inside of a gate and go on to the next one and five had watched a man say that it was very good of her.

The woman of about thirty-eight who keeps the second gate came out about nine paces down the lane after him and stood there until he was out of the rain, which he was not, and then went back in.

Aren Kest said one thing to her, out loud, in a lane, and nine people on that lane heard it and about four understood it.

“**You said you had not been told.**”

“**I have not been told,**” the woman of about thirty-eight said. “**And he said it was very good of me before I had got as far as saying anything, which I have thought about every day since and I have not got past it and I am not going to get past it today.**”

“**You will,**” Aren Kest said. “**Not today.**”

Nobody thanked him and four people in that lane had heard a man tell a woman that she would get past it and five had heard a man with a stick say a thing about a day.

He got to the fifth gate at about the eighth hour and the sixth at about the eighth hour and the seventh at about the ninth hour, and at each one it took about four minutes, and at the eighth gate the person at the gate said that he had not been told, and at the ninth gate there was nobody at all and he stood at the high side of it for about a minute and a half in the rain and then went on up the lane.

Aren Kest was at the low side of the ninth gate and about nine paces off when the man of about thirty-one came up the lane with the deal under his arm and the chalk in his hand, and he was four feet from the gate and about four seconds from asking.

Then the gate lifted and came out about nine inches and stopped, and a woman of about thirty-nine came out of it in the rain and stood behind it, and she said it.

“**Before you ask. Do you have the right?**”

Nobody said anything on that lane.

The man of about thirty-one stopped. He did not say anything for as long as it takes to turn a cart and four people on that lane counted it and about five did not.

Then he took the small book out of his coat, and he did not open it, and he said the true thing about it out loud, in the rain, on a lane, to nine people.

“**Then that is the whole of the answer and I have got nothing to put in it,**” he said. “**I ask which place or road is meant. I have not asked. I have got nine gates on this lane with a date on them and a line on each one and about nine households that have not been told anything, and you have just said no before I got to the gate, and there is no line on my page for a person who has said no to a question nobody asked.**”

“**Then write one,**” a man of about thirty-eight said, from the low side of the lane, where he had been standing since the fifth hour.

The man of about thirty-one said that he could not write one and he gave the reason in nine words and he gave it in the ordinary way, and nine people on that lane heard all of it.

“**I write what was said and not what was meant, and what was said was no, and there was no question, and if I write that a person at the ninth gate said no then in about four years a man in a room will read it as a house that will not give an answer, and it will not be a lie, and there will not be one word in it that a person can get at.**”

Nobody thanked him and four people on that lane had heard the whole of that and about five had heard a man be careful about a book.

Then the woman of about thirty-nine said the rest of it, out loud, in the rain, from behind her own gate, and nine people on that lane heard it and it is the thing this block had to have on the page.

“**I asked the question, though,**” she said. “**I walked two days in the wet to ask whether you have the right, and nobody in nine counties could tell me, and I have had about nine weeks of not being able to put it down. And here is the answer and it took me two days and it took the copyist one sentence and it took nobody anything at all. You have the right. You are going to ask. And the answer is no, and I have said it before you asked it, and you can go and write that down in whatever book you have got and read it back to me, and I will stand here in the rain while you do it.**”

Nobody thanked anybody and the man of about thirty-one said that he would read it back and that he was sorry about having to come up a lane nine times in about four weeks, and he said it in the ordinary way and about four people on that lane heard a man apologise and about five heard a man say a thing he was taught.

He did not write it in the book. He said that he was going to write it on the back of the page for the ninth gate and not in the small book, and that it would be under the one for the ninth gate where a person could find it, and four people on that lane understood that and about five did not.

Then he went on up the lane with the deal under his arm, and nine people on that lane watched his back, and the lane went up for another four hundred yards and there was one more gate at the top of it.

Then the man of about thirty-eight came the rest of the way down the lane and stood at the low side of the ninth gate and asked her one question, and he asked it in the ordinary way and he asked it once.

“**Will you say it again.**”

“**No,**” she said. “**I have said it once and there are four people in my house and they heard it and there are nine people on this lane and they heard it and that is the number of times a thing has to be said in a country with no head in it.**”

“**I am not asking you to say it again,**” the man of about thirty-eight said. “**I am asking whether you would say it if he comes back on the fourteenth of Rainmonth, which is what he said, and I would like to know now so that I can shut my gate before then instead of standing in the rain.**”

Nobody said anything and the woman of about thirty-nine looked at him for about as long as it takes to turn a cart.

“**I do not know,**” she said. “**I have said it once and it came out of me before I had got as far as being asked and I cannot promise the same thing twice. What I can promise you is that you will be able to stand in your own gateway on the fourteenth and know that somebody else on this lane said it first, and that is not nothing and it is not much.**”

Nobody thanked anybody and the man of about thirty-eight went back up the lane and shut his gate at about the tenth hour and it was shut, and four people on that lane noticed that it was shut and about five did not.

Nobody thanked her and the woman of about thirty-nine stood behind her own gate in the rain for about as long as it takes a carter to get a load round, and the four people in her house had come to the door and four of the households on that lane had shut, and the rain came off four hundred yards of made road above the lane and went across it and down the ditch, and she said one last thing out loud to nobody at all, and it was not for the man on the lane.

“**I have said it out loud and nine people heard me, and that is the whole of what nine counties has and it is one sentence at one gate, and it is mine now, and there is nobody in nine counties I can hand it to, and nine people on this lane are going to come to my door.**”

**And the cost of that Friday is one sentence said at one gate before anybody asked it, and about nine households on a lane two days off who will be asked at their own gates on the same morning by a man who does not need to knock, are not in it and none of them has a price.**

**a bar of iron at 23, a gate socket at 24, a dozen of hobnails at 25, a hoop for a pail at 28, a wicket at 29, a bit of chalk at 32, a pound of twine at 34, a rush basket at 35, a hanglock at 36, and a shutter bolt at 37.** Twenty-three, twenty-four, twenty-five, twenty-eight, twenty-nine, thirty-two, thirty-four, thirty-five, thirty-six and thirty-seven is three hundred and three pence. **A mark is forty-eight pence and three hundred and three pence is six marks and fifteen over.** The man of about thirty-one got to the gate at the top of the lane at about the ninth hour and about the tenth hour and about nine people on that lane had heard everything that had been said at the ninth gate and nobody in the top house had heard any of it, and he put a board on the inside of that gate in about four minutes and read out the county, the gate, the day, his own name and the line, and then he came back down the lane in the rain and did not go to the ninth gate again and did not look at it, and nine people watched a man of about thirty-one walk past a gate where a woman of about thirty-nine had said no to him before he got to it.
