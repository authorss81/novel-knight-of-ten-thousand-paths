# Chapter 0683

**Wolfmonth the sixteenth, YR 321, a Sunday and not a market morning, with the room over the weigh-house shut behind its own door, a copyist of about twenty-six ruling a tenth question in nine hands of minutes, and a man of about fifty-eight with a badge and no head objecting to it at a door and being refused with perfect standing.**

The room over the weigh-house is shut on every day that is not a market morning, and it had been shut since the middle of the afternoon on Saturday, and nobody in nine counties has ever found out by whom and nobody has asked.

It is one room with a door on a lane, and the deed of it is thirteen words and it costs fourpence a year, and the weigh-house under it stops weighing at noon on a Saturday and starts again at the seventh hour on a Monday, and the room is shut while the weigh-house is shut, and that is the whole of the reason and it is the correct one.

The copyist of about twenty-six came down the thirteen stairs at about the ninth hour on the Sunday morning and unlocked the door and did not open it, and then opened it, and stood in the lane while it stood open and let the cold off a room that had been shut since Saturday afternoon.

Nobody thanked her and four people in that lane had seen her come down and about five had not, and the lane is a lane and a person in a lane on a Sunday is a person in the way.

The deed of that room is thirteen words and costs fourpence a year, and fourpence has been paid in every year for four and a half years out of a box in the weigh-house under it, and nobody in nine counties has ever queried it, and nobody in nine counties has ever queried a great many things about that room, and about four people in nine counties have said out loud that fourpence a year is the price of a room and about five have said that it is the price of being the person a thing arrives for.

Nobody thanked anybody and four people in nine counties had said both of those things and about five had said one.

There is a press in that room that stands open. It has stood open for four and a half years and it has never been dated and no person in nine counties has ever given a date for it, and about four people have said the date is the first of Longlight and about five have said the date is the day the room was taken and neither of those is a date anybody can put on a thing.

There is a register on the table under the window. It has nine questions in it. There is no finding under any of the nine, and there has not been since about the middle of Harvestmonth, and above the ninth question there is a fourth ruled head over a place where a finding goes, and that head is empty, and it has been empty for about ten months, and the copyist has said out loud, twice, in two different months, in two different rooms, that she is not going to rule a fifth head and that neither is anybody else.

Nobody thanked her for that and four people in nine counties have repeated it back to each other and about five have said that a rule about what a keeper of records will not do is a head with a different name on it.

There is a day-book on the table beside the register with ten entries in it.

And there is a drawer about eleven inches deep under the counter, and it is shut, and there are about nine leaves in it that have no head over them, and there are about four hundred returns a month that come into this room in a good month, and nobody in this room takes a paper out of that drawer to count what is in it and nobody ever has.

Nobody thanked anybody and four people in nine counties know that the drawer is not counted and about five think that it is counted in the winter.

The copyist put the register down flat on the table and squared it against the edge with two fingers, which she does about nine times a day, and then she did the thing she had come down to do and she did it in nine hands of minutes and she did not hurry any part of it.

The first thing she did was to read the nine questions out loud to an empty room, all of them, in order, in the flat way she asks things, and nobody heard them.

The second thing she did was to put her thumb on the fourth ruled head and keep it there for as long as it takes to turn a cart, and to say out loud that it was empty, and to say out loud that she was not going to fill it and that she was not going to rule a fifth head over it, and that anybody who wanted that done could come and do it himself and would not be argued with.

The third thing she did was to take a leaf out of a second book, which is the book the questions are copied into before they go in the register, and to write on the leaf, and to stop, and to put the pen down.

Nobody thanked her and nobody in the room said anything and four people in nine counties have said since that she stopped, and about five have said that she had not stopped at all and that a woman deciding how to word a question is not the same as a woman not writing it.

Then she picked the pen up again and wrote the tenth question, and she wrote it once and she did not copy it out and she did not improve it, and it took her about four seconds and it is on the page.

“**Of a thing with no head, and of a road that will not be named: what may be said to have been decided, and by whom it may be brought to.**”

Then she wrote the day in the day-book, which took that to eleven entries, and then she ruled nothing, and the register still has a fourth head ruled over an empty place, and now there are ten questions in it, and there is no finding under any of the ten, and about nine people in nine counties know that and about five do not.

Nobody thanked her and about four people in that room, if there had been anybody in it, would have understood that a tenth question with no finding under it is exactly the same object as nine questions with no finding under them, and that the only person in nine counties who could tell the difference between this month and the month before is a woman of about twenty-six standing in a shut room on a Sunday.

Then she went and opened the door the rest of the way and stood in the lane and looked up the lane toward the market town, and about nine people in that lane could see her and about five could not, and she sent a boy of about eleven who lives at the first house with a message.

Nobody thanked the boy and he came back in about nine minutes and said that the man with the badge was at the boards and would come if he was going to come.

The badge-man came at about the eleventh hour. He came on foot and he came slowly and he had not been asked to come and he had not sent anybody, and four people in that lane noticed that he had come on a day on which he has never once come, and about five did not.

“**You have sent for me,**” he said, at the door, in the ordinary way.

“**I have,**” the copyist said. “**I have ruled a question and I want one person in nine counties to hear me say what it is before anybody else hears it, and you are that person, and I did not send for anybody else because I have not got anybody else.**”

Nobody thanked him and about four people in that lane had heard a woman say that she had sent for a man because there was nobody else, and about five had heard a woman send for a man of fifty-eight on a Sunday.

He came in and shut the door behind him, which he does not usually do, and the room has a door on a lane and the door is the only thing in it that anybody locks.

Nobody thanked him for shutting the door and he looked at the register on the table and he did not touch it, and he read the tenth question, and it took him about four seconds, and then he stood away from the table with his back against the counter and said the objection out loud, and he had thought about it on the way up the lane and nobody in nine counties could have taught it to him.

“**That is a question and it will be a finding,**” he said. “**I am not saying you will write a finding under it. I am saying that in about four years a man in a room will open that book at that page and he will read ten questions and he will read the empty head where the finding goes, and he will not be able to tell which of the two he is looking at, because a book does not say. Nine of your nine have stood empty since Harvestmonth and nobody has ever been hurt by that, because nine is a number people can hear. Ten is a number people can hear. What will hurt you is the eleventh, and the one after it, and about four years from now somebody in this county is going to want that empty head to have been empty all along, and it will be, and I want nine people to know that I said this in a shut room on a Sunday and not in a market street where it would have gone further.**”

Nobody thanked him and four people in that room had heard the whole of it and about five would have heard a man complaining about a book.

Then the copyist refused him, and she refused him with perfect standing, and she refused him in sixteen words, and about nine people in nine counties have said since that it was the best thing anybody in this county has said in about a month.

“**It will be a question because I wrote a question. That is what a question is.**”

Nobody said anything and four people in that room had heard a woman refuse a man of fifty-eight with sixteen words and about five had heard a woman say that a question is a question.

“**And I am going to say the rest of it out loud, because you came and because you asked,**” she said. “**A question in that book is a question because I have said so and because nobody in nine counties can take it out of there. If somebody in about four years reads ten questions and an empty head and decides that the empty head means a finding, then somebody in about four years has decided that about four and a half years of this county was a county that put down what it knew. And they will have decided it about a book that never once wrote down a thing it did not know, and that is a thing I can live with, and it is not a thing I can prevent by writing less.**”

Nobody thanked her and four people in that room had understood the whole of that and about five had heard a woman talk about herself in the third person in a shut room.

Nobody said anything and four people in that room had heard a woman describe a book and about five had heard a woman describe a person, and neither of those two things is a fifth head and neither of them is a finding.

“**What happens to a question nobody can answer?**”

Nobody thanked him and four people in that room had heard a man of fifty-eight ask a keeper of records a question in her own trade, and about five had heard a question.

“**It stays,**” the copyist said, “**for four and a half years, which is how long the other nine have stayed, and then somebody answers it, usually wrongly, in a market street, and about four people understand the answer and about five repeat it, and in about four years it is in a book in a room four hundred miles off with a head over it, and nobody ever comes back and checks it against the question.**”

Nobody said anything and four people in that room had heard a keeper of records describe the whole of her own work and about five had heard a woman be exact.

Then the badge-man said the thing that is true about it and he said it and he did not add to it and that is the whole of what he did in that room on that Sunday.

“**Then there is a thing in this county that is older than that head and I am not going to say what it is, and about nine people in nine counties already know and about five do not, and that is the whole of what I came for.**”

Nobody thanked him and about four people in that room looked at the table and about five looked at the door, and nobody asked him what he meant and he did not say, and about four people in that room worked out that he had moved the subject and about five worked out that he had not.

Nobody said anything for about as long as it takes to turn a cart, and then the woman of about twenty-nine who keeps that room came down the thirteen stairs with a slate in her hand and stopped at the bottom of them, and she had heard the last of it, and nobody in that room said anything to her about it.

“**Say the part about the fourth head,**” she said.

Nobody thanked her and the copyist said it in the flat way, once, without looking up.

“**The fourth head is ruled and it is empty and it has been empty for ten months. It is empty because there is no finding to put under it. It stays empty. And I am not ruling a fifth head, and neither is anybody else, and that is the whole of what I have got to say about the heads in this room.**”

Nobody said anything to that and four people in that room had heard the fourth head argued out in her own trade for the third time in three months and about five had heard it for the third time and were tired of it.

Then the woman of about twenty-nine said the other thing and she said it in her own place and she was not arguing with anybody and nobody argued with her.

“**Then I would like it recorded that I think the fourth head ought not to be there,**” she said, “**and that I have thought so since the middle of Harvestmonth, and that I keep the room and not the book, and that I have said this out loud twice in about four and a half years and both times to about four people in this room and neither time in a market street, and I am not going to say it in a market street.**”

Nobody thanked her and the copyist said no in about four seconds and gave the reason in twelve words and was not argued with.

“**Because it is not mine to rule and not yours to take.**”

Nobody said anything and four people in that room had heard a keeper of a room refuse a woman who keeps it, and about five had heard two women refuse each other politely, and the register on the table between them had ten questions in it and no finding under any of them.

Then Aren Kest came up the lane at about the fourth hour, on foot, with his stick in his right hand, and he did not knock, because the door was shut and it is shut on Sundays and he has known that for four and a half years and there is nothing in nine counties that would make him knock on a shut room.

He stood at the low side of that door for about as long as it takes to turn a cart and the copyist of about twenty-six saw him through the window from the table and did not get up and did not open the door, and the woman of about twenty-nine saw him as well and said nothing to anybody about it, and nothing between the two of them is in any document and neither of them spoke to the other and the room was shut and both doors stayed shut.

Nobody thanked anybody and four people in nine counties have said that he stood there and about five have said that he went past, and he did not go past and he did not knock, and he stood there for about four minutes and then went down the lane to the made road.

Nobody said anything about that and the badge-man, who had seen him from the window and had said nothing, said one more thing before he went, and it was about four minutes long and it was the last thing anybody said in that room that day.

“**You have ruled a question and there is no finding under it and that is the same as the other nine, and about four and a half years ago this county had nothing in that book at all and a man in a livery at a gate could write down whatever he liked. So the tenth question has not made this county worse. It has made this county a place where a man can look and find nothing, which is not the same as a place where a man can look and find nothing he can use.**”

Nobody thanked him and the copyist said one word, and it was the word no, and about four people in that room had heard a woman say no to a man of fifty-eight in a shut room on a Sunday and about five had heard nothing at all.

Nobody thanked anybody and the copyist shut the door at about the fifth hour and bolted it and went up the thirteen stairs, and the register was on the table under the window with ten questions in it and no finding under any of them and a fourth ruled head over an empty place, and the day-book had eleven entries in it, and the drawer was shut.

Nobody thanked her and about nine people in that lane saw a door close on a Sunday and about five did not.

**And the cost of that Sunday is a tenth question in a book that has had no finding under anything since the middle of Harvestmonth, four minutes of a man of thirty-two standing at a shut door, and a woman of about twenty-nine who thinks the fourth head ought not to be there and cannot get it unruled, are not in it and none of them has a price.**

**a goose quill at 4, a cake of ink at 11, a rule of boxwood at 25, a sheet of foolscap at 27, a bundle of tapes at 28, a lamp of tallow at 29, a pound of twine at 31, a horn button at 32, a set of weights at 35, and a drawer front at 39.** Four, eleven, twenty-five, twenty-seven, twenty-eight, twenty-nine, thirty-one, thirty-two, thirty-five and thirty-nine is two hundred and sixty-one pence. **A mark is forty-eight pence and two hundred and sixty-one pence is five marks and twenty-one over.** A boy of about nine came to that door at about the sixth hour in the evening with a paper in his hand about the size of a man's palm, which is a thing nobody at that door has seen in about four years, being a thing from a road and not from a parish, and the copyist of about twenty-six read it standing up in the lane, gave it back to him, and said out loud that she was not going to put a boy's paper into a room that had ten questions in it and no finding under any of them, and the boy of about nine said his mother had told him to bring it in, and she said out loud that his mother was entitled to be told no, and about four people in that lane heard a keeper of records say no to a boy of about nine and about five did not.
