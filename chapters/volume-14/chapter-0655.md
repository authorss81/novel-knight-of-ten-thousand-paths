# Chapter 0655

**Longlight the twelfth, YR 321, a Sunday and not a market morning, the second hour at the counter boards with the room over the weigh-house shut behind its own door, a printed sheet with a Crown seal and a name at the top of it, and about nine people standing in a market street reading a thing out loud that does not shut a single road.**

The clerk had put it up on the boards on the Saturday morning as the copyist had told him to, in the middle of the market where nobody could miss it, and it had been taken down on the Saturday afternoon by a man he had never seen who said he was a bailiff of a thing the clerk had never heard of, and it had been put up again on the Saturday morning after that, and it had not been taken down again.

It was a sheet about two foot by one and a half, printed on both sides, folded in four, and it was on the boards at about the second hour on a Sunday with about nine people in front of it, which was more than came to a Sunday.

There is no market on a Sunday. There were about four carts in the street and the man with the bar had been and gone. The nine people were there because the sheet was on the boards and everybody in this county now knew it was on the boards, and they had come to look at it before it came down, which is what people do about a thing they expect to be taken away.

“**The room is shut on a Sunday,**” the copyist of about twenty-six said, to about nine people who had not asked. “**It has been shut on a Sunday every Sunday since before any of you were born and it is shut now and the register is upstairs and it is not going to be brought down for a sheet. We are going to stand at the boards and read it out loud and I am going to read it out loud whether or not it is the right thing to do and I would like somebody else to tell me it is not.**”

Nobody told her it was not.

The badge-man asked for the sheet and the clerk brought it and the badge-man unfolded it at the low end of the boards with about nine people in front of him and about four behind, and he read it out in the voice he used for the standing notice, which is a voice that a person about four hundred paces away can hear, which is a thing you learn in about thirty-one years of standing in streets.

The first side was a quarter of it and the clerk had said that about nine hundred people had read it in a week and about nine hundred had not understood it and that he had read it to himself twice and had understood about half.

“**Whereas the peace and quiet of the realm require that the works, anchors and roads of Bellweather, and of every road of every kind standing in connection with them, be taken into the keeping of the Crown,**” the badge-man read, “**and stand taken from the hour of this sheet, which hour is the hour of the sealing of it, and that all and every the persons, horses, carts, goods and labour of the district wherein those works stand, are required to give such aid to the officers of the Crown as those officers shall require, at such times and in such manner as the officers shall set out, and that the keeping so taken shall be had by the marshal's officers and by no other, and shall be surrendered to none.**”

He stopped there and looked at the sheet and then looked up at the nine people.

“**That is the whole of the order,**” he said. “**There is no second order and there is nothing in a corner and I have looked. The back of it is a list of counties. The district clerk in this town said to me on Friday that there were nine pages of it and this is not nine pages of it, and I do not blame him for saying it, because he had never seen it and he was told there were nine pages, and he has been in this office for about six years and he does not print things he has not seen.**”

Then he read the back of it, which was a quarter as long again, and about half of it was the names of four counties it did not include.

And then he came back to the top of the first side and read out the four words that are at the top of it, and he read them the way a man reads a thing he has not expected to see on a page on a Sunday morning.

At the top of it, in the place where a name goes on a thing that is going to be acted on, in a hand that was not the printed hand of the body of the sheet, which had been in a press in nine hundred copies and had the same three breaks in the same three places in every copy anybody in this street had seen:

“**By the Marshal's hand. Edric Hunn.**”

The badge-man read that name out loud in a market street on the twelfth of Longlight in YR 321, and about nine people heard it, and about four of them had heard it before and about five had not.

He did not stop. It took him a second and a half to go on and anybody standing at that board could see that it cost him something, and about four people saw it and about five did not.

He finished the sheet, and he turned it about so that the nine people could see the top of it, and he said the thing about the name out loud, and he said it in about nine words, and then he did not say anything else about it for the rest of that morning.

“**I have not said that name in this street in about eleven years,**” he said, “**and I said it every day for nine years when I was a younger man and I have not said it since and I am not going to say anything else about it.**”

Then he asked whether anybody present was holding a piece of paper with three clauses on it.

Nobody answered at once and then about three people at once, which is what happens in a street, and the badge-man said that he wanted two of them out of the three and not three, and that one of the two would read the first clause and the other would read the other two together, and that he would take whoever offered first, and that he was not going to ask anybody a second time.

The woman who keeps a road in the county where a list of names was burned, and who is about thirty-six, stood up off the wall of the weigh-house where she had been standing since about the third hour, and read the first clause out loud in the street in about forty words, and she did not have it in a hand and she said so before she read it.

“**No road is shut until the man who proposes to shut it has been to the end of that road and has said it out loud, in words that road uses, in a place where somebody who works that road can hear him, and on a day that road is being used.**”

“**The last four words are mine and the reason is that a clause that can be complied with on a day the road is shut is a clause that will be complied with,**” she said, “**and I want that said out loud in a street in a month.**”

Nobody thanked her and about four people in that street said nothing and about five looked at the sheet on the boards.

Then Hester Vane read the second and the third, out loud, standing at about nine paces off with her arms down at her sides.

“**No road is shut without a witness standing on that road at the time, and the witness is named by the people who work that road and not by the man proposing to shut it, and if that witness cannot be found afterwards then the road is open. And the burden of showing that a road may be shut lies with the man who says it may be shut, and if that man cannot be named and found afterwards then nothing has been shown and the road is open.**”

“**All right,**” the badge-man said, to about nine people, in a market street, on a Sunday, at about the third hour.

“**That is the whole of the finding and I am going to say it once and I am not going to say it twice and I am not going to soften it, and then I am going to stop talking for a minute, and the nine of you can say whatever you like after that.**”

What he said was this.

“**That sheet shuts nothing. I have read it twice at about nine paces and I have had nine pairs of eyes on it and none of the nine of them shut anything. There is no road in it. It does not name one. It says the works and the anchors and the roads of Bellweather and of every road standing in connection with them, and every one of those is a thing about somebody else, and none of them is in this county, and I have read about nine hundred of these in thirty-one years and I know what a paper that takes something off a person says, and it says the thing is taken, and it says who takes it, and it says from when. This one says all three and it has never once asked what road it is going along.**”

Nobody in that street said anything for about as long as it takes a carter to get a load round.

“**So the notice does not apply because nothing is shut. The witness does not apply because nothing is shut. The burden does not apply because nobody has said that a road may be shut. I have three clauses in about nine hands and I have never once, in four and a half years, been in a room where anybody picked them up and found they did not reach, and I have been waiting for that to happen since Mudmonth of last year, and it has happened on a Sunday on a piece of paper, and it took me a year and a half to build it and about ninety seconds to find out that it does not do the one thing I wanted.**”

About four people in that street understood the whole of what he had said and about five understood some of it.

Then the badge-man turned round at the high end of the boards and asked Aren Kest whether he would stand in nine counties as the man that a warrant is put in a place for, and about nine people in the street found out at the same moment that he had been standing at the high end of it for the whole morning.

“**I am not asking you to be anybody,**” the badge-man said. “**I am asking you to be the name that goes at the top of a thing when a thing comes up that road, so that there is somebody in nine counties that it can be put in front of, and there is nobody, and I have been getting round to asking you for about nine days and this is the last morning I am going to.**”

“**No,**” Aren Kest said.

“**Say the reason,**” the badge-man said.

“**Because a name at the top of a thing is the whole of what we have been talking about for a year,**” Aren Kest said. “**A woman of about twenty-nine told that room in Mudmonth that a name in a room is a head at the top of a thing. If I put mine on it then this sheet has an address and the address is a man, and a paper with an address on it is a paper that can be delivered to, and I would rather it went to a county that has nobody in it.**”

The badge-man took the badge out and turned it over in his fingers the way a man turns a thing he has carried too long, and then he put it back inside his coat.

“**Right,**” he said. “**And I am not going to ask you again, and I want that in a street, and I am saying it now because in about a month about nine people are going to ask me whether I had offered it and I am going to have to say that I did and that you said no and that I did not ask twice.**”

Nobody said anything about that and about four people in that street looked at the ground.

Then the badge-man said the last part of it, and he said it to Salla Dunnock, who had been standing at about four paces from the boards since about the second hour with her hands in her sleeves.

“**You came eleven days to ask me whether you were to be an army,**” he said. “**I did not know then. I do not know now, and I am not going to pretend to. What I can tell you is what has happened since, which is that a piece of paper has come up that road, and it does not ask you for anything, and it does not shut anything, and it is in force from the hour it was sealed, which is a phrase that means that it has been in force for about nine days, and there are about nine hundred of them, and I have been in this work thirty-one years and I have never seen a paper that begins in the past.**”

Nobody thanked anybody.

Then the copyist said the thing about her own three series, and she said it to the copyist of about twenty-six, which is to say she said it out loud in a street to about nine people, and she did it without being asked and then said that she had done it without being asked.

“**We keep three series and none of them is made out of another,**” the copyist said. “**About nine hundred sheets a month out into nine counties. About a hundred and four in one band on a fixed day. About four hundred and nine parish rolls a month in off the roads. And I have sat here with this in front of me for a quarter of an hour and I have been waiting for myself to want to put it in one of them, and I have wanted to, and I am not going to, and I would like somebody in this street to understand that that is a whole afternoon's work in four and a half years and it has taken me about fifteen minutes to refuse.**”

“**Why?**” Salla Dunnock said.

“**Because it is not a return and it is not a roll and it is not a sheet going out to a district,**” the copyist said, “**and if I put it in one of those three then for the rest of its life somebody will take it out of a drawer and put it beside a return and say that the district told us a thing on a Tuesday and we filed it. And if I make a new one for it then there are four and there is a person in it, and the four would be in a drawer in one room in nine counties with a door on a lane and about eleven inches deep, and a paper that came from a marshal's office would then be the fourth of four series and would be kept by one woman of about twenty-nine who has said out loud about five times that she is not the person that things like that end up with.**”

“**And nobody is,**” Salla Dunnock said, “**if nobody puts them with it.**”

That got nowhere and about four people in that street could see that it had got nowhere and about five could not.

Aren Kest stood at the high end of the boards for the whole of it.

He had the finding. That is what he had and nobody else in that street had it in that form, and he had known it since about the fourth hour on the Tuesday when Tovan Kest had said the words taking the level of the run and not of the road at a ford, and he had been carrying it down four miles of bad road on a stick and up a second four miles, and he had had it in his hands at about the eighth hour the night before, in the two rooms over a saddler's, with the half leaf still face down on the table and about eleven lines of a notebook with no words in them.

A rule about shutting a road cannot be used against a man who does not shut one.

He looked at about nine people in a market street on a Sunday morning and four hundred paces of lane between the market street and the room over a weigh-house, and the woman of about twenty-nine who keeps that room was about nine paces off with her hands behind her back and had said nothing at all that morning, and he understood that she had said nothing on purpose, and it was the first thing about the arrangement he was glad of, and he was not going to say so to her and he did not.

He could have said it. He could have said it in about nine seconds and about nine people would have heard it and about four of them would have understood it and the badge-man would have looked at him and about four people in the street would have known that the man who could be sent for had finally been asked something by somebody who was not the Crown.

He did not say it, and he was asked about nothing, and about nine people in that street did not look at him at all, and the reason he did not say it is not modesty and is not the separation and is that he has been the only person in nine counties who could see the load-bearing problem in a place for about four years, and every single time the rightness of it had been the reason people had put him in charge of something, and a man who has been right four times in a room is a man whose fifth finding arrives as an order.

So he said nothing, and it cost him, and nobody noticed, and nobody thanked him, and he was not thanked for saying nothing because nobody knew that there was anything to be thanked for.

**And the cost of that Sunday is a column and it is nobody's, and about nine hundred printed sheets of a paper that is not nine pages long and a name read out once in about eleven years are not in it and none of them has a price.**

**a printed handbill at 3, a bundle of faggots at 6, a pot of tallow at 13, a hearth brush at 21, a stick of sealing wax at 27, a broadsheet at 32, a blanket of baize at 34, a seal matrix at 38, a riding hood at 40, and a great seal at 41.** Three, six, thirteen, twenty-one, twenty-seven, thirty-two, thirty-four, thirty-eight, forty, and forty-one is two hundred and fifty-five pence. **A mark is forty-eight pence and two hundred and fifty-five pence is five marks and fifteen over.** The sheet stayed on the boards through the whole of that Sunday and through the night, because about four people in this county who had never in their lives left anything about after dark on a public thing decided separately and about nine days apart to go out at about the ninth hour and look at it, and about nine of them found it there, and nobody touched it and nobody moved it and about four of them stood in front of it in the dark long enough to read the top of it twice.

Then the woman of about thirty-six, when it got late and there were about four people left, said the sentence that has no written form, out loud, in a market street, on a Sunday, to the four who were left.

“**And a road may refuse to name a witness. If a road refuses, then nothing happens to that road. The man who said the road might be shut has not shown that it may be shut, and the road is open. No road has to say out loud that it has refused, and nobody is going to be able to get inside it afterwards and find out.**”

And the badge-man said that he had asked her about four times in a year whether she wanted to say that in a room, and that she had said no four times, and that this was not a room, and that he had not asked her this time either, and that she had said it because of what was on the boards and not because of him.

And she said that it was because of what was on the boards, and that he was not to get used to it.
