# Chapter 0654

**Longlight the eighth, YR 321, a Wednesday and not a market morning, the ferry stone at the low end of a road that a woman has kept for nineteen years, about nine men in a livery this county has never seen, and a bridge carpenter from two days off who has refused to hand over a bar.**

The ford was wet, and the ferry stone at the low end of it is a block of granite about four feet high that has been standing at the edge of the water since before anybody now living was told anything about it, and it is not a ferry and there is no ferry in this part of the county and the stone is there because somebody put it there for a reason nobody has written down.

Six men were in the water at about the ninth hour and they were not crossing it, they were standing in it up to their knees with a rope between them putting a plank across on two trestles, because the made road comes to the water on the low side and the other nine yards of it are made road only in the way that a thing somebody has said is made road is.

They came down on the Tuesday from about four miles up the lane and they were not part of the delegation and they had nothing to do with the woman of about thirty-eight, and they were not in a hurry.

“**We are not putting it up,**” said the man of about thirty-one who was holding the chalk, which was not a chalk but a stick with a bit of chalk on the end of it, which is what a man uses when he is marking stone that is already in the ground. “**We are putting a gauge on it. There will be four of these between here and the milestone and I have a book and you can watch me put them in.**”

He said it to a man of about fifty who keeps a road and had come out to see what was happening at her low end, and she said that she had not asked anybody to do anything to her road and that nobody had asked, and he said that he knew that and that that was not the question, and that he was going to put four marks in her road anyway and that she could stand there and watch him do it or she could go home and read about it in a book in a week.

That is the whole of a Crown officer at a ford on a Wednesday morning, and the woman of about fifty stood and watched, and about nine people watched with her, and the man of about thirty-one marked four stones on her road in about two hours and wrote in his book after each of them, and he read out what he wrote.

“**Ferry stone, four miles to the ninth milestone, ford, bad in a wet season,**” he said. “**Crossing workable for a loaded cart, not for a herd.**”

“**That is my road written down,**” the woman of about fifty said, “**and I have been walking it for nineteen years and I could have told you all of that and you have got it off the ground in two hours off about four sticks.**”

“**Yes,**” the man said, and he did not say it unkindly, and he went back to his book.

What nobody in that party said, and what Tovan Kest worked out from about the fourth hour without being told, was that none of the four marks were about the road.

They were about the water. The man of about thirty-one had a second book, smaller, and he had come down the water side rather than the road side, and at each mark he put his hand on the stone and looked up and down the run, and at the third one he stayed a minute and a half and Tovan Kest went and stood near him and was not asked to go away.

“**You are taking the level,**” Tovan Kest said.

“**I am taking the level.**”

“**Of the run between the two marks and not of the road.**” Tovan Kest said it as though he had been thinking about it since the Tuesday. “**A road can go anywhere and a level has to be the same twice. You are not marking where the road is. You are marking where the water stops.**”

The man of about thirty-one looked up at him, and Tovan Kest was twenty-nine and had the bar in his hand and about two men behind him and a barrow with a false baulm on it that he had built himself out of two lengths of board and nine inches of nothing.

“**That is a fair reading of it,**” the man said. “**I have not written it down like that and I am not going to, because I have been told what to write down, and I am telling you what I have been told because you have a bar and I do not.**”

He did not say who had told him.

Then Tovan Kest went back to his own work, which was a length of the approach to the ford that a winter had moved four inches and that two carts had found out about in a fortnight, and which needed a false baulm and about nine days, and which he had been hired for by a road office that does not pay for work it has not ordered, on the strength of an order that had come down the district with a name at the top of it, which he had read and had not commented on.

Aren Kest came down at about the eleventh hour with his stick, having walked the lane from the market town, which is about four miles and about nine hours on a bad road, and which he had done on the strength of a carter saying at a gate four days before that there were men in the water at the ferry stone, and there had been men in the water at the ferry stone on the Tuesday.

He saw the party at a quarter of a mile before the stone and he saw the barrow and the false baulm and the man holding the bar, and he stood still in the road for about as long as it takes a horse to drink and then he went the last quarter of a mile at the speed of a man with a limp and a stick who has decided something.

Tovan Kest looked up. He did not stop working, because the baulm was under load at the time and a man does not stop under load, and he looked up for a second and a half, and he went back to it, and the going of the day went on exactly as it had been going.

“**You have got four hours of daylight left and about nine feet of soft going on the far side of that,**” Aren Kest said.

“**I have got four hours of daylight and I have got two boards and nine inches and a bad length, and I have been on it since before light,**” Tovan Kest said.

“**Give me the bar.**”

“**No.**”

“**You have been on it since before light and you will be on it after dark and the far side is soft for nine feet going and you are twenty-nine with a yard behind you and no cart on it.**”

“**Give me the bar, brother,**” Tovan Kest said, “**when you can tell me what the load is doing.**”

And then he did not say anything else about it for about four seconds, and neither did Aren Kest, and about nine people who were standing at the low end of that road in the middle of a wet ford at about the fourth hour of the afternoon saw two brothers stand on a bank about four feet apart and not move, and about four of them understood what had just been said and about five of them only understood that one man had refused another man a bar.

Aren Kest stood on the bank and looked at the baulm.

“**The baulm is taking it,**” he said. “**That is why the far side is soft going and not the ford. You have taken four inches out of the top and there is nothing under the near end holding it back, so the whole thing is on the two trestles and one of them is on the soft and it is going to print into four inches and in about nine days the end of it will be lower than the middle, and then it will be a sump, and in the first real rain the sump will take the near nine feet off the approach.**”

“**It is not going to be a sump.**”

“**It is going to be a sump,**” Aren Kest said, “**and I am not going to hand you the bar for it, and I am going to stop in the middle of that sentence and I would like you to notice that I have stopped.**” He stopped. “**It is not because of anything except that I have read nine thousand feet of false baulm and I have made about four hundred and I know what a baulm prints into and you know what it prints into, and you have known since the Tuesday, and you did it anyway, and I would like to know why.**”

Tovan Kest put the bar down in the mud and wiped his hands on his coat, which took him about four seconds, and then he said the reason, and he gave it the way he gave everything, which is as a length of road and a number.

“**Because the order came down on the Saturday with a name at the top of it and it said make the crossing good by a day it named,**” he said, “**and a good crossing on a day it names is worth about nine days of a proper job to about forty households, and the proper job needs the ford low and needs two weeks of dry, and there are not going to be two weeks of dry in Longlight and everybody in this county knows that including you.**”

Aren Kest stood on the bank and did not have an answer to that, and it was the first time in about eleven years that he had not had an answer to something Tovan Kest said a length of road.

The two men who were with Tovan Kest had stopped and were standing about nine paces off and had not come back, because two men who work a yard for four hundred yards up a road somewhere else know what it looks like when the owner and his brother are doing this.

“**Then you have built it wrong on purpose,**” Aren Kest said.

“**I have built it good for a day,**” Tovan Kest said. “**It will be a sump in about nine days and you can come back and fix it in about four days in Harvestmonth and I will put a stone on the mistake so anybody can see it came from me and not from the weather. Write it down if you want to. I have been putting stones on my mistakes since I was seventeen and I have about nine of them in this county and nobody has ever taken one up.**”

He picked the bar up again and looked at the trestle on the soft ground, and then he did something that Aren Kest did not expect, which was to drive a wedge under the near end of the baulm, from the hard side, so that the load came off the soft trestle and went onto the hard one and the far end of the baulm came up a quarter of an inch.

“**There,**” Tovan Kest said. “**Now it prints into the hard and not the soft, and the end will come down a quarter of an inch instead of four, and in about four days you can pack it and be done, and it will be a good crossing for a year and it will be a good crossing in about nine days instead of about four and that is the whole difference.**”

“**That cost you a wedge.**”

“**That cost me a wedge and about six minutes and it was the whole of the argument,**” Tovan Kest said, “**and you did not have to give me the bar to have it, and I would rather somebody worked it out than somebody handed it to me. I have been handed a bar about nine times in my life and I have said no to about four of them and the four were all from you.**”

Nobody said anything about that and Aren Kest did not answer it and the two men at the water's edge about nine paces off looked at the water.

The woman of about fifty came up the bank then, at about the fifth hour, with her slate in her apron and the last column of it begun and nine days of nothing on it, and she did not say anything about the two men, and she said the thing that turned that day over.

“**You have been up here since the Tuesday,**” she said to the man of about thirty-one, “**and I have watched you and I have not come and asked you once, because I came on the Friday and you were working and I am not a woman who goes and asks a man at work, and you have had about four of my four miles and you have not asked me one question.**”

“**I have not.**”

“**Then that is the answer to my question,**” the woman of about fifty said, “**and I would like it said out loud in front of about nine people so that in a month neither of us can pretend it did not happen.**”

“**What question is that?**” the man said, and he had genuinely not asked it, and about four people at the water's edge understood that he had not asked because nobody had told him to.

“**Mine,**” the woman of about fifty said. “**You are nine men and a book and you are standing in my ford, and I have wanted for about four days to know whether you are going to shut it. That is what a road keeps me up about and that is what all of this is for, and I have not asked you because you have been at work, and you have not asked me because nobody told you to, and I would like both of us to have that in a street somewhere.**”

The man of about thirty-one stood in a foot of water at the third mark and looked at her for about as long as it takes to count a thing twice, and then he said what he was going to do, and he said it out loud.

“**I am going to shut this ford on the day the works are taken,**” he said. “**That is not mine. I have been told that the works are taken and I have been told what happens on that day and it is shut, and I have written the day down, and I have not written anything else down.**”

Nobody thanked him for saying it out loud and nobody thanked her for asking.

Then he went back to his four marks, and about nine people stood on a wet bank at the low end of a road and watched a Crown officer take the level of a run of water, and the woman of about fifty took her slate out of her apron and did not write anything on it, because it was not a number.

**And the cost of that Wednesday is a column and it is nobody's, and nine wedges and four inches of false baulm and about nine marks on a woman's four miles of road are not in it and none of them has a price.**

**a nosebag at 3, a curry comb at 4, a coil of rope at 12, a sheet of oiled paper at 19, a barrow of gravel at 25, a hurdle at 30, a hone at 34, a sack of shingle at 37, a feed bag at 39, and a saddle tree at 41.** Three, four, twelve, nineteen, twenty-five, thirty, thirty-four, thirty-seven, thirty-nine, and forty-one is two hundred and forty-four pence. **A mark is forty-eight pence and two hundred and forty-four pence is five marks and four over.** The barrow came off the soft at about the fifth hour and the far end of the baulm came up a quarter of an inch, and two men who had been standing about nine paces off for about ten minutes picked up the other end of the barrow without being asked and took it across the hard ground and set it down, and did not say anything about it, and about four people saw and about five did not, and the two men worked for Tovan Kest and were not in this county on any account of their own.

Then Tovan Kest came up out of the water and stood beside his brother at the top of the bank on the side of the light they could both stand in, and neither of them said anything for about as long as a man counts a horse, and then Aren Kest said the thing he had come down four miles to say, which was that a clerk in the market town had said on Saturday that a cart with the originals in it was going through on the coast road, and Tovan Kest said that he had seen it on the Friday morning at the top of the made road four miles up where it comes out of the lane, and that it had gone up towards the district and not down towards the market town, and that he had thought nothing of it because a cart going up is a cart going up.

“**How many in it,**” Aren Kest said.

“**Four men and a serjeant and nine hundredweight of paper,**” Tovan Kest said, “**and the driver told me the horses were fresh out of about nine days and the paper was dry, which is the answer to a question you did not ask me and which I have now answered anyway, because you are going to ask it and I would rather you did not have to come back down here for it.**”

“**I am not going to come back down here for it,**” Aren Kest said.

“**You are going to come back down here for it,**” Tovan Kest said, “**because you have been down here four miles on a bad road on a stick and you did not come for me and I have worked that out in about nine seconds, and I am not going to say what I worked out, and I have not said it, and we will both go on as though I had not said anything about it, and I am going to put that in a stone in about four days as well.**”
