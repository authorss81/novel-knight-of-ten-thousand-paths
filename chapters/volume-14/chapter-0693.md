# Chapter 0693

**Hearthmonth the eleventh, YR 321, a Saturday and a market morning, about four hundred people in that market street at about the second hour and about two hundred of them stopped, a serjeant of about thirty-eight with a sheet and about four men who has been somewhere every three days since the middle of Fallowmonth, and four miles of road that nobody can name.**

The nine days ended yesterday and nobody has said so, and about four people in nine counties have said since that a thing which has an end nobody announced is not a thing anybody is looking forward to, and about five have said that nine days is not an end and that nobody has ever finished anything in this county by saying that it was finished.

A market morning in Hearthmonth is a short thing. The light comes up the east end of that street at half past seven and has gone off the west end by two in the afternoon, and the boards stand in the middle of it where the shadow of the weigh-house does not fall in winter, and a man who wants to be read to from nine feet off has to stand on the sunny side of them. The cold that morning had a crust on the puddles by the ninth hour, and the puddles by the ninth hour were the ones in the ruts, and a man who stands in a market street for two hours puts one foot in a rut and keeps it there, because moving it is worse.

Two men unloaded boards off a cart at the low end and argued the price of the carting, which they do every Thursday, and which on that Thursday took nine minutes longer than it takes, because one of them had heard what was said at the boards and wanted to talk about it and the other one had not and did not. Nobody thanked either of them and the cart went up the lane empty at the third hour with the driver walking beside it and putting his hands in his pockets.

The count of nine gates was not a count anybody made in one place. A woman of about thirty-eight who keeps a tally at a post walked out on the ninth day and came back on the tenth with a number and with which crossings and in which counties, and she wrote it on a leaf and sent it nowhere, and read it out to nine people in a lane and did not write those nine down either. A man of about fifty-two who keeps four miles under contract saw two of the gates from his own four miles, because his four miles join two of the others at the pound. A man of about thirty-eight who puts boards on the inside of gates stood at one of them for an hour and a half and counted nothing else. The other four were reported by four separate people who had been going past them for thirty years.

Nobody thanked any of them and nobody made a total of the total, and the number nine exists in nine counties because four people counted and five heard, and that is a worse record than a man in a room four hundred miles off would keep and a better one than nine counties has ever kept about anything.

On the ninth day there were nine gates standing. That is the whole of the count and it is the only count anybody has, and about four people in that market street had heard that nine gates stood on the tenth of Hearthmonth, and five had heard that somebody had looked.

Then the serjeant came up the market street on foot at about the second hour with about four men behind him at the half-pace, and he was not in a cloak, and he had a sheet folded in his coat and not a small book, and about four people at the boards noticed the sheet and about five noticed the four men, and the four men were the part that frightened them, which has been true of every four men a Crown party has brought into this street since the eighth of Embermonth.

Nobody thanked him and he put the sheet on the boards himself and did not unfold it and did not put it away, and he said what he had before he said what he wanted, which is the order he says everything in.

“**Three things and then I will read. One. I am a serjeant and I have a copy of one line of my captain's warrant and not the warrant, and I have had it since the middle of Fallowmonth, and I have been somewhere every three days with it and about four men. Two. I have got a book with a list of roads in it in order and it has got a figure against each one and I have never once been told what the figure is for. Three. I am not going to ask this street for anything and I am going to read out four miles of road, and then I am going to ask one question, and then I am going to write down whatever anybody says, and I will read it back.**”

Nobody thanked him and two hundred people in that market street had heard a serjeant give the shape of his own visit in three parts before using any of it, and four had understood that he was not going to ask for a name, and five had not.

Then he read the road out of the book, and he read it as a book is read, which is that he read the words on the page and not the shape of the thing, and he read it in one go and did not stop in the middle.

“**High side of the waterworks country, by the field wall, to the turn where the lane takes the low side, four miles, and at the four miles a gate.**”

Nobody thanked him and the market went on and four people had heard four miles and five had heard a gate.

Then he asked the one question, and he asked it to the boards and not to anybody in particular, and he asked it the way a man asks a question when he has asked it before and has not yet learned whether it works.

“**Is that the road.**”

Nobody said anything and two hundred people in that market street had heard a man ask a question and four of them had looked at the boards and five had looked at the ground.

Then the local people came, one at a time, on their own errands, for their own reasons, and they were there, and none of them was asked to be and none of them had been told that the serjeant was coming.

Nobody thanked anybody and the first of them was a woman of about forty-three who had come down to market with eleven eggs in a basket and had come about nine days earlier every week since the middle of Longlight, and she came about nine feet off the boards and stopped and stood there, and she was not asked anything.

Then a man of about thirty-eight who puts boards on the inside of gates came up the market street and went straight to the high end of the boards and stood with his back to them, and he was asked nothing, and nobody thanked him, and about four people in that market street had worked out that he had come on his own account because he had a board to buy and about five had not worked that out.

Then the man of about fifty-one who owns a field on the low side of a cut came in at about the third hour because he had nine sacks of something going up the made road and the cart was late, and he came to the boards and he was asked nothing and he said nothing for about as long as it takes to turn a cart.

Nobody thanked him and four people heard a man say nothing at length and five heard nothing.

Then the serjeant asked the question again, and he asked it the same way he had asked it the first time, word for word, and nobody thanked him for asking it twice and nobody has ever thanked anybody for anything in this county.

“**Is that the road.**”

And a man of about twenty-nine who keeps a smith's shop four streets up said the only thing anybody said in that street for about nine minutes, and he said it to the serjeant and not to the boards, and nobody stopped him.

“**It is a lane. It has been a lane since before my father's father and my father used it with a waggon and so do I, and I could not tell you in the morning if it goes up or down, and I have been asked that by two men in a coat this year and I have told both of them what I am telling you.**”

Nobody thanked him and about four people in that market street had heard a smith say that a lane is a lane, and about five had heard a man refuse to be a map.

Then the man of about fifty-two who keeps four miles of made road under contract for about nineteen years came up the market street at about the third hour and went to the low end of the boards and stood there, and nobody thanked him, and he said one thing and nobody had asked him.

“**That is not my four miles. My four miles begin at the pound and go the other way and I have kept them for nineteen years and there is not a day in the year that anybody can say my four miles are not mine. If that is four miles then there are about nine counties of road and there always have been.**”

Nobody thanked him and two hundred people in that market street had heard a contractor put four miles of road on one side of a market town and the other four on the other, and four of them had understood that this was the answer to the serjeant's question and five had heard a man talk about his own work.

Then the man of about fifty-one, who had come in at the third hour with nine sacks and had said nothing at length since, asked a question out loud, and nine people heard it and nobody stopped him, and it was the first thing said in that market street all morning that was a question about the paper.

“**Then what are you going to write at the four miles?**”

And the serjeant said the true thing about it and nobody thanked him and four people heard it and five did not.

“**I am going to write what I have already written, which is that a lane is a lane and a man does not know which way it goes and a contractor does not keep it. And I am going to write the day on it. And in about four years a man in a room will read that page and decide a lane was not a road, and on that day he will be wrong, and there will be nothing on the page to tell him that he is wrong.**”

Nobody thanked him and four people in that market street had heard a Crown serjeant say that his own page would be the evidence against his own question in about four years, and five had heard a man talk about paper.

Then four of the nine households on that four miles came into that market street that morning on their own errands and none of them was asked for anything, and here is the whole of what four of them said when the road was read out again at the fourth hour.

The house at the turn where the lane takes the low side keeps a dog and eleven hens and came in to buy meal, and a woman of about fifty-seven said the lane is the lane and said it without being asked and said it to the serjeant, and then said that the wall on the high side belongs to two farms and has belonged to two farms since before her mother, and that the lane between the wall and the field is nobody's, because a lane does not belong to anybody, it belongs to using, and that she has never known a person who could prove a lane was his and has never needed to.

The house at the half mile keeps nine hives and came in to sell honey, and a man of about forty-four said that he uses it, that he has used it since he was a boy, that a man who uses a thing is not the owner of it, and that in his trade nobody owns a lane and everybody answers for it, and that this was the only thing he had come down to say and he had said it to a Crown officer because he had been asked and because it was true.

The house at the fourth mile is the gate, and the gate was standing, and the people at it did the ordinary work of that gate while nine people watched them do it and did not speak to them.

The house at the one mile and a half has a daughter at market and came in for nothing else at all and stood by the boards for about nine minutes and went out again.

Nobody thanked them and four people in that market street had heard four households say four ordinary things about a lane they have used for thirty years, and five had heard nothing and had gone to buy meal.

Then the serjeant wrote, and he wrote the way the man of about thirty-one in a livery writes, which is that he wrote down what was said and not what was meant, and he wrote for about four seconds, and then he read it back to nine people and about two hundred heard it.

“**Is that the road. No answer given. A smith says it is a lane. A contractor says it is not his four miles. A man says he does not know which way it goes.**”

Nobody thanked him and about four people in that market street had heard a serjeant read back five sentences in the order they had been said, and about five had heard a man keep a thing out of a book.

Then he asked the question a third time and he asked it of the boards as a whole, and nobody answered it, and he waited about as long as it takes to turn a cart, and then he closed the book.

Nobody said anything and about nine people in that market street had seen a serjeant of about thirty-eight ask the same question three times in about four minutes and take no answer three times, and about four of the nine had understood that he was not going to press it and about five had understood that he had run out of three days.

Then he said the thing that is why he is not called a knave and is not called a fool in this county, and he said it to the boards and he said it once, and nobody thanked him.

“**I have been doing this since the middle of Fallowmonth and I have asked that question about nine hundred lengths of road in about nine counties and I have had an answer to about nine of them, and I am going to write this one down as no answer given, and I am going to carry it up the made road and put it in front of my captain, and he is going to be annoyed, and that is the correct arrangement and I would like about nine people in this street to notice that I have said so myself before anybody noticed it.**”

Nobody thanked him and about two hundred people in that market street had heard a Crown serjeant account for his own difficulty in public and about four had understood that he had done it on purpose and about five had heard a man be tired.

Then the local people went back to their own errands, one at a time, and the woman with the eleven eggs went out of the market street and up the lane with the basket on her arm, and the man with nine sacks went out at about the fourth hour and found his cart, and the man who puts boards on the inside of gates went up the lane at the ordinary pace of a man going to buy a board.

Nobody thanked them and about nine people in that market street had watched about nine people leave a market street in about four minutes without having been asked anything, and about four had understood that none of them had said the serjeant was wrong and about five had understood that none of them had said he was right.

A man of thirty-two with a stick stood at the high end of those boards from the second hour until the fourth with his back to the sun and his mouth shut, and about four people in that market street had come that morning to hear what the man who wrote nine hundred words would say to a serjeant with a book, and about five had not, and he said nothing.

Then the badge-man said the one thing he said about the whole of that morning, and he said it at the low end of the boards in the open, and it was about the serjeant and not about the road, and nobody thanked him for it.

“**He has not asked you for a name. He has asked you nine hundred times for a name and he has never once asked for a witness, and there is no clause in about nine hands that could give him either, and I have said that same thing about a captain in this street and I am saying it about his serjeant and I am not going to write one down.**”

Nobody thanked him and about four people in that market street had heard a man of fifty-eight say the same true thing twice about two men in one coat, and about five had heard a man keep a thing in his mouth that he had been carrying for a month.

Nobody gave the serjeant an answer and nobody thanked him and about nine people in nine counties have said since that a serjeant of about thirty-eight came into a market street on the eleventh of Hearthmonth and read four miles out of a book and went away with the same four miles, and about five have said that he will come back on a three-day interval as he has come back on a three-day interval, and that nobody in nine counties has worked out why the four miles did not hold and about four have said that nobody in nine counties is going to work it out, because working it out is a thing a man does on his own account and there has never been one.

Nobody thanked anybody and the nine days were over and nobody counted them, and the column was on a made road nine miles north with about four men on it and a captain on it, and about four people in that market street had worked out that a serjeant who has an answer travels back down the made road and a serjeant who has not does not, and about five had worked out nothing at all.

**And the cost of that Saturday is four miles of road that nine households use and that none of them can name, a serjeant of about thirty-eight who will write no answer given, and nine days of held crossings that ended without a person announcing the end, are not in it and none of them has a price.**

**a sheet of foolscap at 1, a stub of chalk at 2, a mile of string at 15, a field book at 17, a kettle at 30, a pair of shears at 32, a hurdle at 34, a gate shoe at 35, a barrow wheel at 36, and a pair of barrow handles at 40.** One, two, fifteen, seventeen, thirty, thirty-two, thirty-four, thirty-five, thirty-six and forty is two hundred and forty-two pence. **A mark is forty-eight pence and two hundred and forty-two pence is five marks and two over.** The serjeant folded the sheet back into his coat at about the fourth hour and went up the made road on foot with about four men behind him at the half-pace and got to the pound at about the seventh hour where the man of about fifty-two was cutting a rail, and neither of them said anything to the other, and the serjeant put his hand on the gate post on the way up and left it there about as long as it takes to turn a cart, and about nine people in nine counties have said since that a man who puts a hand on a gate post is a man who is going to be asked about that post again.
