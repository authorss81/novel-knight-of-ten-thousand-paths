# Chapter 0667

**Harvestmonth the twentieth, YR 321, a Friday and not a market morning, the fourth hour at the market town's own gate and then a mile and a half up a lane on foot, the room over the weigh-house shut behind its own door, and a barrow that came down that lane at about the ninth hour on the seventh with nothing in it and a man of about twenty-two not on it.**

The barrow came down the lane at about the ninth hour on the seventh of Harvestmonth, and by the ninth hour at the end of Harvestmonth it was dark in a lane with no made road in the last half mile, and it was the lane's barrow with the cheek that was not cracked, and the handle on the low side was wet and the handle on the high side was dry, which is what a barrow does when one man has been carrying it and then stops carrying it.

Nobody saw it come down. That is the part that matters and it is the part that took nine people fourteen days to be able to say out loud. The lane is a mile and a half and it comes out on the made road at the top of a rise, and nine people in this county have said since the seventh that a barrow came down a lane in the dark and nobody was on the road to see it, and four of them have said the sentence and five have not been able to.

The man of about twenty-two was a carter's mate and he came out of a yard at the low end of the market street and he went up the lane with the barrow because a barrow weighs forty pounds empty and the man in the livery had nine men and four hundred yards of sill and no time, and because four people at the low side of that cut said that he ought not to, and he said that he would be back before dark, and he was, in the sense that the barrow was.

His name was not on anything and nobody in nine counties had written it down, and four people saw him go up the lane and not one of them wrote the name down afterwards, and about four of them have said since that they should have and about five have said that there was no reason to.

Aren Kest went up the lane at about the fifth hour on the twentieth with his stick, on his own account, and nobody had sent him, and four people at the market town's gate watched him go and five did not.

He had been up to the top of the cut at the fourth hour. The work on the barrel drain was done and the frost had gone and the water was going through it, and the man of about fifty-one was not at the top of the cut, which on a Friday is where he stands with his board and a bar, because he has done it every Friday since the middle of Rainmonth, and a man of about thirty-eight was there instead, filling in, who said he had been asked to and had not known what he was filling in for and had been told to stand at the top of a cut with a board in his hand on a Friday, and he said that on the Friday before there had been a woman of about thirty-one there instead, and that the man of about fifty-one had gone to a market four miles off, and that nobody in nine counties had arranged any of it.

Aren Kest did not stay at the cut. He went on up the last half mile, where the lane is not made at all, and at the place where it comes out on the made road there is a rise with a gate at the top of it and a field on the high side.

There was nothing there. That is what he found and it is what there is. There was a Crown crew's waggon standing at the made road about nine hundred yards up with a timber in it and about four men round it who were not the crew that had been there on the seventh and who had come from the coast side and had not been in this county before the twelfth, and they did not know what he was asking and told him so in the ordinary way.

One of them said, after a minute, that there had been a crew at that gate on the seventh and at that gate again on the fourteenth, and that they had gone on up the coast road in between, and that the man who had the crew had said that the lad with the barrow came up the made road behind them at about the fourth hour of the afternoon and went on with the crew for about a mile to a gate that is on no map any of them has, and that at about the seventh hour, when the crew turned off the coast road and went to quarters at the ferry stone, the lad was not with them, and that nobody had asked him to and nobody had stopped him and it had not been entered in the book, because there is nothing in the book for a boy on a road.

“**He turned back,**” the man said, and then stopped, and said the other half of it himself, in the flat way a man from a crew says a thing. “**No. That is the half of it I have been asked about four times in a fortnight and I have not got the other half. What I have got is that he was not with us at the seventh hour, and that the barrow came down the lane without him, and I did not see that either.**”

Then the man got on with a timber, and Aren Kest stood at the top of a rise on a Friday for as long as it takes a carter to get a load round, and then he went back down a mile and a half of lane and up four miles of made road to the market town's gate, and he was at the boards at about the second hour, and it was a Friday, and the room over the weigh-house was shut behind its own door.

The carter of about fifty was in the market street and he was the one who said the second half of it, and he said it to a man of about thirty-three who had asked him, and Aren Kest heard the whole of it from about nine paces.

“**I have been up and down that lane every week for about nine years,**” the carter of about fifty said. “**And I have asked about the end of it four times since the seventh, and the answer is the same every time, and it is that there is no end to it. That lane goes a mile and a half and comes out on the made road, and the made road goes up to the coast, and that is the whole of it, and a thing that joins a road is not a place anybody keeps a list of.**”

“**And you cannot get past that,**” the man of about thirty-three said.

“**I cannot get past it at all,**” the carter said. “**I have been turning it over for fourteen days and here is where I have got to, and I am telling nine people because I am going to say it out loud once and then I am not going to say it again. Fourteen days ago a man asked nine people for a barrow and nine people gave him one, and a lad of twenty-two carried it up a lane, and that is the whole of what has happened on that lane in about a year, and I have been trying for fourteen days to make that into something and I cannot, and I am not going to be the one who does it.**”

About nine people in that market street have said since that nobody was thanked, and it is the truest thing anybody has said about that morning.

Then the badge-man asked one question, and it was the only question he asked about it, and nine people in that market street were standing there waiting to find out how many he was going to ask.

“**Did anybody see him go up?**”

“**Four did,**” the copyist of about twenty-six said, and she had come down the lane from the weigh-house without her coat, and four of the nine people in that street understood that the woman who keeps a room had walked a mile and a half in the cold to stand at a board and had not brought the register, and five did not. “**A man of about thirty-eight, a boy of about thirteen, the man of about fifty-one and me. We were at the low side of the cut and we saw him go up the lane at about the ninth hour and we did not see him come down. That is the whole of what four people saw and I have got it right and I have not added to it since the seventh and I would like nine people to notice that a woman who keeps a book has not added anything to it in fourteen days.**”

“**Good,**” the badge-man said, and said it the way he says a thing he is going to be asked about again in a month and wants to have got right.

Then he said the rest of it, and he said it to about nine people at the low end of the boards on a Friday, and he said why he was not going to ask a second question and he gave the reason in about nine words and did not soften it.

“**I am not going to ask anything else about that, and I have been asked by about four people this week whether I would, and the reason is that a man who asks a question a second time is not asking it because he wants an answer. He is asking it because he does not like the one he has got, and about four years ago I did that to a woman about a road and I have not been able to get it back since and I am not doing it to a lad of twenty-two.**”

Nobody said anything to that and four people at the boards looked at the ground and five looked up the lane.

A woman of about forty-nine who keeps a house four doors up the market street was at the high end of the boards through the whole of it and nobody had asked her anything, and about nine people in that market street had worked out in about nine seconds that the badge-man was not going to ask her anything, and about four of them had understood why.

“**He is not going to ask me because he has asked me once,**” the woman said, before anybody had said it, “**on the ninth, in a lane, and he asked it straight, and I answered him straight, and he said that it was very good of me and wrote it on nothing.**”

Nobody said anything.

“**I have got a fortnight,**” she said. “**I have got a yard with nobody in it and a barrow that came back wet on one side and I have got four people who have said nine different things to me about it and not one of them was wrong, and that is the part I cannot put down, and I am not going to say the rest of it in a market street because I would like about nine people in nine counties to have a chance of hearing whatever it turns into, and I have not got to that yet and I am not going to stand here and find it out in front of a bar.**”

“**That is the right way round,**” the badge-man said, and four people at the boards did not understand that he had agreed with her about anything, because it was the first time in a year that he had.

Then the badge-man read out, in the open, on a Friday, at about the third hour, the reason nobody had stopped a man of about twenty-two walking up a lane on the seventh of Harvestmonth, and he read it out as a list of about nine things, and nine people heard all nine of them and four understood that the list was the finding.

“**One. The road was not shut. It has never been shut and nobody in nine counties has ever shut it, and the three clauses are about a man who proposes to shut a road and there was not one on that lane.**”

“**Two. The barrow was lent. A thing that is lent is not a thing that is kept, and the nine people at the low side of that cut gave a barrow away in about nine seconds and there is not one clause in about nine hands about a thing that is given.**”

“**Three. The man in the livery said he would have it back inside a day and he did, and about four of the nine of us watched him do it, and there is not one clause about a man who does what he said he would do.**”

“**Four. Four of us told the lad not to go and he said he would be back before dark. About five of us did not tell him anything and about four of us did not think of it.**”

“**Five. There is no head in nine counties and I am the man with the badge and I was at the market town's gate and I was not at the top of that cut, and if I had been at the top of that cut I would have said nothing, and I want that in a street, and it is the worst thing on this list and it is mine.**”

“**Six. Nobody in nine counties has standing to stop a man walking up a lane, and I have been getting round to that for about a year and I have not got round to it.**”

“**Seven. There is no rule anywhere in nine counties about a boy on a road.**”

“**Eight. He was twenty-two and had been out since he was eleven and had a barrow in his hand and was going to a gate about four hundred yards away, and about four of the nine of us had seen that exact thing go by a gate and go home in about four years.**”

“**Nine, and this is the one I have been turning over, is that every one of those eight is a reason and not one of them is a person, and a thing with nine reasons in it and no person in it is a thing that will be done again in about a month, and the second time there will not be a barrow involved, because the second time the barrow will have been the first time.**”

Nobody thanked him, and a woman of about forty-nine in that street did not go home and did not ask anybody to.

Aren Kest stood at the high end of the boards through all of it with his stick in his right hand and did not say the finding, and the finding was in him and had been for about ten weeks, and it was three sentences long, and one of the three was the only one anybody in nine counties could have argued with.

A rule about shutting a road cannot be used against a man who never shuts one.

He had walked nine miles that day to look at the top of a rise and had found a waggon and four men who had come from the coast side, and he had a mile and a half of lane behind him and about fourteen days of a mother's fortnight behind him and a boy's name that was on nothing, and the second sentence of his finding came out in his head while the badge-man was reading out the ninth of nine reasons, and it is this, and it is the part that has cost the most and that he has said out loud exactly once.

That the instrument has no road in it, and that the second clause asks for aid, and that a man in a livery can ask a woman for a horse and say that it was very good of her before she has said no, and that neither of those is a closure, and that a carter's mate of about twenty-two can carry a barrow a mile and a half up a lane to a gate he has no business at and be the only thing anybody in nine counties has lost.

A county can be taken a barrow in nine seconds and a boy in a mile and a half and there is not one clause in about nine hands about either, and both of those went through the same place, which is a gate, and the gate was open both times.

He did not say it. He had said one true small thing about a ford in the rain on the nineteenth of Rainmonth and about nine people had turned round and looked at him while he said it, and he had said nothing for a month since, and four people in that market street on a Friday were waiting for him to say something and five were not, and the badge-man noticed the waiting and said out loud that they were waiting, in the manner of a man who has been saying things out loud in streets for thirty-one years, and did not ask him for it.

**And the cost of that Friday is a column, and it is nobody's, and a mile and a half of lane after dark and a handle wet on one side and a boy's name that is on nothing are not in it and none of them has a price.**

**a wicket at 8, a lamp of tallow at 17, a gallon of oil at 24, a second sheet of oiled paper at 30, a week's bread at 31, a water butt at 32, a lock for a gate at 34, a pail at 35, a second bar of iron at 37, and a second horse collar at 39.** Eight, seventeen, twenty-four, thirty, thirty-one, thirty-two, thirty-four, thirty-five, thirty-seven and thirty-nine is two hundred and eighty-seven pence. **A mark is forty-eight pence and two hundred and eighty-seven pence is five marks and forty-seven over.** The man of about fifty-one was at his own gate at about the fourth hour that Friday and was told by about four people in about nine seconds and said one word, and the word was not a name and did not go in anything, and about nine people in this county know that he said it and about five of them know what the word was.

Then the boy of about thirteen came down the lane at about the ninth hour with the board in his hand to put it against the wall at the top of the cut, because a man of about thirty-eight had asked him to and because the man of about fifty-one had said on the Friday before that if the board was not stood against the wall by dark somebody would come up that lane in the dark and stand in nine inches of water, and nine people in nine counties did not know that a man of thirty-eight had asked a boy of thirteen to do a thing that a man of fifty-one had thought of first, and four of them found out in a year.
