# Chapter 0653

**Longlight the fourth, YR 321, a Saturday and a market morning, the second hour in the market street with about four hundred people in it, a question that came eleven days put in the open at the boards, and the answer given by the only person in nine counties who has counted a road.**

The market was on because it was a Saturday and the street had about four hundred people in it and about nine of them standing at the low end of the counter boards, and the rest of it doing what a market does on the first Saturday of a month, which is more than it does on the others and not much more.

Salla Dunnock had been in the market town eight days and had stopped being a woman who had come eleven days, which happens faster than anybody expects, and had become a woman who was in the market, and the difference was that on the third day people asked her about the coast instead of about the eleven days.

The badge-man had put her question where he puts things, which is in the open at the boards at about the second hour on a market morning, and he had done it without a name at the top of it, and he had said out loud that he was doing that on purpose and that the reason was that a question with a name at the top of it gets answered by the person whose name it is, and that this question was not going to be answered by anybody in particular because it was not going to be answered at all in this county and it was going to be answered eleven days from now by about nine hundred people who had never seen a market street.

About four hundred people heard him say that and about nine hundred who were not in the street did not, and about four of the nine who were at the boards understood that he had just told the whole market that nobody here could answer it.

“**Ask it,**” Salla Dunnock said. “**Ask it in the street and let about nine people hear it and let about four hundred hear you ask it.**”

So the badge-man asked it, and it took eleven seconds.

“**What is a road for?**”

And nobody answered it, and it sat in the street for about as long as it takes a carter to get a load round, and about four hundred people found about nine things to do.

Aren Kest was at the high end of the boards with his stick and had said nothing, and Mara Vey was nine paces off at the edge of it with her hands behind her back and the door of the weigh-house four steps behind her, and the two of them had about four feet of market between them all morning and had each asked the other one question about the day's returns at about the third hour, and it had been the ordinary question and it had been answered the ordinary way, and neither of them had made a thing of it and about four people had noticed and about five had not.

The woman of about fifty came into the street at about the fourth hour with her slate in her apron and nine days of nothing on it and the last column of it begun, which is what she does in the first week of a month.

She has kept the road from the ferry stone at the low end to the ninth milestone at the top for nineteen years. It is four miles. There is a ford between them and the ford is dry in most summers and was wet for about four days in Longlight and she went out to it twice and did not charge the delegation and has said so out loud, in the street, in front of about nine people, so that nobody can make it a thing later.

She said it because she was asked whether she had wanted the delegation on her road, which is a question a person asks a woman who keeps a road about another woman standing on it.

“**No,**” the woman of about fifty said. “**They were wet to the knee and the old one could not get down and the young one could not carry her and the boy carried a bundle the shape of a person four hundred paces, which I have not seen a child carry before and did not know could be done. I went out twice with a bar and made the crossing twice and charged them nothing, and I am telling nine people so that in a month when somebody says I kept a delegation back I can say I did not and you can say you heard me.**”

Nobody thanked her for that either, and then about nine people at the boards heard what she said and one of them said it again in her own words further up the street and by the time it got to the low end it was about twice as long.

Then she answered the question.

She was not asked twice and she did not offer. She had been in the street for half an hour with her slate and she had heard the question go round and she answered it the way a person answers a question about their own work, which is that she gave the finding and not the argument.

“**A road is not for getting anywhere,**” she said. “**That is what a road is for and everybody in this street already knows it and about nine hundred people have just been told it by a woman who came eleven days. My road goes from a stone in the mud at the low end to a milestone at the top. I have walked it in every weather about nine hundred times. In four years of counting how many days in a year it can be walked, the number has been about nine in ten in a dry year and about four in ten in a wet one, and in a wet year the rest of the people go round by the long road, which is nine miles further and costs four times the money, and they go round, and they get there.**” She turned the slate round so that the nine columns faced out and did not read any of them. “**So that is not the answer and I have given it to see if it is the one you want. A road is for the people who cannot go round.**”

“**Which is how many?**” the copyist asked, from about nine paces off, and asked it the way she asks things, which is that a figure is a figure until somebody makes it an argument.

“**In my road, on the wet days, out of the households at the two ends, it is about two in ten who cannot go round,**” the woman of about fifty said, “**and it is four in ten in the worst week, and those are two numbers about the same road and they are not one number and I will not make them one. And I have never once in nineteen years counted a house that went round and came back the same day and I am not going to start.**”

Nobody thanked her and about four people at the boards said the word two out loud to each other, and the copyist of about twenty-six looked at her own hands for a second and a half and did not say a number out loud, which for the copyist was the loudest thing anybody could have done in that street.

Then the badge-man said no, and he said it out loud, and about nine people at the boards stopped.

“**No,**” he said. “**And I am going to say why, and I have said the same reason once in this street before and I am saying it twice and I would say it as many times as it takes. Two months ago a woman stood in this street and asked whether the right to refuse was dangerous, and I refused to let it be answered that day, and a man of about forty-four who has not got anything to do with any of this wrote down afterwards that I was right to refuse. That is the trouble with asking a thing at the same time as you want to answer it. A question that has been asked whether it might be softened has already been softened. I am not going to take two in ten out of the mouth of a woman who keeps a road and make it into a rule, and I would ask the nine of you not to do it either, because in a year there will be a man who wants a number and there will be this number sitting in a street with our names near it, and the next woman who keeps a road will be asked whether her road is as good.**”

“**Then what do we do with it,**” Salla Dunnock said.

“**Nothing,**” the badge-man said. “**That is the answer to half the questions that get asked in this street and it is the correct half and nobody likes it.**”

Nobody argued with him and about four people at the boards agreed with him and about five did not and none of them said so, which is how a street works.

Then a man of about thirty-one from the Crown's road office came through the market with a letter in a leather band and stopped at the boards, which he had never done in about four years, and said that he had been told to want it and that he did not want it, and he said the whole of it in nine sentences because about four hundred people were in the street and he would not have said it twice.

He said that the district had written to the road office on the Thursday. He said that it was not a letter about roads. He said that it was a notice that had been printed somewhere and that the office had been sent nine copies of it to put up in nine places, of which this town was one, and that the office did not put up things and that this one was going to have to be put up because the district had said it would be.

He said that it concerned the works and the anchors and the roads connected to them, and that it was not addressed to this county, and that the office had been asked to find out where those roads were, and that it had nine copies of a sheet and no instructions.

Then he said the thing that turned the market morning over, and he said it while he was still holding the leather band.

“**There is a cart on the coast road with the originals in it,**” he said. “**It came on at about the fourth hour on Friday and it was still going on at about the seventh hour last night. It goes through here today.**”

Nobody in the street thanked him and the copyist of about twenty-six asked him one question and asked it flatly.

“**Did the district say where it was going to put it up?**”

“**It said put it up,**” the man of about thirty-one said. “**It did not say where to put it and it did not say who had to be able to read it, and I have been a clerk in this office for six years and there is not one notice I have ever handled that came with an instruction like that, and I have been thinking about it for a day and a half and I cannot make it mean anything else than that it does not matter where it goes because they expect it to be in everybody's hand inside a week.**”

The copyist looked at him for a second and a half and then she said something that about nine people heard and about four understood.

“**Put it up in this market,**” the copyist said, “**on the boards, on the Saturday, where it cannot be missed, and do not put it up on a gate.**”

Nobody argued with her about it and the man of about thirty-one said that he would have to think about it, which is more than most of them did.

**And the cost of that Saturday is a column and it is nobody's, and two numbers about the same road that are not one number and a ford that was wet for about four days and nine months of a slate in an apron are not in it and none of them has a price.**

**a lump of chalk at 3, a pot of ink at 4, a bundle of hazel rods at 9, a pair of shears at 16, a wicket latch at 24, a string of twine at 30, a board of deal at 33, a barrow wheel at 37, a bag of nails at 39, and a gate latch at 41.** Three, four, nine, sixteen, twenty-four, thirty, thirty-three, thirty-seven, thirty-nine, and forty-one is two hundred and thirty-six pence. **A mark is forty-eight pence and two hundred and thirty-six pence is four marks and forty-four over.** The three courses of sill under the town's own gate were holding where they were put in Greenmonth and the top of them had gone grey and the joints had grown a finger's width of weed in the joint and the man of about fifty who laid them came down at about the fourth hour, looked at it, put his hand on the top course, said that it would do and would not do better than that, and left the mallet where it was, on the line of stone for the fourth course at the top of the bank, where anybody coming off the road could see it, and said that a course is a course and not a lie, and nobody asked him about it and nobody thanked him.

Then the cart came up through the gate at about the seventh hour with the woman's road behind it and the boy of about nine sitting on the load with his boots on this time and the woman of about sixty telling him he had not earned them, and about nine people in the market watched it go by and about four of them said to each other that the delegation was going back, and the delegation was not going back for about three weeks and had not said so to anybody except the carter, and the carter told nobody.

Then the badge-man said the last thing of that morning and said it to Salla Dunnock and not to the market, and it was nine words long.

“**I have said that nothing has come up that road and I have now said it twice,**” he said, “**and I would like to be the only man in nine counties who has said it.**”

She said that she would hold him to that, and he said that he would not want her to, and about four hundred people in the market street went home to about nine counties with the cart already behind them.
