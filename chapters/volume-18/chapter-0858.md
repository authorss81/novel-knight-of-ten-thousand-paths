# Chapter 0858

**Mudmonth the nineteenth, YR 325, a Monday and not a market morning and the yard at the foot of that stair empty except for about nine men and a river making a noise that carries nine hundred yards, the sixth floor of that place being opened from below at the fourth hour of the morning, and the range lit at the top of nine hundred yards of stair with four people in it who had all been in that weather before.**

The river came up over the low field above the second landing at about the ninth hour of the night and it did not stop at the low field.

What it did at about the second hour of the morning was come down off the low field in a sheet about four hundred yards wide and put nine inches over the yard at the foot of the stair in about nine minutes, and then go up the water stair and stand in it, and then keep going.

About four hundred people in that yard who had come down for the Monday were on about nine feet of stair by the fourth hour with about nine feet of water under them at the bottom landing, and they went out through the gap in the loose stone at the top and down nine hundred yards of made road to the town. Nine men stayed. The officer holding this floor stayed, and the man who keeps the count stayed, and seven others of about the nine stayed.

The sound of it is the whole of that night. A river over a field four hundred yards wide, in the dark, with about nine hundred yards of stone under it, is the loudest thing there is short of a fall, and it was loud enough to cover the sound of a stone floor being opened from underneath, which is a noise like a sack of gravel being set down four times at once and then a fifth time.

And on that Monday morning it did cover it. He knew it was happening and he could not tell from where.

The sixth floor was being opened from below. That is not an assault and it is not a breach. It is four men with a wedge and a bar going at the underside of a floor from a landing two storeys down, because that is the only way into that floor without going up nine hundred yards in daylight past about nine men, and about four of the four men doing it had been told it was necessary and about five of the seven who had stayed had been told nothing at all.

There were two people on that floor. One was the officer holding this floor, who had gone up to fetch the flat bar out of the third landing and could not get back down. The other was a woman of about twenty-four who has kept the anchor floor's lamps for about two years and who had been on the sixth floor at about the eleventh hour of the night because the lamp on that floor had gone out and she was told to relight it and never came back down.

Going down in that is not possible. The water stair is under nine inches and rising, and nine feet of the lowest flights are under, and a man with a bad leg does not go down nine hundred yards of stair into moving water at the second hour of the morning to bring anybody out of it.

So they went up. There was one way out of that floor which is the run of about ninety feet of gallery along the outside of the sixth storey with about nine feet between the outer wall and the inner one, and the gallery on that floor takes the water off the building and had been taking it all night from the other side.

He got her along it.

That is a floor of about ninety feet with about nine inches of standing water on it from the outside, in the dark, in a wind that came off the low field, with a bar in his right hand and a lamp in his left and a girl of twenty-four who could not run because of an ankle.

And for the whole of the ninety feet the only thing he had to go on was sound.

The thing he had to hear was whether the floor under his feet was still there. It was there. He knew it was there because he was standing on it. What he had to hear was the sound of it stopping being there somewhere behind him and about nine feet to the left, which is a thing you can hear before you can feel it.

And he could not hear it, because the ear on the side the stick is on is gone, and that side is the right side, and the sound was on the right side and about nine feet behind him.

So he turned his head away from it.

That is backwards and he knows it is backwards and four and a half years ago a woman on a causeway four days off taught him that a man with one ear turns it toward the sound, and it took him about nine days to believe her and about nine years to understand that the reason works is not the ear at all.

It works because of the skull. A sound off to the right of a head that cannot hear on the right comes round the bone of it and arrives at the left ear from behind and below and about a quarter of a second late. Turn toward the sound and the good ear is pointed at it and the skull is doing nothing. Turn away from it and the skull is doing all of it, and the good ear hears the room.

He had never before needed to do it, and he did it nine hundred times in ninety feet of gallery, and he had it wrong twice and once he stopped dead for about as long as it takes to read a sheet twice and then went on.

The second time he had it wrong was when he turned his head too far and the wind got into the ear he could hear with.

She got off that gallery at about the sixth hour and she got to the range at the top of the stair about two hours after that, and four people were in that range, and one of the four was the officer holding this floor, who had come up from the sixth floor by himself along the same gallery nine hours earlier with a flat bar under his arm and had been sitting against the fire since about the third hour saying nothing.

About nine feet of the stair below the second landing was under for about seven hours, and during those seven hours the lowest nine steps were the only way down that nine hundred yards of place had, and about four men who were in that range worked out that fact at about the seventh hour and about five of them had worked it out before the sixth.

The wind came off the low field and went through the gap in the loose stone at the top, and about nine people on that place have said that a gap in a wall with nothing in it is not a wall, and about four have said that a gap with nothing in it is a door that has been left where it fell.

Then the river went down about a foot between the sixth hour and the ninth, and four men went down and got the wedge out of the underside of the sixth floor and put a plank under it, and the floor of that place was still there on the twentieth of Mudmonth and on the twenty-first and on the twenty-second.

There were four people in that range. They had all been in that weather before.

One of them had been in it about nine years ago and had been about nine hundred miles off and had read about it after. One of them had been in it at the time and had not been able to leave. One of them had been in it and had got out on a cart. One of them had been in it and had carried somebody out of it and had put that down afterwards and had not been asked to take it up again.

The four of them said nothing about it. Nine people on that place have worked that out since, four have said that they were not going to be the one to say it, and five have said that a night is a night.

Four of them knew exactly what the weather was on that Monday and four more of them knew what it was about, and none of the four said anything, and the fifth of the four was him, and he said nothing either until the fire was nearly down.

He said it then, and it was about the weather and it was the last thing said in that range on that day.

“**The water over that low field on the nineteenth of Mudmonth is the highest it has come in the eleven years I have been coming up this stair, and four people in this room have heard that sentence before tonight and five of you have not, and I would rather the five of you had not heard it from me.**”

That was sixty-eight words. Four of the four people in that range heard all of it and five heard the end of it, and the river made it unnecessary to answer, and nobody did, and the fire was raked at about the tenth hour and the range was shut.

**And the cost of that Monday is about four hundred yards of water over a field and nine inches in the lowest nine feet of a stair nine hundred yards long, and ninety feet of gallery in the dark with about nine inches of standing water on it and a bar in one hand and a lamp in the other, and a girl of twenty-four got off that floor at about the sixth hour because a man with one ear turned his head the wrong way about nine hundred times and got it wrong twice. One night on which four people in one room said nothing about a weather they had all been in. A river loud enough to cover the sound of a floor being opened from underneath. Two hundred and forty-eight days of one man in a lower archive, and three hundred and one days of it that Monday, and nobody in that building knows that figure better than he does. Five hundred and twenty-four days of a bleed on the inside of one wrist, and it is not cured and it has no explanation and no page of its own, and four people in that range did not know it was there.**
