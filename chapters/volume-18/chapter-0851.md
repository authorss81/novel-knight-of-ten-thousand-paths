# Chapter 0851

**Goatmonth the twenty-sixth, YR 324, a Thursday and a market morning at the low end of the made road where the made road stops and the stair begins, about four hundred people in the yard at the foot of it and about nine hundred yards of stair above that, the gate at the top of the stair off its hinges since the breach and not replaced, the water stair under to the ninth step, and the shortest interval anybody on that place has been given nine hours and counting down.**

The carts came into that yard before the light and about nine of them stood in it by the fourth hour, and about four hundred people came down off the made road to buy what was on them. Nothing came up. That is the whole of the market at the foot of that stair, and it is a market in the ordinary way, with a woman selling eggs off the back of a cart and a man selling nothing at all who keeps his two hands in his sleeves, and about nine hundred yards above all of it there was no market and no road and about nine men with a warrant.

The man of thirty-two with the stick went up at the fifth hour. He had come in on the cart road the night before with a blanket roll and a bag of wadding and about nine days of work in his head, and he had said in the yard at about half past four that he would be down by dark, and about four people near enough heard him say it and about five did not.

The stair goes up in eleven flights and turns four times and does not stop anywhere. A man who cannot get off grass on the first attempt can get up it in about nine minutes if nobody hurries him and if the ninth step of the water stair is dry, and on that morning the ninth step was not dry.

The water stair is the lowest nine steps of it and it is where the run-off from the upper floors comes down, and it had been under since the wet of Thawmonth in the year before and had come up twice since and twice gone down again. That morning it stood about two inches over the ninth step and covered the tenth, and there was a skin on it that would take a boot and lose it, and the only way past was along the stringer on the left with a hand on the wall. The nine hundred yards of it have a name that four people in nine counties can now say out loud and that about five had never heard before the breach, being the name of the place itself, which is the Gatehouse, and the man of thirty-two had never once used it in four and a half years and used it that morning because there was a market in the yard below him and four hundred people in it.

He went up it sideways with the stick in his right hand and his left on the stone, and the stone was colder than the air and colder than the day, and about nine feet below him the water came over the ninth step and lay still.

What he had come up for was a door. There is a corridor off the third landing with ten plates set into the wall of it at about the height of a man's chest, and the door of that corridor was the only door between the third landing and the lower floors that a bar would go across, and it had stood open for about four days.

The bar was off the wall of the third landing where it had hung on two brackets all that time, and it is two people long, and it is ash, and it weighs about as much as two of the men who were going to hold it.

The officer holding this floor took the other end. He is a man of about fifty with one glove on and a warrant folded inside the breast of him that he has not shown to anybody in about nine days and does not intend to show again, and he took the far end of that bar without one word about it and set his boot against the wall and looked up the stair the whole time, at nothing.

Between the two of them and the wounded man there was a piece of order that took about an hour and a half.

The wounded man had been on the second landing since the day before. He is about forty and he came off the range on the sixth floor with a rail through the left side of him, and the wound had been dressed twice and was dressed again that morning with a strip of sailcloth and a kettle, and he could not walk, and he could not be got down about nine hundred yards of stair on his own feet, and he had been asking since the middle of the day before to be put on the cart road in a barrow and taken down.

Nine hundred yards of stair is not nine hundred yards of level. A barrow goes down it on two men and a third man at the head of it with a rope, and about nine steps of every one of the eleven flights have to be taken sideways, and the two men who take a barrow down do not have a hand free for the stringer.

So they did it in the order it had to be done in. The bar stayed on the wall until the barrow was on the fourth flight and clear of the door, and then two men went back up for it and carried it to the third landing and put it in the brackets, and the officer holding this floor took the far end and Aren Kest took the near end and they drove it up until the iron rang and the brackets took it.

The sound the bar makes when it is home is a hard flat note, and it carries. That one carried, and went on carrying for about as long as it takes to read a sheet twice, and about four people on that landing heard it and about five more heard it from the flights above and below, and nobody came.

Then the two of them stood with their boots against the wall either side of that door while the man who keeps the count read out what was left of the interval.

The man who keeps the count is about thirty-four and keeps the interval and the count of plates and the count of men on each floor, and he keeps all three in the same book because he was told to keep them in one book and has been told nothing else since. He reads the interval out loud on that landing at the fourth hour and again at the sixth and again at the ninth, and on that morning he read it at the fourth, and read it at the sixth, and did not read it at the ninth, because the river had come up over the low field above the second landing in the night and shortened it again.

“**Nine hours at the fourth and five at the sixth and one and a bit at the ninth, and I have written all three in the book, and the one at the ninth is the shortest this place has ever been given and I would like somebody to tell me what a man is supposed to do with a number that short.**”

That was sixty-two words. About four people on that landing heard all of them and about five heard the end of them, and the officer holding this floor did not turn round, and the water came over the ninth step about nine feet below and lay still.

A number of that size has a use and the use is written down, and what is written down about it is that the interval between one count and the next is a thing a keeper decides and about nine men on that place decide it and the whole of what they decide is when. They had been giving nine hours in Mudmonth and nine hours in Thawmonth, and about four of them would have said that nine is generous and about five would have said that nine is the longest anybody has managed.

The count is read out because a count that is read out cannot afterwards be said to have been changed, and that is the whole of the reason for it, and about nine people in nine counties have worked that out on their own without being told.

Then the officer holding this floor put his shoulder against the bar, and Aren Kest put his back to the door and pushed with his left leg because the right one would not, and the bar held, and the door held, and the corridor was shut for the length of one interval.

Two hundred and forty-eight days ago a man went down into the lower archive of that place and has not come out of it, and he is still down there. The central engine of the whole thing is damaged, and the damage is two hundred and sixty-two days old counted from the morning a clerk at the top of that stair wrote it down in a fair hand, and nothing has been done about it since and nothing will be done about it in this year and it is nobody's achievement and nobody's work.

He had about four hours of that interval and he spent three of them on the ten plates.

The barrow with the wounded man in it went down about nine hundred yards between the eighth hour and the second hour of the afternoon, and about nine men took turns at the head of it with the rope, and the man who keeps the count came down with them and wrote the time of the last flight off the turn in his book on the second landing and stood at the bottom of the stair afterwards for about as long as it takes to turn a cart twice over, looking up at nine hundred yards of it.

About four hundred people were still in the yard when the barrow came out, and about nine of them looked at it, and a woman selling eggs off the back of a cart asked the man at the head of the rope whether there was anything to carry down and was told there was not and went back to her eggs.

She had come up that made road on the twenty-second of Goatmonth with about nine trays and had sold on eleven mornings since, and about four people in that yard have said since that a market at the foot of a stair nobody has heard of is a market like any other and about five have said that the eggs went up and nothing else did. That is the shape of about nine hundred yards of stair as well as anything else.

There are ten of them, and they are set into the wall of that corridor in a line with about nine inches between them, and they are about the size of a dinner plate, and they are sunk into iron rings, and each one has a shape cut into its face and each shape is different, and the cut is deep enough to feel with a thumb and not deep enough to read with the eye at any light there is.

He had seen them from about nine feet off perhaps thirty times in four and a half years. He had never once had his hands near one of them.

What he had come up to find out was not what they were. What he had come up to find out was whether there were ten of them.

He had been saying for four and a half years that the thing at the bottom of that stair was a key made out of ten pieces and that the pieces were kept apart on purpose, and it had been his word for four and a half years and it had never once been anybody else's word, and a thing that is one man's word is a thing that one man can be wrong about. He had never needed it to be anybody else's word before, and he could not have said why he needed it now.

He went along the line with the lamp held low in his left hand and read the ten shapes by the shape of them. About four of them he would have said were roads. About six of them he would have said were not roads and had never known what they were, and about two of the six he had been sure about for years and about four of the six he had not.

He put his hand flat on the stone beside the sixth plate and did not touch the plate, and there was a cold coming off that iron ring that had nothing to do with the draught off the stair.

Then one of the ten made a sound.

It was low and it was short and it came from about the middle of the line, and it was the sound a plate makes when it drops the last half inch and takes its seat against the ring behind it, and that is a sound that carries about as far as a dropped pan does.

And it was not that sound. It was thinner than that and it sat higher in the chest when it arrived, and it came with a second noise about a quarter of a second behind it, a dry tick with no weight in it at all, like a fingernail put on the rim of a plate and drawn once.

The whole of the corridor heard the pair of them, and only one of the two was a sound that plate makes when it seats itself properly.

Then nothing came out of the ten for as long as it takes to turn a cart twice over.

He did not touch it. He stood about nine inches back from the line with the lamp in his left hand and the stick in his right and he looked at the place the sound had come from and could not tell which of the ten it was, because about four of the ten look alike at that light and he had not counted them off and there was nobody in that corridor to count them off for him.

The man who keeps the count was on the landing the whole of the time and heard both of the noises and wrote neither of them in the book. The officer holding this floor heard them too and put his weight back onto the bar and did not look at the wall, because a man who holds a door does not look at a wall while a door is being pushed.

About four hundred people in the yard at the foot of that stair went on buying eggs for about another hour and a half.

The gate at the top of those nine hundred yards came off its hinges about two years ago and has been lying on its side across the top landing ever since, and nobody has put it back on and nobody has cut a new hinge, and about nine hundred people on that place walk past it about nine times a day. There is a gap where the gate was that a man can get a barrow through sideways, and both ends of that gap have been built up with loose stone to about the height of a man's chest, and the loose stone is the only thing between the top landing and the stair.

**And the cost of that Thursday is about nine hundred yards of stair taken three times with a bad leg and a stick, and nine hours of interval shortened to about one by a river nobody argued with, and a bar of ash driven into two brackets where it cannot be got back out without two men and a decision. The central engine damaged for two hundred and sixty-two days and not mended and nobody's work. Ten plates in a corridor and one of them making a noise that is not the noise it should make, and one man who has said for four and a half years that there are ten of them and has never once had it said by anybody else. The drawer eleven inches deep in a county he is not in not opened once in four hundred and twenty days. Two hundred and forty-eight days of one man in a lower archive. About nine hundred yards of grass four days off in a county he is not in that will not come back. The use of one ear, the use of one leg, and a year, none of which is in any of it and none of which has a price.**
