# Chapter 0853

**Thawmonth the seventh, YR 325, a Monday and not a market morning and the market street of Merefen shut behind its own shutters for the ordinary reason, four days north of the place on the stair and eleven miles of made road off it, the yard of the Old Toll with the weigh-house on one side of it and the gate post on the other, and the burn nine feet under the boards of that crossing and rising a finger an hour.**

Merefen is nine hundred people and has one street of made road through it and four miles of that road outside it which is the smoothest four miles in nine counties and has been since the fifth of Wolfmonth. Nobody in nine counties has asked anybody why, and four people in nine counties have said out loud that a road that smooth was made by somebody paid by the yard and five have said that a road that smooth was made by somebody who was not paid.

The Old Toll is at the north end of the street where the road crosses the burn, and the crossing is held in the municipal trust and the trust is the guild's, and the guild has held it by a piece of writing that goes back further than anybody in that street can remember. There is a weigh-house on the near bank and nine yards of boards on the far side of them, and the boards are lifted twice a day in winter by two men and nine times a day in summer by the same two.

The bell is on the gate post at the head of the boards.

It is a hand bell the size of a fist and a half, and it has a crack across the shoulder of it that goes two-thirds of the way round, and it has a long iron staple through the crown of it driven into the post at the height of a man's eye, and it has no clapper of its own because the clapper came off it nine years ago and was never replaced. It was bought as scrap for two pence by a road warden who did not want the weight of it in a cart, and it has been on that post twenty-two winters.

The man who keeps the crossing is called Wendall Crake and he is fifty-eight and has kept it about twenty-two years, and he is the one whose name is on the minute book, and that is a thing that happened gradually.

Aren Kest came into that yard at the seventh hour on the Monday with the stick in his right hand and nine feet of cord over his shoulder and no notebook, and he had said in the street that he wanted to know about the bell, and about four people near enough heard him say it and about five did not and about nine heard about the bell in that street that week regardless because a bell on a gate post is not a private thing.

Wendall Crake took him into the weigh-house and put a lamp on the counter and did not sit down, and the first thing he did was ask him which trade he kept, which is a question nine people in nine counties have asked him and which he has answered twice before.

Then he told him about the trust for an hour and a half.

He told it well. He told it from the inside, with dates in it, and nine of the dates were the years that the minute books begin and four of them were years when the crossing was nearly taken out of the trust and two of them were years when a warden said something clever in a council and was outbid in a different council. He said that a trust is not ownership and is not a lease and is not a favour, and that a trust is the only kind of holding that outlasts the man who holds it, and four people in that room understood that at once and five did not.

Aren Kest asked him one question in the middle of it, which was who signs, and the answer was that any two of the guild do, in the minute book, at the end of a quarter, in the room at the back.

The bell came up on its own in the fourth hour of that Monday and it came up the way a thing comes up when somebody has been putting off a question, and the man who keeps the crossing got to it after half a minute of the hour and a half and did not pretend he had got to it sooner.

What he said then was the best-argued thing anybody said to Aren Kest in that town in four and a half years, and four of it has not been printed anywhere and five of it was repeated by nine people in that street inside a week.

“**A trust is a promise made about a thing by people who will not be here when it is kept, and a crossing is a thing that nine hundred people cross and four of them are here every day of their lives and five have never once thought about it, and if the trust lapses then it does not close, it only stops being held, and a thing that only stops being held gets taken by whoever is nearest, and four of the nine hundred would tell you that is what happened at the last crossing that lost its trust, and five would tell you that what happened there was a flood.**”

That was eighty-eight words. He put the minute book down on the counter when he finished it, and nine people have said since that a man who puts a book down at the end of an argument has won it, and five have said that he put it down because his hands were full.

Then Aren Kest asked about the bell.

He asked it in his own trade, which is a thing he is careful about, and he asked what it is for, and he did not ask what it does, because those are two questions and a man who asks both at once gets the answer to the one he did not want.

The man who keeps the crossing looked at the gate post for a while.

Then he said the year.

“**I signed it off the books in YR 302. I put it in the quarter book and I put nothing beside it, and I did not put a word in the minute book that year, and there is no reason in that book for the bell or the weight of it or the staple, and it went in as iron.**”

That was fifty-one words. He said it standing up, with his back to nine feet of masonry, and he said it the way a man says a thing he has said to himself four hundred times.

Aren Kest asked him what the bell does.

The man who keeps the crossing said that it was iron on a post and that it had been there about twenty-two winters and that it was not rung. He said that nobody rang it and that there was no clapper on it and had not been for about nine years, and that the guild had not replaced the clapper because the guild had been asked about the bell once and had answered.

He did not say what it does. He was asked and he did not say it, and four of the nine people in that weigh-house noticed that he did not say it and five of them did not notice at all, and the asking was let go of on purpose by the person who asked it, which is a thing four people in nine counties can do and five cannot.

There is one more thing about that bell that is on the page because it is the shape of the yard and not because it settles anything.

The minute book of that trust is kept in a press with a glass front in the room at the back, and it is a bound book of nine hundred leaves with a quarter's entry on each of four of the nine hundred, and in the entry for the third quarter of YR 302 there is a line in a different ink that says iron, gate post, two pence and there is nothing after it, and no vote is recorded against it and no minute is recorded of anybody asking anything about it.

Wendall Crake began putting his own name in the trust column three years ago, in his own hand, where the guild's name should be, and four of the guild found out a year later and five have said nothing about it. He has not yet told the guild about the line in YR 302. Nine people in that street know the year and four know the ink and about five have never been in that room.

A toll is a sum taken for a crossing and the sum at that crossing is a farthing for a hand barrow and a penny for a cart with four hundredweight in it, and about nine hundred people cross there in a week in winter and about four hundred of those are people who live within nine hundred yards of it and cross it out of habit rather than for any reason they could name, and about nine of the four hundred do it twice in a day.

The trust takes the sum and the sum pays for the oil in the two hinges and for the new boards twice a winter and for about nine feet of coping stone on the far side which has cracked and been mended three times, and it has never in about twenty-two years paid for anything else, and about four people in that guild have said that a trust which pays for oil and boards and coping and nothing else is the most honest thing in nine counties and about five have said that it is a trust with nothing in it worth arguing over.

There is one other thing on that gate post and it is worth about nine words and it is the thing he was actually in the yard for.

The bell is not on the list of the trust. A list of the trust of that crossing is four leaves long in the room at the back and it names the boards, the two hinges, the coping, the gate and the gate post, and it does not name the bell, and it has not named the bell since YR 302 because in YR 302 the bell was put in the quarter book as iron and the list was not altered.

About four people in that street have said that a thing which is not on the list is not held and about five have said that a thing which is in the quarter book is held. Nobody in that town has ever put both of those sentences in the same breath, and the man who keeps the crossing put them in the same breath in the weigh-house that afternoon and then did not look up.

There are about nine hundred crossings in nine counties and about four hundred of them are held by a trust of some kind and about five hundred are held by nobody at all and are kept by whoever stands on them that week, and about four people in nine counties have said that the nine hundred is the number that matters and about five have said that the four hundred is the number that matters.

A roadkeeper of about fifty-two came down that four miles of made road on the Saturday and counted the four crossings on it and wrote the four numbers on the back of his hand and rubbed them out before he got to the town, and about nine people saw him do it and about four of them said nothing and about five said he was a fool. He has been doing that for about nine years.

A bell on a gate post is a thing people in a county notice in a particular way. Four people in that street had said out loud, before he arrived, that the bell at the Old Toll does not ring, and five had said that nobody has ever heard it, and nine had not thought about it at all, and the two counts that matter in that yard are the four and the five.

He had been in that yard for about six hours by then and about nine feet of cord still over his shoulder, and he had heard the trust argument and the two questions and the year, and about four hundred yards of the far bank of that burn have gone down since Mudmonth in the year before and about nine hundred yards of the kerb on the near bank went with it, and nobody in that town has had the making of a road contract since.

Then, in the middle of the afternoon, in a yard with nine people in it, the bell went.

A man who works a crossing keeps about nine things in his head and the boards are two of them, and the two of them that most people forget are the gate and the water. He had said on that Monday that the boards would want lifting twice tonight instead of twice tomorrow, and he had said it about four minutes before the thing went, and about four of the nine people in that yard worked that out afterward and about five of them did not.

He had also said, in the middle of the hour and a half, that the two men who lift those boards are about sixty and about forty-one and have between them about forty winters on that burn, and that neither of them has ever once been asked to write anything down about what they do on it.

It is not a ringing. A ringing is a sound with a swing in it and this had no swing in it and it was over in about as long as it takes to read a sheet twice. It was a hard note with a crack in the middle of it, and four of the nine people in that yard turned round, and five of them did not.

There had been no service that day in Merefen and there was no hour in that yard at that hour when any bell in nine counties goes, and the two men who lift the boards at that hour were ninety feet down the burn with their sleeves rolled and neither of them moved.

About four of the nine people in that yard had stood in about nine hundred other yards and about five had not, and a bell that answers when nobody has touched it is a thing that about four people will carry home and about five will be glad not to have heard.

The yard said nothing about it for as long as it takes to turn a cart twice over. Then Wendall Crake said that the burn was up two fingers since the sixth hour and that the boards would want lifting twice tonight instead of twice tomorrow, and nine people took that for the end of it and four of them did not. He said it in about the tone of a man who has about nine things to be doing and has just done one of them out loud, and about five people in that yard heard the tone and about four heard the words.

**And the cost of that Monday is an hour and a half of the best argument anybody has put to him about holding anything, and a year in a quarter book with a line in a different ink and nothing after it, and the first time in twenty-two winters that anybody on that bridge has heard the thing he had never told the guild about, and the standing of it is now on nine pairs of ears in that town. Four miles of made road that is the smoothest in nine counties with no question asked of anybody about it. The engine damaged two hundred and seventy-four days. A man in a lower archive two hundred and sixty days. A drawer eleven inches deep not opened once in four hundred and thirty-one days. One ear, one leg, and a year, none of it entered in the quarter book of any trust in nine counties.**
