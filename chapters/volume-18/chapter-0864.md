# Chapter 0864

**Mudmonth the forty-second, YR 325, a Wednesday and not a market morning, about nine hundred yards of occupied stair between the yard at the foot of it and a corridor about four paces long with ten plates in the wall of it, the lamps lit at each end from the fourth hour, about nine men on the anchor floor, and a book on a low table under the near lamp.**

The thing about the stair is that it cannot be hurried by anybody and it is the same to a man whether he is in a hurry or not.

He went up at the fourth hour with a lamp in his left hand and his right hand behind his back, which is a thing he does in a place where he has promised not to touch something, and nine hundred yards of it took him about two hours and twenty minutes and about four people on that place counted the time by the lamps going out on the landings above him.

The river had been up about nine feet above the low field on the twentieth of Mudmonth and had gone down by the twenty-second and had not come back since, and the water stair was dry to the ninth step for the first time in three weeks, and about four of the nine men on that place had said that a dry water stair means nothing and about five had said that a dry water stair means the river is somewhere else and would be back.

There are nine men on that floor and eight of them are there because they are there, and the ninth is the man who keeps the count and he is there because it is the fourth hour.

One is the officer holding the fourth floor and he is about fifty-one and has held that floor since the breach. He came up nine hundred yards of stair on the day of it with a bar in his hand.

One is a man of about thirty-four who came up on the second of Longlight with a frying pan and nine feet of rope. He has never been on a book and keeps nothing. Four people on that floor have said that a man of thirty-four who is on nobody's book has nothing to lose by standing in a corridor. Five have said that he has the most to lose and has not worked it out.

One is a woman of about twenty-four who has kept that floor's lamps for about two years. One is a man of about forty who came down ninety feet of gallery in the dark on the nineteenth of Mudmonth and has not been down any stair since. One is a man of about nineteen who worked the low end of a chain in nine inches of water for two days and told a man of thirty-two how he was holding it.

And four of the nine are men nobody at that place can put a trade to and four of those four have been on that place since before the breach. Five of the nine men on that floor have said that the four of them are the reason the count has never once been wrong, which is a thing about arithmetic and not about anything else.

The corridor is about four paces long and about six feet wide and has ten plates in it in a line on the left-hand wall at the height of a man's chest, with about nine inches between them, and a lamp bracket at each end and a floor of stone flags with a channel cut across it about nine inches from the wall.

There is one rule about that corridor and it is not written anywhere. You do not put your hand on the wall beside a plate and you do not stand in front of one closer than about nine inches, and the reason given for the second half of that rule is that a plate takes an impression of what is in front of it and has done since before anybody on that floor was born.

He stood at nine inches. He had stood at nine inches in that corridor about four hundred and fifty days ago and had not stood closer since and about nine men on that floor had watched him not stand closer and four of them had stopped watching.

What was in that corridor on that Wednesday was not a question. What was in it was a form on a hook, and the form is a sheet of about nine inches by six with nine lines ruled on it and a head at the top with a name already in the head, and that name had been in the head since about two years before anybody on that place had heard of him.

The man who keeps the count read the nine lines out. He has read them at the fourth hour and at the tenth hour every day for about two years and about nine men on that floor have stood in that corridor every one of those times and about four of them heard the whole of it and about five heard the first line.

The nine lines are a count and the count is about intervals and about who set them and about how many crossings on the network were worked that day. That is all it is.

It is a form of nine lines and it has been read out in that corridor about four thousand times, and nothing has ever come of it except the count.

A bridgewright does not answer a question by reading it. A bridgewright answers a question by standing at the place the question is about, which is why the nine lines have a column in them for who set them and why that column has never once been left empty by a man who was not on the ground.

The nine men on that floor had been reading those nine lines for about two years in a voice that belongs to somebody else, and four of them had known for a year that it did not and five had not, and the knowing had not changed what any of them did on the fourth hour or on the tenth.

Then the officer holding the fourth floor, who is named by his office and not by his name, asked the question that had been arranged before anybody came up that stair.

He asked whether the man of thirty-two who stands at nine inches would answer the nine lines himself, out loud, in that corridor, and be recorded as the one who answers.

Four of the nine men on that floor wanted to hear the answer and five did not. Neither of them had been asked, and both of them found out afterwards which of the two they had been.

He said yes, and about four of the nine men on that floor heard the whole of the word and about five heard the end of it.

Then the nine lines were read again, one at a time, and at the first eight he read the number and the name off the form in the flat voice a man uses when he is reading somebody else's writing out loud in a place where the writing is being checked, and at the ninth line he stopped reading.

What he said at the ninth line was the answer to a question that a thing in the wall of a floor about nine hundred yards below that corridor puts nine times a day, and it is a question and not a request, and holding means answering it and holding does not mean gripping anything.

“**I am the one who answers here and I am not going to be asked twice, and I am telling this room now, out loud, that I mean to go on answering them for as long as this place is standing and nobody has put a date on that and neither am I.**”

That was fifty-three words. All nine of the men on that floor heard every one of it and about four of them had understood the whole of it before he finished the third sentence.

What it means, and it means it whether anybody on that floor can follow it or not, is that a question which is answered by one person every day for about two years without knowing that they are answering it has become a question that nobody else may answer, and that this had happened to him in a corridor of four paces and had been found out on a sheet about nine inches by six with four lines on it and no name on it.

He could not unsay it, and that is the whole of what taking them is.

Four of the nine men on that corridor worked it out within a minute and five worked it out in nine days. Nine people in nine counties have since said that the cost of answering a question nine times a day for two years is that you cannot stop answering it.

There is no door out of that corridor at either end that a man can use without answering nine lines, and there has not been one for about two years, and about four of the nine men on that floor had worked that out before he came up the stair and about five worked it out while he was standing there.

Nobody had put a date on it and he had said so in the room, and the reason nobody had put a date on it is that every person who could have put one had asked the same question in the same week, and about four of the nine men in that corridor had asked it out loud and about five had asked it of a clerk four days off.

The question was always the same and it was never the same question twice. It was whether the nine lines were being read correctly. It was whether the interval was safe. It was whether a man on the sixth floor ought to be told that the water had come up a foot.

Nine questions in four and a half years, and every one of them the same shape. The shape is that a person who can be asked is a person who has been made into an office.

Four people in nine counties have said that this is how a road gets a keeper without anybody deciding to give it one. Five have said that it is how about nine hundred crossings got the keeper they have.

Then the officer holding the fourth floor stopped the count for that hour, which is not a thing anybody had ever seen done, and the man who keeps the count took the pen off the low table under the near lamp and wrote.

He wrote it in the book and not on the form. The form stays on the hook with a name in the head of it that has been there about two years. The book is a different book, kept by a different man, about nine inches deep and about eleven inches wide with a linen cover worn through at one corner, and the entry went at the foot of a page that had nine counts on it above it.

He wrote four lines. The first is the date, which is the forty-second of Mudmonth. The second is the hour.

The third says that the man of thirty-two who stands at nine inches answered the nine lines in person and in his own voice and not by reading them off a paper.

The fourth is the name. It is in the hand of the man who keeps the count and not in the hand of the man it belongs to, and that is the whole of what is different about the two of them.

Nobody in that corridor asked him to write it and about four of the nine men on that floor watched him do it and about five had gone to the stair by then.

Four of those nine have said since that the form on the hook and the entry in the book are the same claim, and that one of them is a forgery and the other is a record.

Five have said that the difference between them is a hand and that is all it is. Both of those are right and neither of them is what happened.

What happened is that he took the form off the hook and folded it once and put it down on the low table under the near lamp, next to the book, and pushed it towards the man who keeps the count, and did not take it back, and the man who keeps the count put it under the book and not in the book.

There is a custodian of every crossing on that network and each of the nine men on that floor keeps one.

Four of them have said that a person who keeps a road is the only kind of keeper there is. Five have said that a person who keeps a road for somebody else is a person who is about to be replaced by somebody who keeps a road for himself.

He had known what taking them was before he came up that stair. He had known it for four and a half years in the way a man knows the depth of a river he has walked into every morning.

It is the thing everybody wanted. Nine people in nine counties had wanted it in writing, and about four hundred places writing the same paper out by hand this winter had between them found no way to ask him for it that did not also give him the answer.

It is the one thing he has ever been wanted for that nobody has ever had to put in a document to get.

**And the cost of that Wednesday is about nine hundred yards of stair taken twice up with a bad leg and a stick in about four and a half hours, one question answered at the ninth count in a corridor of four paces that cannot be taken back, one sheet of about nine inches by six taken off a hook and put under a book and not into it, and about nine men on one floor who heard a man say out loud that he means to go on doing it. About four people in that corridor who understood the whole of it inside a minute and about five who took about nine days. The engine damaged three hundred and thirty-eight days and nobody mending it. Three hundred and twenty-four days of one man in a lower archive and he is not in this room and was not in that corridor and may not be. Nine hundred crossings on the network and about nine thousand and none of them has a keeper this corridor knows about. One ear, one leg, and a year, none of it in the book and none of it in the four lines.**
