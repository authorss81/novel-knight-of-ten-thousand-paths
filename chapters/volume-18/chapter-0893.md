# Chapter 0893

**Wolfmonth the tenth, YR 325, a Saturday and a market morning, the court room above the guild hall in a town of about nine thousand people, nine benches, about forty people in it at about the ninth hour, and the first sitting of that court since the fourth of Longlight.**

A man went down into a lower archive four hundred and ninety-three days ago and has not come out of it, and nobody in that room asked about him and four of the forty people in that room have not thought about him once.

Nobody thanked anybody in that room either, and four of the forty people there had been ready for a vote of thanks and about five of the forty had been ready for the opposite.

The room is above a guild hall and it has been used as a court room since before anybody in that town was born, and about forty people were in it and about nine hundred were in the street below it and about four hundred of those had heard nothing at all.

The benches are nine and the fourth from the far end has a cushion on it that has been there since the ninth of Harvestmonth, and four of the forty people in that room had looked at the cushion and about five had not, and nobody has ever moved it because nobody has ever wanted to be the fourth bench.

The forty people are about nine clerks, about four people from the trade that mends the roads, about eleven from the council that pays for the hall, and the rest of them are people who came to sit where they could see a face.

About four of the forty people in that room had wanted a trial and about five had wanted nine lines, and neither of those wanted a judgment, and about nine hundred people in nine counties will be told what happened in this room in about nine words each.

The thing that was done in that room was not a trial. It was a judgment, and the difference between the two was written on the face of the record before anybody spoke, and about four of the forty people in that room had not known there was a difference and about five had known it in advance.

That is the whole resistance of that Saturday and it is not a resistance anybody put in the way. It is that the occupier's trial is ordered and has not been held, and that a room which hears one of them is going to hear the other one wrongly, and that about four hundred people in nine counties are going to hear about this room from nine words each.

A man of about fifty with a seal on his chain stood up at about the ninth hour and said the thing out loud so that it would be in the minutes, and four of the forty people in that room agreed with him and five did not.

What he said was that a judgment read in a town where a trial is ordered becomes the trial in about nine days, and that nine days is about as long as it takes a thing said in a market to become what it is not.

She answered him in one sentence and then let the minutes show that she had answered him.

What she said was that the order for that trial is on the record of that court already, with a date on it, and that a judgment with a date on it cannot be mistaken for a trial by anybody who reads both, and that the nine days belongs to the people who will not read both.

About four of the forty people in that room agreed with that and about five had wanted the finding to be read without a date on it at all, and about four have said since that the date is the whole of the difference and about five have said the date is a formality on a thing that is not formal.

Then she read the order out, which is four lines long and is the order made in the corridor nine hundred yards up a stair, and about nine of the forty people in that room heard every word of it and about thirty had heard it in a lane in about nine weeks.

Then the woman with a court mandate and no army read the finding out, and her name is Lysa Fenn and she has been in this manuscript since an early year of it, and four of the forty people in that room knew that and about five did not.

She read four findings and gave each of them a number, and the numbers are one, two, three and four, and the first of them took about nine minutes and the fourth of them took about four.

The first finding is that the culling was ordered by the occupier and by the council that sat with him, and that the order is in the record of that council and has been since the year it was written.

The second finding is that the man named in this record held the outer bridge under orders that were incomplete when they reached him, and did not reach the inner city, and four of the forty people in that room had wanted that finding split into two and about five had wanted it in one piece.

The third finding is the one that costs, and it is that the same man afterwards withheld his own field notes from the outer bridge for about nine years and repeated the official fever account in about eleven debriefs in nine counties.

The eleven debriefs are named in that record by the date and the room and not by the people in them, and four of the forty people in that room had wanted the names and about five had wanted the dates, and the dates went in and the names did not.

The first of the eleven is the ninth of Mudmonth of the year of the culling and the last of them is the fourth of Harvestmonth of the year after, and four of the forty people in that room had not known that the last one was that late and about five had known and had thought about it for a year.

Nine of the eleven were written down by other men. Two of them he wrote himself, on the same morning, about an hour apart, and four of the forty people in that room wanted to know what was in the hour between and about five did not ask.

What was in the hour between was nothing that anybody can now prove, and that sentence is in the record in those words, and it is the only sentence in nine lines that anybody in that room argued about at length.

About four of the forty people in that room had not known that count and five had known it and had read it somewhere, and one of the five was the man who wrote it down in a book in a corridor nine hundred yards up a stair in about the ninth hour of a day in Embermonth.

Then the field book was put on the table, and that was the physical of that Saturday, and it cannot be undone by anybody who was not in the room.

It is ninety leaves of a bridgewright's day book and it is his own hand, and four of the forty people in that room had seen four pages of it before and five had seen none of it.

He carried it in himself and set it down in the middle of the long table at about the tenth hour and did not open it, and then he said that four pages of it did not agree with the fever account and that he had not sent those four pages in.

The book is about nine inches by six and it has a marbled board cover that somebody has re-covered twice, and four of the forty people in that room had seen a copy of it in nine counties and about five had not seen one at all.

It is a day book, which means it is one line a day and nothing else, and about four hundred lines are in it and about five are illegible and about nine of them are in a hand that is not his because a man of about forty-eight kept that crossing for a year in YR 318.

That is in the book too. He did not take it out and he was not asked to, and four of the forty people in that room had wanted the nine lines out and about five had wanted them left where they were, and they were left where they were.

The clerk asked whether the whole book was going in and he said the whole book, and four of the forty people in that room had expected him to hand over four pages and about five had expected nine lines, and he gave the whole of it.

“**I wrote these on the ninth of Mudmonth at the fourth hour with the water coming over the second gallery and I did not send them, and then I said the word fever in eleven rooms in nine counties for nine years, and every one of those eleven rooms took it from me and not from the council. That is the whole of my part in it and I am not going to stand here and make it smaller.**”

That was seventy-nine words and every person in that court room heard all seventy-nine of them, four of them carrying every clause of it and five only carrying the first fifty.

She read the four pages into the record at about the eleventh hour and the clerk wrote them out and about four of the forty people in that room had wanted them summarised and about five had wanted them read as they stand.

What came out of it is that he did not know what the culling was for, and that his silence helped make the lie durable, and four of the forty people in that room had wanted those two findings joined and about five had wanted them printed in that order with a space between.

They were printed in that order with a space between, and about four people in nine counties have said since that a space between two findings did four and a half years of work that no speech did, and about five have said a space is not a finding and it is not.

He had a version of that morning in his head in which he was asked nothing, and he gave that version up somewhere between the ninth hour and the eleventh, and four of the forty people in that room had watched a thing go out of a man and five had not been watching him at all.

What he wanted, for about nine years, was to be asked nothing about the ninth of Mudmonth, and four of the forty people in that room have said since that he is the only man they have ever met who wanted to be asked less and got more, and five say that wanting to be asked less is not the same as having wanted less.

Then the judgment itself, and it took about four minutes and it is in nine lines and the ninth line is the one he will keep.

His knighthood is restored and his full back pay is restored, and both of those are restored as matters of record and not as a favour and not as an honour, and four of the forty people in that room had expected those words to be said first and about five had expected them to be said last.

The ninth line says that the court does not return him to active command while the Lantern Guard holds the same unreformed powers it held on the day of the culling, and four of the forty people in that room had wanted that line struck and five wanted it printed in those words.

The back pay is nine years of it and it goes to him and not to anybody else and four of the forty people in that room had wanted it to go to the households of the dead, which is not a thing a court can do and about five had known that before it was said.

What was said about it, by the clerk, in nine words, is that a sum of money has a person in it and a record is where the person is written down, and four of the forty people in that room had not thought of a roll of pay as a document and about five had.

Nobody asked him whether he wanted it and four of the forty people in that room had wanted somebody to ask him and five had known that asking him would have been the ninth line again in different words.

Then four other judgments were named in the same morning, and each of them is on its own acts and on nothing else, and four of the forty people in that room had come for those four and five had come for his.

They are Rell and Halven and Sabin Dain and Iselde Senn, and four of the forty people in that room knew all four names and about five knew two.

Sabin Dain is a woman and a Field Warden of the order that carried the cull out, and she surrendered on the twenty-second of Longlight, and her surname is not shortened in that room and was not shortened once.

What she is judged for is stated in four lines and it is that she carried an order into a street and obeyed it and then said in a debrief that the order had been ambiguous, and about four of the forty people in that room had read that line before and five had not.

“**I will not be sorry in this room and I will not be sorry anywhere, and I would like it entered that I did not ask anybody to be sorry, and if that is a thing to be judged then it is judged and it is not the worst of the nine days.**”

That was fifty-three words and about six of the forty people in that room heard all fifty-three of them and five heard the first twenty.

Rell is judged for what he wrote in a quarter book and Halven is judged for what he did not answer and Iselde Senn is judged for what she let be carried out of a room she was standing in, and four of the forty people in that room had wanted the four findings read at length and about five wanted the four names and four lines.

What none of the four was judged for is anything the man in that record did, and that was argued for about nine minutes and it was settled on the face of the record in one sentence, and four of the forty people in that room had wanted it argued longer.

He stood up at about the twelfth hour and did not argue with the sentence.

Then he sat down, and four of the forty people in that room have said since that the sitting down was the part they would remember, and about five have said they remember the standing up because they were behind him.

Nobody in that room told him he had been right and four of the forty people there had wanted to and about five had understood that telling him would have made the ninth line into a tenth.

The court then read the four other judgments, and it did that in one sitting and at one table and four of the forty people in that room had wanted four rooms and about five had wanted one.

That is the whole of the physical of that morning after the book went on the table, and four people in nine counties have said that a man who stands up to say nothing has done the hardest available thing, and about five have said he stood up because he was told to and not because he chose to.

Then the occupier's trial was named in that room and was not held in it, and the order is of the twenty-fifth of Embermonth and four of the forty people in that room had brought it with them and about five had not.

Nobody sent anybody to fetch him. About four of the forty people in that room had wanted a carter sent that day and about five had known that a carter is not a custody and that a man nine hundred yards down a stair is not brought up by the post.

That room did not know where the ninth of the nine stones is and has never been told, and four of the forty people there had wanted to be told and five have not asked since.

**And the cost of that Saturday is a knighthood restored and a full back pay restored and neither of them returned to him in any office, ninety leaves of a bridgewright's day book opened in a court room for the first time in nine years with four pages of it that contradict the fever account read out in a room of forty people, four lines naming what a woman of about thirty-eight did on a street in Longlight, and a man standing up at noon and not arguing with nine lines. Four hundred and ninety-three days of a man in a lower archive and nobody in that room sent for him. The judgment was not a trial and the trial was not held. Nobody thanked anybody.**
