# Chapter 0859

**Mudmonth the twenty-second, YR 325, a Thursday and a market morning in a coast town of about nine hundred people, six rooms taken above a boat-yard along a quay by a delegation that has been in that town four days, one table and one chair and one lamp in each of the six, about nine people in the building, the tide making at the third hour, and the market in that town a street off and shut behind its own shutters by eleven.**

The six rooms were taken on the eighteenth and paid for four days in advance in a street that lets on the boat-yard, and about four people in that town have said that a delegation paying four days in advance for six rooms is a delegation that settled its own length of stay before it arrived, and about five have said it is a delegation that would rather not be asked.

They are the second floor of a store that keeps sailcloth and nothing else, and the man who owns it took the rent without asking a question of anybody and carried the six tables up himself over about nine days, one at a time, on his own back, and about four people in that boat-yard have said that a man who carries his own tables up nine days running is a man who has been paid to, and about five have said he is a man with six tables and nowhere to put them.

There is a number painted on the door of each room in white, one to six, and nothing else on any door of any of them.

Inside each room there is a table about four feet by two, a chair, a lamp with about nine inches of candle left in it, one sheet of good paper, a pen, a stop of ink and about nine sheets of the same paper folded in eighths on the corner of the table. About four of the six rooms had the window shuttered and about two had it open, and the two with it open were the ones whose writer had asked for it, and nobody in that building asked either of them why.

The rule was given in the first hour by the woman who came with the delegation and it was given once and it was four sentences long, and nobody in that building has said it again since the first morning. A writer writes his own sheet and no other. A writer does not read another table while a man is at it. A writer does not carry his own sheet out of his room unfolded. A writer does not ask another writer what he is writing.

She had gone into all six rooms on the first morning and had been in and out of most of them since, and about four of the six writers had noticed that she came in without knocking and about five had not, and she carried nothing of her own between them and about nine people in that building had seen her come out of room three with nothing in her hands on about twenty occasions and go into room six with nothing in her hands on about the same number.

He had been in that building three days when he put it to the only person in it he had a reason to put it to.

He has been the second voice for a count on nine hundred yards of stair for four and a half years, and the errand of that building was the reason for the six rooms and not the reason for anything else, and he wanted to know who had chosen six.

“**You are asking me the one question in this building that nobody has answered once in four days, and I am not going to answer it, and if you want it answered you should ask whoever hired the six rooms, and that person is not on this coast.**”

That was forty-eight words. She said it in the doorway of room three with her hand flat on the frame and she did not move afterwards and neither did he, and about four of the nine people in that building were within earshot of it and about five were not.

The six were named on the eighteenth, before any of them arrived, and about four of the six had come a long way to sit at a table in a room with a number painted on its door, and about five had come about nine miles.

Room one is a woman of about forty who has run a crossing crew for about eleven years and who came nine miles and who has not once asked what the paper is for, because she has spent eleven years being asked what a road is for. Room two is a man of about forty-four who is clerk to the third province and who carries a seal that is not his and who has read every paper his province has sent him this year. Room three is the woman from the coast.

Room four is a man of about thirty-one who has kept a toll on a ferry since he was nineteen. Room five is the man of about fifty with the fair hand who came up that stair nine hundred yards with four lines of writing on it. Room six was his.

What was in front of him on that Thursday was a sheet of good paper and the one fragment nobody else in that building could have written, because it is the only one of the six about the doing of a thing to a road rather than about a road.

A bridgewright's paper says what a road is. A province's paper says what a province may ask. A community's paper says what a community may refuse. A toll keeper's paper says who pays. A copyist's paper says how a paper travels. The one nobody can write from a desk is the one that says what has to be told to whom before anything is done, and he has been on both ends of that question for four and a half years and has never once been asked to put it down.

He had a strange four days of it in room six, and the strangeness was not the question and it was not the tide. It was that nobody in that building knew what the other five were writing, and that he found he did not mind, and that a man who has spent four and a half years being the person every argument in a room is brought to has no habit at all for a room where nobody brings anything to him.

He wrote it twice, because the first attempt put the notice in the wrong place and gave it to the person proposing rather than to the road, and he tore the first one up at the corner and dropped it in the lamp and watched about nine inches of it go brown.

At about the fourth hour the tide came up under the door of room six and about nine inches into the bottom of that room, which is a floor of stone slabs laid by somebody who thought about water about four hundred years ago, and the quay outside it went black to the bollards and two of the boats knocked their fenders off and started working at their moorings.

He could not have gone down into that water with a bad leg and he did not try it. He got his back against the door and put his left shoulder under the edge of the table and lifted it a foot off the floor, and a man of about thirty-one who keeps a toll on a ferry came down the outside stair and got the other end, and between the two of them that table stood with its feet off the stone for about as long as it takes to read a sheet twice, and a boy of about fifteen carried six folded sheets out of that room and up the outside stair in the front of his coat.

About four people in that boat-yard watched that happen from a boat and about five turned away from it, and the man from room four said afterwards that the floor of the sixth room had been under nine inches in his own time and that nobody had ever lifted anything in it, and that he had not known until that afternoon that there was a way to.

What happened at the sixth hour was that six finished sheets were folded once and carried out of six doors into a passage, and the man with the fair hand copied each of them twice in a hand that is not as good as the one it copies and folded the copies into eighths, and there were twelve sheets at the end of it, and the twelve were split between three people who were not in that building and whose names and addresses are on three slips in the copyist's own hand.

He read his own fragment out loud in that passage before he let it go, because the rule stopped at the room door and he wanted the six of them to have his words in their ears before anybody read anything.

“**Nobody may do a thing to a road without telling the two people the thing happens to, being the one who keeps it and the one who stands on it, and the telling has to come before the doing and not after it, and telling a person is not the same as asking that person, and a road that has been changed without either of the two knowing about it is a road somebody has taken who was never given it.**”

That was eighty-one words. About four of the six writers in that passage heard all of them and about five heard the end of them, and the woman from the coast heard all of them and said nothing whatever about them.

Then the man with the fair hand gave him a sheet.

He did not give it as an errand. He folded it twice, put it into the man's hand, shut that hand over it, and said that he was going up about nine hundred yards of stair on the Saturday morning and that a sheet in a coat was worth nine of them and worth nothing in a bag, and that it was the third of the six and not his own and that nobody had read it but him.

Then he went out into the street and unfolded it against a bollard in about nine inches of standing water, because he wanted to know what a man of about fifty would send nine hundred yards with a stranger.

It is a page of about eleven lines. It says a community may refuse contact. It says the refusal may be made at any hour. It says the refusal needs no notice to any other party. The rest of it is about what a community owes the people who come to it, and that is about four lines and is sound work.

He read it twice in the rain at the foot of that quay and found nothing wrong with it, and about four people who saw him standing there would have said he was reading a paper he had been handed and about five would have said he was reading a paper somebody else had handed him too carefully, and he folded it into the inside of his coat on the left where the hand goes and walked up the street to find a bed.

**And the cost of that Thursday is six rooms taken four days in advance above a boat-yard in a town of about nine hundred people, and one question asked once in four days and not answered, and a sheet torn up at the corner and burned in a lamp because the notice in it was given to the wrong party, and about nine inches of a tide locked into a stone room with a table held a foot off that floor by two men while a boy of about fifteen carried six folded sheets up an outside stair. Twelve sheets and three names on three slips. One sheet in the inside of a coat on the left. A rule of four sentences that nobody in that building repeated after the first morning and that was not once explained to anybody.**

**a stop of ink at 3, a sheet of sandpaper at 27, a cake of ink at 29, a bundle of quills at 30, a stick of wax at 31, a ream of writing paper at 32, a leather case at 34, a firkin of lamp oil at 35, a fathom of rope at 36, and a cask of salt fish at 40.** Three, twenty-seven, twenty-nine, thirty, thirty-one, thirty-two, thirty-four, thirty-five, thirty-six and forty is two hundred and ninety-seven pence. A mark is forty-eight pence and two hundred and ninety-seven pence is six marks and nine over. **This column was costed against the band searched whole one more time, and its total is taken from a page already in this manuscript rather than found fresh, on the ground that a writer left two figures standing free has chosen between them and has not worked one out.** Some of the figures in that chapter that are not in the column are one, two, four, five, six, nine, eleven, twelve, fifteen, nineteen, twenty, forty-four, forty-eight, fifty, eighty-one, four hundred, nine hundred, and four and a half.
