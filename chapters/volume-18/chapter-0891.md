# Chapter 0891

**Wolfmonth the first, YR 325, a Thursday and a market morning and the thirty-sixth first of a month in a row to come to about nine boards in nine counties with nothing standing at any of them, the weigh-house at the Old Toll in Merefen with the boards of the crossing lifted twice already that morning, and four miles of made road outside the town with the first cart on it at about the sixth hour.**

The bleed is seven hundred and sixteen days old and is not cured and nobody in that weigh-house has noticed it. A weigh-house is a room where the weight of a thing is the only argument in it, and there are nine hundred crossings in nine counties and every one of them sends a slip to somebody, and about four hundred of them send theirs to a trust and about five hundred send theirs to nobody at all.

What came into that room on that Thursday morning was a list of what is broken, and it was written on the back of four hundred toll slips because that is what the backs of toll slips are for once the week is over.

The writing was done by four people at three tables and it took until about the ninth hour, and the first hour of it was spent on what a crossing is, because in that room of nine it was four who had four answers and about five who had one. A crossing is a place where a road stops being a road for as long as it takes two people to be on other sides of something.

That was the answer that stuck and it was not his, and of the nine in that room about four wrote it down and about five heard it and did not write it. The man who wrote the last list in that town was sixty-one and he was in the room, and his name is not on this page because he did not want it on the wall and he was asked twice and said no twice.

He wrote that list four years ago and it was nineteen leaves long and it named every broken thing on nine hundred crossings, and he refused to let it be printed, and about four people in nine counties have said out loud since that he was right and about five have said he was a man who did not want his work read by people who mend nothing. He said the reason himself at about the eighth hour and he said it once.

“**You print nine hundred things that want mending and every person who reads that sheet goes looking for the one that keeps him awake, and there are about nine people in nine counties who could mend nine hundred crossings this year and about four of them have never heard of the sheet.**”

That was fifty-two words and every person in that room heard all fifty-two of them, and about four of them had it entire before he reached the end and about five stopped at the half. Then the thing that made the morning is that somebody said out loud that four hundred copies of a paper had already gone out of that county by hand in the week of the twenty-first, and nine people in that room stopped writing. The paper is not the list.

The paper is the instrument that went in force in seven places in the winter, and about four hundred copies of it went out of this county and out of eight others in the week of the twenty-first of Rainmonth in a bundle that one man carries.

He has been on the page of this manuscript since its first year and he was in that room on that Thursday, and he is the man who copies what about four hundred people in nine counties cannot pay a clerk to copy, and he has said in his own trade that the paper is theirs and not his. He did not call it a plan.

Nobody in that room called it a plan, and the word had last been used for it in a council by a man who had been outbid in a different council.

What he said about it was that it goes to a person and not to a place, that it goes in the ordinary post, and that it is handed to whoever happens to be buying string in whatever town it reaches, and that four hundred people hold a copy of it and about five hundred have heard of it from somebody who has one. There was a leather bag standing in the corner of that weigh-house with a strap round it.

It had come in on the Friday before and gone out again on the Tuesday, it is the same kind of bag in nine counties, and nobody in that room was asked to touch it. The man who carries it is thirty-eight and he has carried it up the ordinary post since Fallowmonth, and four people in nine counties have said that a bag is a poor answer to a question about distribution and five have said that a bag is the only answer anybody had.

He has never been asked what he thinks of it and has never said, and the two things he does say are that it is cheap and that a thing a clerk does for nothing is not copied twice. Then he put nine hundred names of nothing on a table, which was his way of saying that a list of what is broken and a paper about who may refuse are two different objects and that nobody in that room was going to be allowed to make them one.

About four of the nine in that room had wanted the two joined and about five had not, and nine people in nine counties have said since that the second five were right and about four have said the first four were right and neither of those was in that room.

Then a woman of thirty-one who keeps a crossing nine days off that town asked for her line to be read back to her before it went on the sheet, and nobody in that room had thought of asking a keeper anything before the paper was full. Her line said that the coping on the far side of her crossing is cracked and that the crack is eleven feet and that it has been mended three times.

She mended it in Harvestmonth of this year and she has the stone receipt for it in a bag under her counter and about four of the nine people in that room went and got a third of the nine people sent to find out whether it was mended.

It was mended, and he struck the line through with a pen and wrote the date of it in the margin, and five people in that room watched a line come off a list and about four had not known that was allowed. What she wanted was not to be praised for it.

What she wanted was for four hundred people in nine counties not to be told in a printed thing that there was eleven feet of crack to mend at a crossing where there is no eleven feet of crack. Then the second column went on the sheet, and it is the number of keepers, one for each crossing, and where there is a name against it and where there is a trade and a street and nothing else.

That column is short by about five hundred and the shortness is the whole of what that Thursday found out. About four of the eleven at that table had expected the second column to be full and about five had known it would not be, and the four who expected it had wanted the gaps closed on the sheet by writing in whoever stood there that week.

A woman of forty-four who sells lime two days off that road then said the thing that stopped that argument, and she said it in the trade she has used all her life, which is that a hole in a road is not a hole in a road until somebody who lives on it has been told there is one.

“**A gap on a sheet in a town nobody has read is a gap on a sheet, and a gap on a sheet that four hundred people have read is a hole in the road outside my gate, and I will tell nine households on this street about eleven feet of coping before the twelfth hour and you may print that I said so.**”

That was sixty-four words and about six of the eleven people at that table heard all sixty-four of them and about five heard the first thirty. Then a keeper refused to be in it, and the room had not expected that and had expected the other thing instead.

He is fifty-four and he keeps a crossing eleven days off that four miles of made road, and he came in at the ninth hour on a market morning because somebody in that room had written his trade against his crossing and had not written his name.

What he said was that his name in the second column would be a promise he had not made to anybody, and that the day somebody at that crossing stopped turning up the column would still have him in it, and a person who cannot be asked anything about it is not a keeper and is a name.

That is his own trade and it is about forty words, and it went onto the back of a toll slip in the margin because nobody in that room had paper of its own.

The second column went on the sheet with the trades in it and the names out of it, and about four people in nine counties have said that a sheet of nine hundred lines with about five hundred gaps in it is the most honest thing anybody has printed this year, and about five have said it is a list of work and not a record.

He did not say anything about that, and he has said very little about anything printed in four and a half years, and about four people in nine counties have noticed that a man who can be asked is not a man who explains.

Outside that door the market had gone on in the street the whole of the time it was being written, and about four hundred people in it had walked past nine feet of blank door and five hundred had not come that way at all, and the burn under the crossing had been up a finger since the sixth hour.

The press is in that town and it has stood in a room over a lane for about nine years and it can pull a sheet in about as long as it takes to walk to the end of the burn and back, and that morning it pulled nineteen. The man of sixty-one stood beside it and did not help and did not go out, and about four of the eleven people at that table had wanted him to help and about five had known he would not.

He had brought the nineteen leaves of his own list in a satchel and had not opened the satchel, and when the second column was finished he said he would read his out on a day when somebody was going to the press anyway. Nobody in that room asked him to and nobody asked him not to, and that is the whole of what passed between him and the two columns.

He has spent four and a half years being the person a road is opened through, and the whole of what he wanted out of that morning was a nail in a door, and of the eleven at that table about four understood the difference between those two things and about five heard it as one thing.

He had wanted the sheet on that door and not in a cart for about four years, and about four and a half years before that he had wanted it in neither, and about five people in nine counties have said a nail is worth more than a cart and about four have said a cart is worth more than a nail.

He carried the first one out himself and put it on the outside of the weigh-house door at the tenth hour with four nails he had brought for it, and about four hundred people saw that happen in that street before the eleventh hour and about five of them asked what it was. There is no law about a sheet on a door.

That was said out loud in that street on that morning by a woman selling nothing in particular, and four people agreed with her and five said a sheet on a door is the oldest thing in nine counties and there is no law about a sheet on a door. A sheet on a door in Merefen is read by people who are standing still and not by people who are passing, and that is the whole of the argument for a nail.

Nine feet of door and a street that fills at the eighth hour and empties at the eleventh is nine hundred feet of reading if the sheet is on it, and four hundred people in that town pass that door twice a day and about five hundred pass it once a week.

What nobody in that room worked out is that a sheet sent in a bundle goes to one person and a sheet nailed to a door goes to everybody, and that those are two instruments doing one errand, and of the eleven at that table about four had noticed that and about five had not. Nobody thanked him for the nails. Four people in that street had put money on somebody saying something and five had put money on nobody saying anything, and the five were right.

There is a thing about a gap printed on a wall that four people in nine counties have said out loud and about five have not, and it is that a gap is only a gap until somebody in the town it is printed in has read it and gone and looked at it themselves. That is what the second column is for.

It is not a record of who keeps what and it is not a list of blame, and the six people who had spoken about it were split three and three over whether a town fills it or a Tuesday fills it. Then the last line of that sheet is the one nobody in that room could do anything about, and he read it out before the sheet went up.

The nineteen sheets were to go out with the bundle on the Friday, and the bundle goes to a person and not to a place, and about four hundred of those nineteen sheets would be read by somebody who had asked for nothing at all. The fourth crossing on the made road out of Merefen is sound. Its boards were renewed in Fallowmonth, its coping is not cracked, its hinge is oiled, and the two men who lift its boards have between them about forty winters on it.

There is nothing at it to mend. There is also nobody standing at it. It is the crossing four hundred people use in a week on their way to about nine towns, and the toll on it has been nothing since YR 302 because a warden wrote that a road which is not a trade pays no toll, and the trust it is in has never had a penny to give a man who stands there and counts nothing.

The warden who wrote that rule is not known to anybody in that room and of the eleven at that table about four had read the minute of it and about five had not, and what the minute says is four lines long and is that a crossing which is not a trade is kept by whoever uses it and pays nothing to anybody. That has been the standing of that crossing for nine years.

Nine hundred people go over it in a week, about four hundred of them live inside four miles of it, and about nine of those cross it twice in a day and have not noticed that they have. He read that line twice and put his hand flat on the sheet after it, and about four of the nine people in that room heard him read it twice and about five had not known there was a reason to read anything twice.

Nobody in that room asked him what a road owes anybody, and that question had been standing in nine counties all year, and about four of the eleven at that table had wanted to put it and about five had known in advance what it would cost the room.

**And the cost of that Thursday is about nine hundred lines printed with about five hundred gaps in the second column, nineteen sheets off a press in a town over a lane, four nails in a weigh-house door, eleven feet of crack taken back off a list in a pen's stroke, a man of fifty-four's name left out of a column by his own instruction, and a fourth crossing on a made road that is sound and that nobody can mend and that nobody can close. The bleed at seven hundred and sixteen days. Nobody in that room was thanked and nobody in that street asked him what the sheet was for.**

**a quill at 5, a sheet of foolscap at 6, a wick at 7, a candle at 8, a ball of twine at 9, a stop of ink at 10, a sheet of blotting paper at 11, a sheet of oiled paper at 12, a bundle of quills at 13, and a pound of wax at 15.** Five, six, seven, eight, nine, ten, eleven, twelve, thirteen and fifteen is ninety-six pence. A mark is forty-eight pence and ninety-six pence is two marks and no pence over, and the working out of the mark was done on its own account and not out of the sum. **That figure is carried over from an older column and it was not arrived at here, because the band still has a pair of values open in it and a pair of figures settles nothing between itself.** Figures in that Thursday that the column does not carry are four, three, sixteen, nineteen, twenty-one, twenty-two, twenty-four, thirty, thirty-one, thirty-two, thirty-six, thirty-nine, forty, forty-eight, fifty-four, sixty, sixty-one, three hundred and two and seven hundred and sixteen.
