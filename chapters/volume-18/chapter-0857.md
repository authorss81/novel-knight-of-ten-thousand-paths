# Chapter 0857

**Mudmonth the first, YR 325, a Thursday and a market morning with about four hundred people in the yard at the foot of that stair, the short interval five hours, the thirtieth first of a month in a row to arrive at that place with nothing standing at it, the river gallery on the second storey under about nine feet of water at the sixth hour, and the anchor floor one storey above it.**

The river came up on the night of the twenty-eighth of Thawmonth and it did not go down on the twenty-ninth, and nine people in that place watched it come and four of them had seen it come before and five had not.

A gallery is a room that runs along the outside of a building and takes the water off it. This one runs about ninety feet along the second storey of that place with about nine feet between the outer wall and the inner one, and it is floored with stone flags laid to a fall of about an inch in nine, and at the low end of it there is a grating and beyond the grating a chute, and the chute was blocked.

That is the whole of the flood and it took about nine hours.

Somebody had put a hod of brick and four lengths of scaffold pole into that chute on the twenty-eighth, and whether that was done to stop the water or by somebody who did not know there was a chute is not known to anybody on that place, and about four of the nine men who worked the gallery said it looked done on purpose and about five said a man carrying a hod at night with a pole under his arm does not stop to think about a grate.

At the fourth hour on the thirtieth of Thawmonth the water in that gallery was up about four inches on the flags and moving. At the sixth hour on the first of Mudmonth it was up nine inches and had stopped moving.

Nine inches is not much. Nine inches in a room about nine feet wide is about four hundred gallons in about ninety feet of gallery, and the way out of it is through a door at the high end which is nine inches wide at the jamb and about five feet six off the floor, and a man goes through it one at a time and sideways and nine of those went through it on the first day and four went through it on the second.

A gallery has one rule of its own and it is older than anybody on that place. Nothing heavy goes into a chute and nothing long goes in at an angle. Nine men who have worked a chute for twenty years will tell you the rule is about the chute, four of them will tell you it is about something else, and five will tell you nothing at all.

That is how the gallery was flooded. Four lengths of scaffold pole went in at an angle on the night of the twenty-eighth by somebody in a hurry in the dark, and about four hundred gallons of a river came up over a floor laid to a fall of an inch in nine because of it, and the four lengths of pole were the only thing anybody ever found in the way.

They cleared it by hand and there was nothing else to clear it with.

That is what two days of that work was. Nine men, about nine hours a day, on about nine feet of water in a room about nine feet wide, dragging brick and scaffold pole towards the high end in a chain of about four people, passing it hand to hand, and putting it out through the door at the high end onto the floor above the water stair.

The high-end door is four floors up from the yard and about nine hundred yards off it, and everything that came out of that gallery went up four flights and along a landing and out through a gap in loose stone at the top of about nine hundred yards of stair.

On the first day the officer holding this floor took the high end and a man of thirty-one who is not on his book took the second place in the chain, and Aren Kest took the low end, because the low end is where the thing has to be put down and not where it has to be picked up. Four of the nine men on that place would have said that a man with a bad leg should not be at the low end of anything, and five said nothing at all.

On the second day his left leg went.

The right one is the bad one and has been for about nine years and he has a plan for it, and the plan is a stick and a wall. The left one had held a chain position for about nine hours a day for a day and a half and it went at about the eleventh hour of the second morning, in the middle of a hand-over, with a hod of brick in it.

He went down on the flags in about nine inches of water and the brick went about nine feet and he was up before the second person in the chain had turned round.

He was not carried out and he was not helped up the stair and he sat on the step of the high-end door for about as long as it takes to read a sheet twice with his left leg out straight, and four of the nine men in that gallery came to the door and five went on with the chain, and the man at the low end was about nineteen and had been on that place for about nine months.

He has been on that place about nine months and he came up that stair on the second of Longlight with a frying pan and about nine feet of rope and a place on a list, and he is not on any book and he is not a keeper of anything and four people on that floor have said that a man of nineteen who works the low end of a chain for nine hours a day is a man somebody is going to lose, and five have said that he will lose himself and that this is not the same loss.

The man of about nineteen said one thing, and it is put down because it is the only thing anybody said on that floor about that fall and because about four people have since said that it was the correct thing to say.

“**You are holding it wrong. It wants taking at the bottom and not at the middle, and you have been taking it at the middle since the fourth hour, and if you take it at the bottom your leg is not in it.**”

That was forty-three words. He got up on the fourth try, on his own, with the stick, and went back to the low end and worked the rest of that day in a chair, which is what a man does at the low end of a chain.

One man is on a list of the injured and the list is kept by the bridge crews and not by anybody on that place, and nine people in nine counties have seen a copy of it and four have noticed that the list has a column for a keeper and that five names in it are keepers of something, and four hundred and thirty people in nine counties are keepers of something.

The count of anchor fragments did not move. It is ten, it was ten on the twenty-sixth of Goatmonth, and it was ten on the first of Mudmonth, and about nine men on that place counted it at the fourth hour and at the tenth hour on that day as they count it every day and about four of them wrote the ten in the book and about five did not.

About four hundred people were in the yard at the foot of that stair on that Thursday for about four hours and about nine of them watched nine men come down about nine hundred yards with nothing in their hands, which is a thing a yard full of people notices, and four of the four hundred said out loud that the water was coming up in the building and five said that nine hundred yards is a long way to carry nothing down.

The anchor floor is one storey above that gallery and the gallery's outer wall is the anchor floor's floor, and when the water in the gallery is up about a foot the water is within an inch of the underside of the plates that are in the wall above.

Then they cleared the chute, which was the last four hours of the second day and the worst four hours of it.

A grate is about four feet by two and a half and it is set in a rebate in the stone floor and it comes up in four pieces, and four men got it up and nine feet of scaffold pole came out of it with a length of brick and a lump of mortar the size of a man's fist.

The water went out of that gallery in about four hours and it went out through a chute about nine inches wide over about nine feet of fall and it can be heard from the floor above for about as long as it takes to read a sheet twice, and nobody on that floor has ever got used to the sound of it and about nine of them have never stopped noticing it.

Then the last thing on that day was measured, because a man who works a gallery measures it, and it was put on the back of the tally stick in chalk, in the trade hand of a man of about thirty-four who keeps the count.

The water was not measured before it went and nothing on that floor has been measured since, which four of the nine men who were in that gallery would have done differently and five of them would not.

The tally stick of the man who keeps the count has about nine notches cut in it for the interval and about four inches of it flat at the end, and the flat part is chalked on about twice a week and washed off by the rain on the landings where it is carried, and nine people on that place have seen the flat part with writing on it and four have read it.

Water standing in a place it has no business standing in, at nine inches, in a room with a channel cut nine inches from the wall, is nine inches deep against a wall nine inches from the channel, and four of the nine men on that floor had thought about that and five had not.

**And the cost of that Thursday is about four hundred gallons of a river out of a room it has no business being in, and a leg that gave out on the second morning and not the first, and nine hours a day for two days on about nine feet of water with brick and a scaffold pole carried by hand through a door nine inches wide at the jamb. One man nineteen years old told him how he was holding it. One name on a list of the injured that the bridge crews keep and not anybody on that place. Ten anchor fragments where there were ten on the twenty-sixth of Goatmonth and the water still coming up the outside of a wall about nine hundred yards from a yard where about four hundred people bought what was on nine carts. The engine damaged two hundred and ninety-seven days. One ear, one leg, and a year, none of it on the tally stick.**
