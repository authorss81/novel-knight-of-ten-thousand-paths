# Chapter 0852

**Thawmonth the first, YR 325, a Tuesday and not a market morning and no market in the yard at the foot of that stair, five days after a bar of ash was driven into two brackets on the third landing, the year turning at that first and nobody on that place turning anything with it, the anchor floor one storey above the corridor of ten plates, and the interval six hours and falling.**

Two hundred and forty-eight days ago a man went down into the lower archive of that place and has not come out of it, and it was two hundred and fifty-three days ago on that Tuesday, and about nine people in that building could have told you the figure and none of them would have said it first.

The anchor floor is one storey above the corridor and it is a long room with a low roof and no window in it that anybody has found. It is lit by two lamps kept burning from the fourth hour to the eleventh, because the plates in the wall below want a steady light, and the plates in the wall below are the reason the floor exists, and about nine men on that place have said that about the lamps and about five of them said it as a complaint.

There are nine of them on that floor. He counted them at the door on that Tuesday, because he had counted them at the door on each of the five days before it. There were nine, and the tenth place in that row is a rectangle of bare stone about nine inches by nine inches where the wall was never cut, and about four people have said the stone was put there on purpose and about five have said it was put there because the tenth plate came out of another place and did not fit.

The nine men on that floor are not guards. Two of them keep the lamps, four of them keep the count, and the other three turn the plates through their cycle on the fourth and the tenth hour, which is the one thing anybody on that floor is allowed to do to a plate, and which is why nobody has ever found a plate out of its ring with a mark on the face of it.

What a plate does when it is turned is not asked about. A man with a leather glove turns it about a quarter of a revolution and takes the glove off and puts his hand in his coat, and the next man comes and does the same, and about nine hundred times in a year that happens on that floor, and about four of the nine men have said out loud that they do not like it and about five have said that not liking it is not an argument against doing it.

The nine men on that floor had never once put nine and ten into the same breath. He had been saying ten for four and a half years, and the man who keeps the count had said nothing at all about it in about two years, which is a long time to say nothing about a number you read out loud every four hours to about nine people.

What he wanted on that Tuesday was the second voice.

He did not put it as a question, because a question can be refused by a shake of the head and what he wanted was not that kind of answer. He asked the man who keeps the count to read out the plate count, because that man reads out counts on that floor at the fourth hour and at the ninth hour and has read that count out every day for about two years without one word of comment on it.

The man who keeps the count brought the book up. It is a fair copy with a canvas cover and about nine hundred leaves in it, and about four hundred of those leaves are empty, and he opened it on the low table under the near lamp and turned to the page the plate count is on and read it out. Then he read out the last four days of it as well, because a man who reads four days is not afterwards asked where the fourth came from.

Then he shut the book, and he stood up with it under his arm, and he said the other thing.

“**Ten on the plates and ten in them, and I will say it out loud because you have never once had it said by anybody but yourself. Ten is not a key. A key is one thing that a person turns, and these are ten things that have to be held by ten people at once, and if one of the ten lets go then the other nine do not close into a key, they stop, and about nine men on this place have been told that in words and about four of them wrote it down because they were told to.**”

That was a hundred and two words. About four people on that floor heard all of them and about five heard the end of them, and the officer holding this floor was not on that floor and did not hear any of it, and the two of them were alone except for about nine men on the floors below who could have heard a shout and did not hear one.

The man who keeps the count looked at the bare rectangle of stone in that wall for about as long as it takes to turn a cart twice over. Then he said that he was not able to say the rest of it in a room, and would say it on a stair.

They went out onto the fourth landing and he said the rest of it there with the river about nine hundred feet under them and coming up.

He said that the count of the ten is the count of the last private keeping arrangement anybody has heard of, and that a count is not a name. He said the arrangement wants a name the way a lock wants a key, and that there is a form in the book for the person who confirms the ten, and that the form has been filled in since before this county knew the place existed.

He did not say whose name was in it. He said he was not going to say whose name was in it on that stair, and that he would say it to one person and not in a room, and that the one person was not going to like it. He said that about four people on that floor had offered to guess at it out loud in about nine days and that he had told all four of them to stop guessing, and that one of them had been the woman who keeps the count and she had taken it as a slight.

Then he asked Aren Kest for a piece of chalk.

There is chalk on that place because about nine men carry a stub of it in a coat for marking stone, and this man had one in the fourth pocket. He turned the book over on the table and wrote nothing on it. He took out the tally stick from the same pocket, which is a strip of wood about nine inches long notched for the interval, with about four inches of one end of it flat and worn, and he wrote the date on that flat part in chalk in his own trade hand, about four inches wide and an inch high.

A tally stick is not a document. It has no second copy, it is not filed anywhere, it cannot be read by anybody but the man who cut it, and when he loses it the thing it says is gone and nobody can prove it ever was there.

The date on it was the first of Thawmonth of the year that had begun the day before. It was the twenty-ninth first of a month in a row to arrive at that place with nothing standing at it, and about nine people in nine counties keep that count, and it is not begun again anywhere, and it is not a count of anything that place did or did not do.

The chalk went back into the fourth pocket. The book went back under the arm. He went down to read out the interval and did not say any of it again that day, and nobody on that place asked him for any of it that day.

What Aren Kest said to the empty anchor floor afterwards was short, and there was nobody on it to hear it, and it is put down here because it is the reason he went to Merefen four days later and got nothing he wanted.

“**I have been right about the number for four and a half years and I have been wrong about what a number is for, and the second of those is the one that costs.**”

That was thirty-four words. The lamps went on burning until the eleventh hour, and about nine feet below them ten plates sat in a line in a wall with a shape cut into each face, and about four of the ten looked alike at that light and about six did not, and one of them had made a noise on the Thursday before that was not the noise it makes when it seats itself properly.

There is a crossing at Merefen four days north where a guild holds it in the municipal trust and where a road warden bought a cracked bell as scrap for two pence about twenty winters back and hung it on the gate post. That is a whole other matter and it has nothing to do with a plate, and about four people in nine counties had said out loud in the last four years that the guild crossings are the only crossings anybody in nine counties knows anything about, and about five had said that knowing a thing is not the same as being asked about it.

He went north on the fourth because a thing held in trust by other people for twenty years is the only kind of holding he had not yet been inside, and because a man who has spent four and a half years being asked to confirm things has about nine days left in him before he starts saying yes to the wrong one. He took no notebook and no roll and the stick and about nine feet of cord.

Then the rest of that Tuesday went on the way a Tuesday goes on in a yard at the foot of about nine hundred yards of stair when there is no market in it. Nine people were in that yard and no more than nine, and four of them went up to the second landing to look at the water and five stood at the bottom of the stair and looked at the top of it.

The woman who brings the trays up on Saturdays had left her cart at the top of the made road on the Monday because of the wet and had come down on the Tuesday to fetch it, and she and a man of about sixty who sells nothing but rope stood under the wall for three hours doing nothing that either of them would call work. Rope is nine feet of it on a coil and she had four coils of it stacked on the tailboard in order of size, and he had nine of his own on the ground at her feet in order of size, and they did not trade once in three hours.

She wanted a market on a Tuesday. That is the whole of what she wanted and she said it out loud once, in her own trade, to the man who sells nothing but rope, and he disagreed with her and said that a market that comes when it is called is not a market, and she said that a market that comes when it is called is a market that a man can starve by, and neither of them said anything else about it, and about four hundred yards of the made road above that yard is the smoothest in nine counties and has been since the fifth of Wolfmonth, and nobody in nine counties has asked anybody why.

About nine hundred people go up and down that stair on an ordinary day. About four of the nine hundred live in the place, and about the rest come up for reasons of their own that nobody has asked anybody about. A gate came off its hinges about two years ago and lies on its side on the top landing, and the gap where it was has been built up to about the height of a man's chest with loose stone, and about nine people a day go past that stone and about four of them put a hand on it as they go, which is a habit nobody taught them.

There is a market at the foot of that stair on Thursdays and Saturdays and on no other morning of the week, and on the Thursdays it has about four hundred people in it and on the Saturdays about nine hundred, and the difference between the two is a man who comes up from the coast road on a cart on the Saturdays with about nine trays of something. About four people in that yard have worked out that the Saturday is a coast market wearing a Thursday's name, and about five have said that a market is a market.

The water came up over the second landing at the eighth hour and stopped four inches short of the ninth step of the water stair. It came up again at the tenth hour and stood two inches under it, and it went down a foot by the second hour and stayed there, and four people on that floor decided that the river had made its choice and five went on watching it.

A hundred and ten yards below the second landing the water stair has a lip cut across it at the ninth step, and the lip is nine inches wide and about four inches deep, and somebody cut it there a long time before the plates existed. There is no writing on it and there is no one alive who is known to have cut it, and on that Tuesday four men of the nine came down and looked at it for about as long as it takes to turn a cart twice over and went back up without saying anything to each other about it.

The bar on the third landing was not touched that day. It is two people long and it is ash and it is in two brackets, and nine men on that place know it is there, and four of them walked past it and looked at the brackets to see whether they had moved, and they had not moved, and that is the whole of what a bar of ash did between the first of Thawmonth and the second of it.

He went down the nine hundred yards at about the fourth hour of the afternoon with the cord over his shoulder and about nine men on that place watching him go, and the officer holding this floor said one thing to him at the second landing, which was that the corridor had been shut for two days and had not been opened and that nothing had come at it.

It is put down because it is the last thing anybody on that place said to him about that door before the fifth of Thawmonth, and because about four of the nine who watched him go had already worked out that he was not coming back up on the Friday and about five had not.

**And the cost of that Tuesday is a thing said for four and a half years by one man and said out loud by a second one, and neither of them able to name whose name is in the form, and a name left out of a room worth more than a name read out in one. Ten plates and ten pieces in them and no key out of them unless ten people hold all ten at once. The engine damaged two hundred and sixty-seven days and nobody mending it. A bar of ash in two brackets. Two hundred and fifty-three days of one man in a lower archive. A drawer eleven inches deep in a county he is not in not opened once in four hundred and twenty-five days. One ear, one leg and a year, none of it entered in any of the ten books that place keeps.**
