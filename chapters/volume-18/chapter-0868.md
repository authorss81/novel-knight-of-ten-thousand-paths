# Chapter 0868

**Longlight the fourth, YR 325, a Thursday and a market morning in a town of about nine hundred people, a toll bar at the low end of the one street of that town with about four hundred people in the street behind it by the eighth hour, the bar down, and the crossing shut on a Thursday morning for the ninth time in four and a half years by somebody who has never once shut it on a Thursday morning.**

That town of about nine hundred people has one street of stone about four hundred yards long and a market at the low end of it and a bridge at the top of it that was built about nine hundred years ago by somebody for the same reason the lime road in the valley was built, being that there is lime in that country and there is water in it and the two go together.

The market is held on Thursdays and Saturdays and about four hundred people come down that street on a Thursday between the eighth hour and eleven and about nine hundred of them come on a Saturday. About four people in that town have said that the bar is the reason the Thursday market is smaller than the Saturday one and about five have said that the bar is the reason both of them are held where they are.

About nine hundred crossings on the network run through towns of about nine hundred people, and about four people in nine counties have said that the crossings in towns that size are the ones nobody thinks about because nothing happens at them and about five have said that nothing happening at a crossing is what a crossing is for.

The woman who keeps that bar is about thirty-eight and has kept it for about nine years and has known him for about nine years and neither of those is the reason she shut it.

The reason she shut it is on nine boards in nine counties and it is not a complicated reason.

A crossing cannot be opened on one man's word on the strength of a paper that four hundred places have not signed. A man who can open a crossing on his own word is a man whose word is the road.

Four people in that town have said that since the breach and five have said it for four years.

He came into that town at the sixth hour with nine crossings still to look at on a list of nine and a half.

He came down that street on foot, because a cart in that street stops for anybody. Four people standing at the bar saw him before he was at it and five did not.

The bar is ash and nine feet long and it drops into two brackets cut in the stone at the low end of that street.

It has been the same bar for nine years and it has a wear mark about four inches up from the left-hand bracket where nine thousand hands have gone over it.

She put her hand on it before he got to it.

That is the whole of what she did. Four of the four hundred people in that street saw her do it and five were looking at a stall.

She said it in her own trade and she said it out loud in the street and she said it to four hundred people, which has not happened to him in four and a half years and is the whole of what that Thursday was for.

“**You can tell me the road is open and I will believe you, and you cannot tell me the road is open and open it, because the day a man can do that is the day a man who is wrong can do it, and about four hundred people in this street will be stood behind that bar and about nine hundred will not.**”

That was sixty-four words and every one of them was heard by four hundred people in that street.

Four hundred people in that street went on what they were doing afterwards and five of them stood still.

She has kept that bar for about nine years and she kept it before her and there has been a bar at the low end of that street for about nine hundred years, and about four people in that town have said that the wear mark on that bar is nine years old and about five have said it is nine hundred and that a thing a cart has gone over can be worn about nine hundred years and nobody has ever been here to check.

She is the third person in this manuscript who has refused him something to his face and she is the first who has done it in a street.

About four people in nine counties have said that a man who is used to being the only person in a room who knows what a road is doing is not prepared for a street, and about five have said that no man is prepared for a street and that it is a thing about four hundred people find out at once and cannot be argued with afterwards.

What he has never had to do is be refused and stay. He has been refused nine times in four and a half years and every one of the nine was in a room with about nine people in it and every one of the nine was about something he could have gone away and thought about. Nobody had ever refused him in front of about four hundred people who all went on what they were doing afterwards.

Then she said the better of one argument, and it was not the argument about the paper and it was not the argument about the bar.

She said that on the Wednesday a man of thirty-four had come through that street at the ninth hour and had gone up nine hundred yards of stair and had answered nine lines out loud in a corridor.

She said the whole of that town had heard it by the second hour of Thursday morning, because a carter carried the story down four miles of made road in one night. A town of nine hundred people had learned in nine hours that the man who opens the roads had put his own name to something.

Four of the nine hundred had believed it and five had not.

She said that a crossing that is kept by somebody a place chose is a crossing that place can shut on a Thursday morning, and that a crossing kept by a man four days off with nine crossings on a list is a crossing that place cannot shut on a Thursday morning, and that she had shut it because she wanted to find out what would happen when somebody did.

Then she stopped, and she was right, and about four hundred people in that street heard her be right.

What he did about it was four things. Four of the four hundred people in that street saw all four and five saw two.

He did not argue with her. He did not ask her twice. He did not say that a carter had carried a story and that a story is not a record. And he did not ask to stay.

What he did do was take out of the inside of his coat on the left, where the hand goes, a bundle of about nine sheets of his own hand going back about four and a half years, being every interval that bar has been given and every hour it has been down and every week in four and a half years that about four hundred people have stood behind it.

He had kept them because he kept them.

Nobody had ever asked him for them and there is no form anywhere that they go on. Four people in that town have said a man who keeps four and a half years of a bar in his coat is a man with a claim. Five have said he is a man with a record.

He put the bundle on the stone beside the bar and squared it against the kerb with the heel of his hand, which is what everybody who works at a table does without being told.

He did not say a word about it and he did not take it back.

Four of the four hundred people in that street watched a man of thirty-two give four and a half years of his own handwriting to a woman at a bar. Five watched a man put a bundle down.

Then the road refused to open for him twice and no explanation was offered to him either time.

The first time was at the seventh hour.

He put his left hand on the bar about four inches above the wear mark and he lifted it as far as it goes, which is nine inches. It came up nine inches and stopped.

A bar that drops into two brackets is a bar with two things to lift it out of, and about nine years of ash in a wet country moves a bracket about a sixteenth of an inch in that time, and a sixteenth of an inch is nothing at all in a gate and is everything in a bar.

It is why a gate is lifted from the end and not from the middle. It is why about nine thousand hands go over a bar at about four inches and not at the end of it. It is why a man with a bad right leg and a stick in his right hand cannot open a gate in that town by himself, and about four people in that town have said that he never could and about five have said that he has never tried in front of anybody.

A bar that stops at nine inches has nothing behind it that has refused anything. It has a bracket, and a bracket that has been worn round by about nine years of ash and weather is a bracket that a strong man can lift a bar out of and a bad-legged man with one hand cannot.

Four of the four hundred people in that street knew exactly what that was and five did not.

The woman who keeps that bar said nothing about it and neither did anybody else.

He tried it again at the eighth hour. The man of thirty-one with the lime and nine other people were standing at it, three of them offered and four did not.

It came up nine inches and stopped again.

Nobody in that street said anything about it the second time.

That is the part four hundred people have said since they cannot get past. Nine of them had offered to help and the rest had watched, and not one of the four hundred said a single word to a man of thirty-two at a bar in nine minutes.

The list of nine crossings was in the inside of his coat on the right where the hand does not go, because the hand that goes on the left had been full since the seventh hour, and he did not take the list out in that street and about four people in that town have said that a man who does not take a list out of his coat in a market street is a man who has decided something, and about five have said he has a bad coat.

Eight of those nine crossings are in nine counties. The ninth is four miles up the same road and it has a keeper that place chose in the second week of Mudmonth and nobody has told him anything about it and he did not ask.

He was not refused by the road. He could not lift a bar out of a worn bracket with one hand and a stick, and everybody in that street who watched it knew that, and nobody said it, and about four people in that town have said since that a street which will not say a thing out loud is doing the refusing and about five have said the street did nothing at all and that the bar did it.

He stayed in that street until the market shut behind its own shutters at eleven.

Four hundred people went past him and five of them said good morning. He stood nine paces off the bar for most of it with the list of nine crossings in his coat and four and a half years of somebody else's bar on the kerb.

What about four hundred people in that street did afterwards is a fact about a market and not about a bar.

They bought what they had come to buy. About nine of them asked the woman who keeps the bar whether she was going to be all right and about five of them asked her husband and about four of them told him what had happened before he got home, and about nine hundred people along about four miles of made road have heard about it and about nine hundred have not.

Then he went up the street and got his horse at the far end of it.

The woman who keeps that bar had gone inside and had shut her own door behind her. Four people in that town have said she shut it to be let alone. Five have said she shut it because she had said what she came to say.

**And the cost of that Thursday is a bar down at the low end of the one street of a town of about nine hundred people on a market morning with about four hundred people behind it, a refusal said out loud in a street in seventy-one words by a woman he has known for nine years, one argument given to him and not argued with, four and a half years of his own handwriting squared against a kerb and not taken back, and a bar lifted twice about nine inches and stopping both times with about nine people standing at it and not one of the four hundred saying a word. Nine crossings still on a list of about nine and a half and none of them done. Eight where there were ten. One ear, one leg, and a year, and one of the three is the reason the bar came up about nine inches and neither of the other two is anybody's business.**
