# Chapter 0863

**Mudmonth the thirty-eighth, YR 325, a Saturday and a market morning in a main-world town of about nine thousand people, a room above a court at the low end of that town's one street of stone, nine chairs and a long table and about nine people in it, the market outside that room going about four hundred strong from the eighth hour, and the interval eleven hours.**

The third province tried three forms of it before the fourth hour and all three of them were refused in that room and one of them was refused by the woman of about forty-four in nine words.

The first was that a refusal lapses unless it is put again within nine days. About four of the nine people in that room said that a refusal that has to be renewed is a licence and about five said it is worse than a licence, being that a licence can be taken back and a refusal cannot.

The second was that a refusal does not count unless it is put in a form the receiving party accepts. The man of about forty-four who is clerk to the third province refused that one himself, and he refused it in his own trade and about four of the nine heard it and about five heard the end of it, and what he said was that the person who writes the form is the person who refuses.

The third was that where a refusal affects a road used by parties outside the community, the outside parties may ask for it to be put to a person named in the fourth column. That one is the clause. It came out of a room four days off the coast where a woman had ridden four days and come back with a curved iron and no writing, and nobody in that room above the court was told that and about four of them worked it out anyway.

What nobody in that room said out loud on that Saturday is worth putting down once and only once, because it is the thing the clause does and not the thing it says.

A column with a name in it is a place where a refusal goes to die in a piece of paper. A column with nobody in it is a place a community may fill, and a community that fills it with a person of its own choosing has done the one thing this paper exists to let it do.

A clause is the smallest thing a paper can carry and it is the only thing a paper can be refused over.

That is the whole finding of a room above a court on a Saturday morning, and it was not found out quickly and it was not found out by anybody who wanted it.

The room above that court is on the first floor and it is reached by a stair of about nine steps that turns at a landing, and the floor is of boards that have been planed once and have not been planed since, and a long table in a room nine paces by five has four people at it comfortably and about nine people at it the way a thing is done.

The town's one street of stone is four hundred yards long and about nine hundred people come down it on a Saturday between the eighth hour and the eleventh. The market at the low end of it is the reason the town is a town.

A court sits over a room like that because the court was built first and the market came to it. About four people in that town have said that a paper discussed above a market is a paper that can be argued with by anybody who buys anything at all. About five have said that it is a paper that can be argued with by nobody, because nobody who buys anything at all comes up nine steps.

The nine people in that room on that Saturday were the woman of about forty-four. The man of about forty-four who is clerk to the third province. Two men of the provisional council who are on no road and have no office except the one they came with.

The delegation's woman from the coast, who had come up that road on the Wednesday. The man of about thirty-one who keeps a toll on a ferry, who had said something on a Thursday in a room over a saddler's concerning a fourth place nobody had asked.

A man of about thirty-eight who is an officer of a court that has a mandate and carries no army, and who had nine written things on him and no soldiers. And him.

There were two others at that table who are worth putting down because neither of them spoke and both of them are the reason that clause is thirty-seven words.

One is a man of about sixty who keeps a counting house at the top of that street. He has counted about nine hundred tons of something a year for about forty years.

When the clause was read out he said one thing, which was that a rule which can be carried further than it was meant to go is the only kind of rule that has ever cost him money.

The other is a woman of about twenty-two who came in with the officer of the court and sat down at the end nearest the door. She wrote down every word that was said in that room.

About four of the nine people there have said that she was the reason the clause is thirty-seven words, because she wrote out a longer version first and then wrote this one underneath it. The two of them are both in her book and only one of them is in the paper.

What was wanted on that morning was one sentence. Six writers had produced six fragments in six rooms above a boat-yard and a join between the third and the sixth of them had been found and could not be mended, and the province that most needed the refusal wording had not sat on what it had been sent, and what the room above that court was for was to find out whether a paper with a hole in it can be carried at all.

It can. The finding on that Saturday was that a paper with a hole in it can be carried and that a paper with a hole in it and one sentence to stop the hole travelling is carried further than a paper with a hole in it and nothing.

About four of the nine people in that room wanted the sentence to be about notice, being that the third fragment already says a refusal may be made at any hour and needs no notice to any other party. About five wanted it about the person on the far side of a refusal.

Both of those wanted different sentences and neither of them wanted the sentence that got written.

The woman of about forty-four who writes out what other people have to agree to had been in and out of that room twice in three days. She had read all six fragments in a copy and had been given the fault in a letter of nine lines.

She came in on that Saturday with nothing written. She wrote it at that table between the fourth hour and the seventh, in front of everybody, and about four of the nine people in that room said afterwards that watching a person think is a thing that cannot be done. About five said that it had been done in that room for three hours and that the only sign of it was that she turned three sheets over and put two of them face down.

He had his own version and he had brought it to that table in the inside of his coat.

What it said is that where a community refuses contact, no other party may treat the refusal as a closure of the road against it, and the sentence is four lines and it is good and it does one thing and not the other.

It was read out at about the fifth hour and about four of the nine people in that room said it was the sentence. About five said it was half of the sentence.

The half that was missing was the half that says what happens to the refusal on the ninth day.

A refusal that cannot be questioned is a closure. Every one of the nine people in that room said so within nine minutes and none of them said it twice, and the man of about forty-four who is clerk to the third province put it in a form that went onto the table and stayed there, being that a refusal which nobody may ever look at again is a road shut by the people standing on it, and that this is the thing the paper exists to prevent.

Then he was asked for his version again, and he gave it, and then the woman of about forty-four asked him one question about it in her own trade and she asked it quietly and about nine people in that room heard it.

She asked whether a person who refuses is on the inside of the refusal or on the outside of it.

He said the person is on the inside.

She asked whether the person can be on the inside of a refusal and be told about it afterwards.

About four of the nine people in that room thought he would say yes and about five thought he would say no.

He took about as long as it takes to turn a cart twice over and he said that a person is on the inside of it and on the outside of it at the same time. He said that this is the whole of what is difficult about a road and that he had been on both ends of about nine of them in four and a half years.

He has been the name in a column three times in four and a half years and each of the three was put there by somebody else.

The first was a tally on a bridge in a county he is not in. The second was a line in a return that a man of about thirty-four with a clerk's hand filled in without asking him. The third is a form with his name in it that four true sentences in the right order on somebody else's paper put there about two years ago, and about nine men on a place about nine days ride off read nine counts a day out of it.

He has never once put himself in a column, and on that Saturday in a room above a court he was asked to do the opposite of that and he did it, and what he did was take a pen out of a woman's hand and put it down on his own side of a table and push it back.

Then the woman of about forty-four wrote a different sentence, and she wrote it in about nine minutes and she read it out before anybody had agreed to it and she read it in her own trade and about four of the nine people in that room heard all of it and about five heard the end of it.

“**A community that refuses contact has not thereby closed anything, and the refusal may be questioned only by the one person named in the fourth column, and no question of the same refusal may be put twice.**”

That was thirty-seven words and it is the whole of what that clause says and it is thirty-seven words long because a clause that is longer than a person can hold in their head is a clause that a province can carry further than it was meant to go.

About four of the nine people in that room heard what it did and about five heard what it did not.

What it did not is in that room and is not in anybody's mouth. It is a clause on a refusal, and about nine people in that room could see that the person named in the fourth column had the better of anybody on the far side of it. About four of them had worked out by the ninth hour who that person was going to be.

He was the obvious one. About four of the nine people in that room had worked that out by the ninth hour and about five had worked it out before she finished the second sentence, and nobody argued on it, because there was no version of that clause in which the person named in the fourth column was anybody else.

He was the tenth of ten. He was the one that the thing in the wall of a floor about nine hundred yards below a corridor of ten plates had been asking a question of nine times a day since before this county knew the place existed, and he had not known that for about two years and had found it out on a sheet about nine inches by six in a corridor of four paces.

The woman of about forty-four did not put his name in the fourth column and she did not put anybody's. She ruled the column and left it empty, and she said in the room that a column with nobody in it is a column that can be filled by the place it concerns, and about four of the nine people in that room heard that and about five did not.

What she did instead was take the pen off that table and hold it out across the table to him, and he did not take it, and about nine people in that room watched a man not take a pen.

Then he took the pen out of her hand and put it down on the table on his own side of it and pushed it back across, and that was the whole of what he did to that clause and about four of the nine people in that room said afterwards that he had been asked to sign it and had not and about five said that nobody had asked him to sign anything and that he had been asked to write it and had not.

A column with nobody in it is a column that can be filled by a person nine people will agree on later.

The room above that court on that Saturday found out that a paper does not need a name in it to have a name put in it. About four of the nine people in that room have since said that this is the difference between a paper and an arrangement.

About five have said that it is the difference between a paper and nothing at all, and that both of those are true and that the finding of that Saturday is in the second and not the first.

**And the cost of that Saturday is four lines of his own that were good and were not the four lines that were needed, three hours of a woman of about forty-four thinking at a table in front of nine people, one column ruled and left empty and one man not taking a pen, and nine hundred people in a street outside going about four hundred strong from the eighth hour without knowing that any of it happened. A refusal that cannot be questioned is a road shut by the people standing on it, said out loud in that room by a man of about forty-four who carries a seal that is not his and who has been right once and knows it. A copy of six fragments read in about four minutes by a person who was not in any of the six rooms. One join about two inches long still unmended and one sentence of thirty-seven words that does not mend it and stops it travelling. The interval eleven hours and holding. One ear, one leg, and a year, and none of the three is in the fourth column.**
