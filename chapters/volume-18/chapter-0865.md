# Chapter 0865

**Mudmonth the forty-fifth, YR 325, a Saturday and a market morning in a crossing house over a toll gate on the fourth mile of a made road that runs about nine miles between two market towns, about nine people in that room before the light and about four hundred on the road above it by the seventh hour, the gate bar on its drop and a fire in the grate, and the interval ten hours and rising.**

Nothing went wrong that morning and that is the whole report.

He had come up the made road on the Thursday with a lamp and a list of about nine crossings on it and had got as far as the sixth one by the Saturday, and he was in that room at about the third hour because the man on the sixth had sent word up the road that there was a thing at the fourth mile worth standing in the rain for.

There is a thing about a crossing house and it is not a narrow thing either.

A crossing house is a room with a fire in it over a gate. The man who keeps it is awake at the third hour and asleep at the ninth. The road above it is nine miles of made road between two towns that both of them buy everything from the other.

That room had a bar on its drop and about nine feet of daylight between the two posts of it, and the bar is ash and about nine feet long and it drops into two brackets cut in the stone, and it weighs what ash weighs when it is nine feet of it and wet.

About four hundred people went over that crossing between the fourth hour and the seventh hour of that Saturday and about nine hundred went over it a fortnight before, and about four hundred people in two market towns have since said that about four hundred is the ordinary number and about five have said that it is not and will not say what it is.

What it was, on that Saturday, was that about nine thousand crossings on the whole network were worked that morning and that they had all been worked by somebody, and that nobody had to be told to do any of it.

That is the whole of the win and it is not a narrow win either, though about four people in nine counties have called it a good week and about five have said that a week is not the unit.

He had come in from the north on the Thursday with the lamp and the list and a bad leg that had been grinding since the fourth mile, and he had gone to the dairy first because the dairy is four miles up the road and the dairy woman had sent word down to a carter and not to anybody else.

The carter had brought the word on the Saturday at about the second hour. He had put it in the mouth of the crossing keeper and had not put it in the mouth of the man of thirty-two.

Four people in that room noticed that and five did not. The carter has said since that he did it on purpose, because a thing a man has been told about by a carter is a thing he can hold, and a thing a man has been told about by a keeper is a thing he has to answer for.

The list was nine crossings on one side of the paper and about nine more on the other, written in a hand that is not as good as the hand it copies, and it was not a list of work. It was a list of places where somebody had to stand and look and say what they saw, and about four of the nine on that Saturday had already done four of them and about five had done none.

He had been in that room about an hour when the interval came through on the fourth, and the man who keeps the crossing read it out of a book the way a man reads a thing out of a book when he has read it out four thousand times, and he read it out and then he read the same line again because he did not believe it.

“**Ten hours at the fourth and ten at the sixth and eleven at the tenth, and I have had that gate shut in front of me on a Saturday morning for about two years and I have not had one hour of it that was not somebody else's fault.**”

That was forty-nine words. About four of the nine people in that room heard all of it and about five heard the end of it, and the man who keeps that crossing is a woman of about forty-four and has kept it for about nine years and she said it sitting down with her hands flat on the table.

The gate bar came off its drop at about the sixth hour and he took one end of it and the keeper took the other, and that is the way it is done on that road because the bar is nine feet of ash and a man of thirty-two with a bad leg cannot lift nine feet of ash off a drop on his own.

What it is to stand on a made road at the fourth hour with nine feet of ash in your hands is a thing that cannot be put in a paper and is not put in one.

The weight arrives in the shoulders first and in the back of the knees four seconds later, and the second person lifting the other end is not a detail of it. Without the second pair of hands the bar does not come up clean. It comes up crooked, and a gate that comes up crooked is a gate that comes down on somebody.

Nobody in that room said that out loud on that Saturday. About four of them had said it out loud before and about five had not, and the man who has kept that gate for about nine years said it on the Thursday of that week to a carter and got told by the carter that nobody had ever heard it before.

What winning is, on a morning like that, is a room in which nothing has to be argued about.

He had waited four and a half years to be useful in a room where something was wrong.

What he got on that Saturday was a room in which four people said four true things about a gate and a boy said a fifth. Every one of the five of them was right. Not one of the five needed him to say anything, and four of the nine people in that room understood that he had been put somewhere he was not needed about two hours before it happened.

He was not unhappy about it and he did not say so. Four of the nine people in that room have since said he looked like a man waiting for a bucket that was not coming. Five have said he looked like a man who had been let off something.

Then four people said the thing that had happened, out loud, in that room, and they said it in four different trades and none of them was in the same trade as anybody else.

The keeper of that crossing has kept it nine years and the name before hers is not on the gate and her own name is not on the gate either, and about four people in two market towns have said that the name on a gate ought to be the name of the person who opens it and about five have said that a gate with a name on it is a gate somebody can be replaced from.

She keeps a book. It is a ruled book of nine columns and about eleven hundred entries and she has kept it since the second of Longlight of the year before last, and it has an interval column in it and a weather column and a column for what she was told, and about four people who have seen it have said that the middle column is the only one anybody ever reads.

The keeper of the crossing said the road was open at the fourth hour for the first time in two years, and she said it kindly and she said it about the road and not about anybody.

A man of about thirty-one came down the made road with a load of lime and had been sitting on a bench outside since the second hour, because there had been no queue to sit behind. He said that nine thousand crossings had been worked that morning, that he had counted four hundred vehicles go over that one, and that he had counted them twice.

A woman of fifty-eight keeps a dairy four miles up that road and had come down once a week for nine years. She said she had brought eleven hundred pounds of butter down that road every Thursday for nine years and had never once been able to say when she would be allowed through. On that Saturday she had been through at the fourth hour and had been home before the fire was lit.

And a boy of fifteen, apprenticed to a wheelwright in the town at the low end of that road, said the thing nobody else in that room had thought to say.

He had counted nine thousand crossings. He did not know how he knew. He had counted them because a man had told him to, and he would be doing it again next Saturday whether he was told to or not, and about four of the nine people in that room said that was the most useful sentence said in that room all day and about five said it was a boy being pleased with himself.

Four of the nine people in that room thought the boy's number was a good number and five thought it was a number a boy had.

The man of thirty-one with the lime said that four hundred crossings is what this road does on an ordinary Saturday, and that nine thousand crossings across nine counties with the network at ten hours is what the same four hundred comes to when you add up everything that is not here. Neither of those was the same thing and four of the nine people in that room did not argue about it.

The strain went down that morning and it went down by a measurable amount.

The measurement is in the interval, which is the only thing about that place anybody has ever been able to put a number on.

The interval had been eleven hours at the sixth hour on the thirty-eighth of Mudmonth. It was ten hours at the fourth hour on the forty-fifth and eleven at the tenth, and the number in that place had been eight hours and holding on the twenty-ninth, and about nine men on that floor had been reading those numbers out loud twice a day for about two years and about four of them had never seen the eighth one.

The thing about a number going the right way on a day when nothing has gone wrong is that nobody can find the person who did it. There is no name to put on the eighth hour and no sheet that says anything about it and nobody had to be asked about it and nobody was asked about it.

What did it was a Wednesday.

On the Wednesday before that Saturday, in a corridor four paces long on a floor nine hundred yards above a yard, a man of thirty-two answered nine lines out loud at the ninth of them in his own voice instead of reading them off a paper. A man of thirty-four who keeps a count wrote four lines in a book in his own hand. A form came off a hook and went under the book.

Nobody in that room in the crossing house knew any of that and nobody has told them, because the ninth hour of that Saturday in that room was about four hundred people and a fire in a grate.

The nine thousand is a number and not a count. About nine thousand crossings were worked on the network on that Saturday morning at about the fifth hour, being about nine times what an ordinary morning does, and about nine hundred of those nine thousand are on made road that a cart can use and about eight thousand of them are not, and the eight thousand are the ones that take people and records and neither of them ever goes on a cart.

Four people in nine counties have said that the eighth thousand is the whole of what this manuscript has been about. Five have said it is a number and not a count, and that a number which cannot be checked is not a record, which is the sentence the woman who keeps a room over a weigh-house has been saying for two years.

**And the cost of that Saturday is about nine thousand crossings worked by about nine thousand people who were not asked and none of whom will ever be told what they were part of, about four hundred vehicles over one gate between the fourth hour and the seventh, and a boy of about fifteen with a number he cannot justify and intends to make again. Nine feet of ash lifted by two pairs of hands at the sixth hour, and eleven hundred pounds of butter home before the fire was lit. An interval at ten hours and rising, and a count that went the right way on a day when nothing went wrong, and no name on it. Nobody mended the engine and nobody came out of the lower archive. One ear, one leg, and a year, and neither of them was in that room.**
