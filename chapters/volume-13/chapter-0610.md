# Chapter 0610

**Frostmonth the seventh, YR 320, a Thursday and a market morning four days off, the tenth open crossing, the sixth hour, a lime kiln on a slope, a question asked out loud in a working voice, and no answer.**

She was on the bad mile and a half before he was and she was on it again because it was a crossing day, which is the only reason there is, and because the water was off the stones, and because the woman of about sixty at the top had said the word for it and had said nothing about any month or any day or any hour of the day, and had not said it twice.

Aren Kest came up the lane at the fourth hour and found the kiln cold and the draw-off open and a barrow of stone stacked against the wall, and he waited, because there is no way to hurry a kiln, and Nell Craddock came over the top of the slope at about the fifth hour with a rope over her shoulder and a boy of about eleven behind her carrying a wedge, and they had come down together from a cut about a quarter of a mile above the bad stretch, and neither of them had sent for anybody.

“**You have not asked me anything,**” she said, when she saw him. “**I have been four days waiting to see whether you would.**”

“**I did not want to be asked,**” Aren Kest said.

“**I know,**” she said, “**that is why I have not asked and that is why I have come anyway, and I will say what I came to say and then you can do what you like with it.**”

The boy of about eleven drove the wedge into the stone and knocked it in with a sledgehammer, and the stone came apart into about four pieces and the smallest piece rolled a little way down the slope and stopped against a barrow wheel, and neither of them looked at it.

She said it in her own voice and with a spade in her hand, and it is the same voice as the one that has been coming out of her mouth for four days and more and is not the oldest voice in that place, because there is no oldest voice in that place, because nobody there has ever agreed on one.

“**A man in a market town four days off has told a room there are seven roads and one of them weighs nothing,**” she said, “**and I have not heard him say it and I have heard that he said a number was wanted, and I have come up this mile and a half with a boy and a rope to tell you that I know what the seventh one is, and that I am not going to stop you, and that you are going to be asked for the number by somebody with a page, and that when you are asked I am not going to be here.**”

“**You are here now,**” Aren Kest said.

“**I am here now,**” she said, “**and I have said the thing and it is out, and it is out in the sun on a slope with a boy in earshot and no wall on this side, and I did not want to say it with a wall.**”

Then she said the thing that is the question of this book, and she said it in a working voice, and she was not looking at him when she said it, and she was shovelling stone into the barrow while she said it.

“**Do you want it.**”

The boy of about eleven stopped with the sledgehammer up. He did not know what the two words meant in that sentence and he would not have known for about nine years, and he stood with the hammer up for about as long as it takes to turn a thing over once.

“**Do you want it,**” Nell Craddock said again, and did not stop shovelling, and “**because the second I know, I can stop bringing you things. That is the first half and it is the whole of my reason and I have thought about it for four days on a slope and I have not found a better way of putting it. The second half is that there is a woman about nine days off who has been stood down nine days for a page with your name at the top of it, and she does not know it was your name, and she came four days of carting to tell a room that nobody could find her, and she has a slate in her apron with nineteen years on it, and she is not on the page, and neither am I, and there is nobody on that page at all except you.**”

He did not answer her.

He has a stick in his hand because he has had one for about eleven years and he was standing on a slope above a lime kiln with a bad mile and a half behind him and a boy of about eleven in front of him, and there is nothing at all difficult about answering a question of two words. He has answered harder questions in worse rooms. **The reason he did not answer is that she had not come four days of carting to be answered and he understood that on the slope, and because the answer he had was a yes, and a yes said on that slope at the sixth hour to that woman would have made him the seventh thing on the page she had just described, and because a man who has been the only person who can see the load-bearing problem in a place three times now has been right three times and has never once been asked whether he wanted it, and the asking had come, and he had about nine seconds of a slope in which to say so.**

The sun came over the ridge. The boy of about eleven put the sledgehammer down in the grass and went to the top of the kiln wall and tipped a bucket of water out over the edge, and the water went down the face of the kiln and out over the draw-off and away in the sun, and all three of them watched it go, and it took longer to go than anybody expected, because the ground under it is lime and lime drinks.

“**Right,**” Nell Craddock said.

She did not say it kindly and she did not say it coldly. She said it the way a woman says *right* at about the sixth hour when there is stone to barrow and about nine households waiting on lime and a woman at the top of the slope with a rope over her shoulder.

“**Then I will take the sledge up at first light,**” she said, “**and you can go down and be in a room on a market morning, and I am not going to say it again and you are not to write to me about it, and if a page comes with a number on it that somebody took out of my mouth on a slope, I will know, because I will know that I did not say it.**”

“**I would not,**” Aren Kest said.

“**You might,**” she said, “**and I have thought about that for four days and I have decided it does not matter. A man who writes down what a person said on a slope is doing what a room does with a page and he is doing it with a person instead of a sheet of paper, and I would rather be in the world with that than not.**”

She barrowed the first load up at the sixth hour and the boy of about eleven took the rope at the front and she walked behind with her shoulder against the load, and Aren Kest walked in front of the sledge for about a quarter of a mile because his legs knew how to and nobody asked him to, and the woman of about sixty at the top said nothing at all for the whole of that quarter of a mile, which is her way, and which is also, this time, the only thanks anybody got.

At the top of the bad stretch he stood and looked back down the mile and a half of height he had come up twice in four days, at a kiln with the fire out of it, at a boy of about eleven who could still say the count, at a woman of about fifty with a slate in her apron about nine days off, at a room with a drawer about eleven inches deep in it and a fourth head ruled and empty for about seven months, at about nine hundred sheets a month going out into nine counties and about a hundred and four coming in on a fixed day and about four hundred and nine parish rolls a month coming in off the roads, and none of the three made out of the other two, and a register with seven questions in it and no findings, and a day-book at nine, and a press standing open with no date anybody would give it, and a half leaf face down on a table in a room over a weigh-house since the sixth of Mudmonth.

The question was on the slope and nobody had answered it and the road was open and the count was in a boy.

**And the cost of that Thursday is a column and it is Aren Kest's, and a question asked out loud and not answered and a bad mile and a half of height and a quarter of a mile walked in front of a sledge are not in it and none of them has a price.**

**a wedge at eight, a sledgehammer at fourteen, a rope splicer at fifteen, a bushel measure at seventeen, a kiln rake at twenty-one, a lime hod at twenty-four, a whetstone at twenty-six, a canvas apron at twenty-seven, a sledge shoe at twenty-nine, and a file at thirty-two.** Eight, fourteen, fifteen, seventeen, twenty-one, twenty-four, twenty-six, twenty-seven, twenty-nine and thirty-two is two hundred and thirteen pence. **A mark is forty-eight pence and two hundred and thirteen pence is four marks and twenty-one over**, and two ends of a road, about a hundred paces of a district, nine counties, about nine hundred sheets a month, about a hundred and four in one band, about four hundred and nine parish rolls a month, a fourth head ruled and empty, four hands of writing, a register of seven questions, a day-book of nine entries, a drawer about eleven inches deep, about four hundred returns and a half leaf face down are thirteen figures of distance and of count and not one of them is a price, and the fourth hour, the sixth hour, a Thursday, four and a half years, about seven months, about three weeks, two years and the tenth open crossing are eight figures of time and not one of them goes in at either end of this column. **A woman of about forty who runs a lime kiln and Aren Kest and a woman of about sixty who holds the other end of a rope and a boy of about eleven who holds a count and a man who keeps a lane four days below and a man of about fifty-eight with a badge in a market town and a woman of about twenty-nine who keeps a room and a copyist of about twenty-six and a woman of about fifty who keeps a road with a slate in her apron are nine figures of persons, and the second of them was asked a question of two words on a slope by the first of them and did not answer it, and the fourth of them was standing four paces off with a sledgehammer up and will not remember any of it.** Nobody thanked anybody and nobody was thanked and nobody wrote it down.
