# Chapter 0633

**Thawmonth the tenth, YR 321, a Saturday and a market morning, the second hour in a room over a weigh-house with the door open, two pieces of paper read out loud, and a clause about words.**

The copyist of about twenty-six had asked for the sixth morning and got the tenth, and Aren Kest was not in the room for either of them and was on the boards outside at about the second hour and heard the whole of the second letter from there, because the band came in on a fixed day and she would not open a thing out of a band early and she would not open a room early, and the two letters went up the outside stair in the middle of a week's returns with about nine other things that had nothing to do with either of them.

She put them face up on the table and did not touch them for about half a minute, and about nine people stood in a room over a weigh-house on a market morning and could hear them, because the door was open and it stays open until the seventh hour on a market morning.

Then she read them out.

The first was from the coast of returning roads and it was short.

It said that the coast would not be sending anybody, and that the reason was not that anybody there was unwilling and that the coast was not asked to think of itself as unwilling, and that the only person among them who could be spared for the length of an argument was the one who holds the post, and that a post does not get left. It said that anything written and sent to the post would be read on the day it came and would not be answered, and that the coast would rather say that now in its own hand than be asked in about a month for an answer that it could not give. It said that it had read the three sentences and that it thought the notice one was right and that it would not be able to do anything with any of them, and it ended with a sentence about a gate and about what a gate is for.

Nobody said anything about it for a while and then the man of about fifty-eight with a badge said that it was the most sensible thing anybody had put in that room and that he was not going to argue with a road about its own post.

The man of about fifty-one who keeps a road in the glass country on the high flats then asked the only question anybody asked that morning that was about the room and not about a rule.

“**Then what is a road that sends nobody?**”

“**It is a road that sends nobody,**” the copyist said. “**It is not a gap and it is not a refusal and it is not a road on a page. It is a road whose own people wrote a letter instead of walking here, and that letter is the whole of what that road has said to nine counties in about four and a half years, and I can read it out in a room with the door open and that is the end of what I can do with it. If you want to know more than that, somebody has to go.**”

“**Somebody has no four days,**” the woman of about fifty said, from the back.

“**Then somebody has not got four days,**” the copyist said, “**and I have been saying that out loud in this room for about a month and about four people have heard it and none of them has done anything about it, and neither have I.**”

The second letter was longer and it was in the hand of a woman of about sixty who has held the far end of a road for about thirty-one years, and it came down off a slope on the back of a cart with a sale on it, and the woman of about forty who runs a lime kiln had carried it and had not come up the stair and had not asked anybody for anything, and that is the whole of what she did about it in about four and a half years.

The copyist read it out and this is what it said.

It said that the road would not be sending anybody, and that it was not able to say why in a way that anybody in that room would accept, and that it would try once anyway. It said that in about four and a half years nobody in nine counties had ever asked that road a question it could answer, and that every question that had ever come near it had been a question about a number, and that it had noticed that the room did not ask questions, which it took to mean that the room did not want answers. It said that the first number anybody says out loud about a place is the number that place has for the rest of its life, and that it is said by whoever says it first, and that it is not chosen afterwards by anybody, and that there are about four houses that owe her for a season and about five that do not. It said that it had a count and that the count was not going into a column and was not going into a schedule and was not going to be read out in a room, and that anybody who wanted it was welcome to come and walk it, and that it understood there was no time for that either.

The room was quiet in a way that the man of about fifty-eight with a badge had not heard in it before, and he has been in rooms in nine counties for about thirty-one years.

The man of about fifty-two, who would not name the road he keeps, was the first of them to move and what he did was turn his chair round so that his back was to the table.

“**I came into this room on Saturday and would not say a road,**” he said, to the wall, “**and I have been in it four days being proud of that, and I have just been handed a piece of writing from a road that has had more nerve in it about its own name than I have had about mine. That is not a complaint either. It is a thing I did not expect and I am going to sit here with my back to the table for about a minute and then I am going to turn round again, because a man who cannot sit in a room with a letter like that has no business arguing about a clause.**”

He turned round again in about a minute and said nothing else all day.

The copyist of about twenty-six said then the thing about herself, and she said it standing at the table with her hand flat on it, and she was not asking anybody to agree with it.

“**That letter is now in about nine people's mouths and it is not in any book,**” she said. “**That is the correct condition and I have known it for about four and a half years and I have never minded it before this morning. A road's own words go nowhere. That is why they cannot be got at and turned into a line and put beside another line. And it is also why nobody can be answerable for having heard one. If you write nine clauses down and nine people carry them, then a letter that came off a slope on the back of a cart will be in the same condition as your rule, and that is the whole of what I have and it is worth nothing this morning and I am not taking it back.**”

Nobody thanked her and nobody told her she was wrong and the man of about thirty-four with a sheet wrote one line and did not read it back.

Hester Vane said the thing that a person in that room had to say or nobody would have said it.

“**Do not use that,**” she said. “**Do not put that letter in the middle of this and let nine people feel bad about it, because it is not a complaint and it is not a request and it is not an example. It is a road telling this room, in its own words, which is what you have all been arguing for nine days is the only words that count, that it has never been asked a question it could answer. And that letter is the argument against the rule you are writing. Every clause you write is a clause that works because somebody can be found. And that letter came down off a slope on the back of a cart because one person carried it. Nobody sent for it. There is no arrangement. It is here because a woman of about forty happened to have a cart going.**”

Nobody thanked her. The man of about fifty-eight with a badge said that the last part of that was the first time in thirty-one years he had heard the reason for the whole of it said in a sentence he could hand to somebody.

The man of about forty-seven said the thing that the rest of the morning was made of.

“**Then the notice clause has a hole in it the size of a road,**” he said, “**and the hole is not the road. The hole is that a notice is a thing somebody carries, and a thing somebody carries is a thing somebody has to decide to carry, and no clause in the world can make a person at the top of a slope decide to put a letter on a cart that is already loaded. So write it anyway and write it in the road's words, and understand that you are writing a thing that will be obeyed by whoever happens to care, and that will not be obeyed by anybody else, and that in about four months the man who needs it obeyed will be in a hurry.**”

“**Then say the words that go in it,**” the woman of about twenty-nine who keeps the room said, from the doorway, and nobody had seen her come down.

So the copyist wrote the notice clause in about four hands' worth of arguing and it is this, and there are two versions of it in the room and the second is the one nine people carried out of the door:

No road is shut until the man who proposes to shut it has been to the end of that road and has said it out loud, in words that road uses, in a place where somebody who works that road can hear him, and on a day that road is being used.

The man of about thirty-four with a sheet said that the last four words were his and that he had put them in because a clause that can be complied with on a day the road is shut is a clause that will be complied with, and the copyist said that nobody was going to give him a column for that and that was correct.

Then a man who had been standing at the back for about an hour asked whether a road that was shut could be said to have been notified on a day it was not used, and there was a long silence, and the answer came from the woman of about fifty, who keeps a road nineteen years, and it was one sentence and she did not expand it.

“**You would be notifying a piece of road,**” she said.

Berta Lomax read the clause out twice because she can read and about four people in that room cannot, and she read it the way a person reads a thing out loud, without any weight on it at all, and when she had finished the first time she said that the word end was doing more work in that sentence than any other word in it and that she did not know what it meant on a road with nine crossings open, and nobody answered her and nobody wrote it down.

Nobody thanked her.
