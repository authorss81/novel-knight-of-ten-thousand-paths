# Chapter 0641

**Mudmonth the fifteenth, YR 321, a Saturday and a market morning, the second hour in the market street, a meeting called with nothing at the top of it, and a woman at the boards who has come to have a road put in a column.**

The carts were in before the light got onto the stones, and the street had the smell wet stone has under a thaw, sour and mineral, with the drain at the low end running a finger of water along the gutter and nobody minding it. Nine days of wet had put a grey rime on everything that stood still, and the boards at the low end of the street had gone the colour of a knife that has been left out. Somebody's cart went over the drain at a slow walk and the whole street heard the boards take the weight of it.

Aren Kest had his stick against the edge of the platform and his good leg on the stones and he had been there since before the first cart came round the corner, and he had been counting. Not the carts. The people who came and stood at the boards and looked along the street for somebody they knew before they said anything. That morning there were nine of them inside the first hour and the ninth was a man in a wet coat who asked the badge-man whether it was on, and the badge-man said that it was, and the man went away without his face changing.

It was the sort of counting he had got good at, and it was the only thing in nine counties he had got good at that nobody wanted paying for.

Then a man of about fifty-eight with a badge came out of the doorway of the weigh-house and stood at the low end of the boards with his back to the weigh-house wall, where the whole street could hear him and not only the people on the platform, and he said out loud that the meeting was not finished.

He said it to nobody in particular, in the lane, the way he said the hearing, and about nine people heard it and about five of them were doing something else at the time.

Then he said the reason, and the reason was the only interesting thing he had said in a month.

“**I have a badge and I have a chain of office and I have thirty-one years, and I have not got a head, and that is not a figure of speech and I am not going to be told it is one by a man who has not spent thirty-one years. A thing is called by a head. A notice, a form, a sitting, a hearing, a meeting, all of them have a name at the top of them and a person underneath the name who can be asked whether it was called. I have not got a name at the top of anything and I am not going to write one, and if I did write one I would be the man who called it, and a meeting that the man who called it can call off is not a meeting. It is a thing a man does.**”

He turned the badge over in his hand while he said it, the way a man turns a coin he has been carrying too long, and put it back inside his coat, and Aren Kest, who had known him a long time, thought that this was the only man in nine counties who had spent the morning getting ready for a refusal.

The man of about thirty-one from a road office had a sheet in his hand before the badge-man had finished the second sentence, and he held it up, and the sheet had a mark at the top of it and four ruled lines under the mark and nothing written in any of the four, and it was the form everybody in nine counties had been putting a keeper's name into since a sheet with a name at the top of it and no sender had been obeyed for nine days.

“**Is this the notice?**” he said, and then, before anybody had answered him, “**It is the proper one. It goes out under a head and it comes back and it is a thing that can be proved.**”

The copyist of about twenty-six did not take it.

She had been at the boards since before him, in an apron with two days of returns folded into the front of it and a pen behind her ear that she did not need because her hands were better than a pen, and she looked at the sheet for the length of a breath before she spoke, and then she put her thumb on the corner of it, which is what she had done to every sheet anybody had put in front of her for four years and a half.

“**Put it on the boards,**” she said, “**and do not take it off again, and I will tell you what I would have to write under the first line, and then you can both decide whether you want it there.**”

He put it on the boards. Everybody looked at it.

“**The first line would be a date,**” she said. “**The second line would be a place, and there are about nine places in nine counties and I could name them in order and I have. The third line would be what the meeting is for, and that is a thing about nine people can be wrong about, and if I write the wrong one down then the meeting is the thing I wrote down and not the thing they came for. The fourth line is the one you have kept empty for about four years and a half and I am not the person who is going to write in it, and neither are you, and I would like it said in a market street that neither of us is going to write in it.**”

Nobody said anything for about as long as a carter takes to get a load round.

Then the badge-man said that she was right and that he had asked her to say it, and that the reason he had asked her was that she was the only person in the market who had a reason to say no to a thing being written, and everybody looked at the man of about thirty-one and he put his hand flat on the sheet and did not pick it up.

Nobody thanked her.

The man of about thirty-four with a sheet wrote out what the copyist had said about the four lines and read it back to her before he put it in the satchel, and she checked it against what she had said and found nothing wrong with it, and he folded it and said that a thing written about a form in a market street would be in nine counties in a week and that nobody could call it back, and that this was the reason he wrote about nine words and not nine sentences.

The woman of about twenty-nine who keeps the room came out of the door and stood at the top of the outside stair and said one thing about where the meeting could be held, and she said it before anybody asked her, and she said it to the street and not to the badge-man. She did not come down the stair. The door stayed open behind her, the way a door does when the person who opened it has decided it is not hers to shut, and Aren Kest did not look up at the top of that stair because he knew what it cost her to stand on a step in front of nine people with the room behind her, and because he could not have said one word of the four he had in him.

“**The room is shut on every day but a market morning and it opens at the second hour and it shuts at the seventh, and I am not going to have it opened on a Tuesday for nine people who asked nicely, and I have said that once before in this lane and I am not saying it twice for a second reason. And after that: the room cannot call anything. It has a door on a lane and a number and a deed of thirteen words and it can be sued and it can sue, and every one of those three things is about the room, and not one of them is about the road. If a meeting is held in there it will be a meeting of the people who can get up thirteen stairs, and that is four of the nine who can, and I would rather be told the truth in a street than find it out in a room.**”

The man of about thirty-one said that a thing held in the open could be walked away from, and that a thing held in a room could not, and that there was a word for what he had just described and it was a hearing, and the badge-man said it was, and that it had always been one, and that the room was built for the other kind and the room had lost that argument about four years and a half ago.

Then the badge-man said how the meeting would be called, and he said it in about nine sentences and he said it in the lane and not on the boards, and he said that this was on purpose.

“**It will be in the open, on a market morning, in this street, in seven days from this morning. There will be nothing at the top of it. There will be nothing written about the fact that it was called, and there will be no piece of paper with a date on it anywhere, and if you want to know whether it is on, it is on, and that is the whole of the arrangement and I am not going to improve on it. Any of you may come. Any of you may not come. Any of you may come and say that it should not have been called and be heard about that before anything else is taken up. And I am telling you now, in a market, with about nine people listening, that I am not going to be the middle of it. I have no head. If somebody wants a middle then somebody with a head has to come and stand in it, and I have watched a man want that for thirty-one years and I have watched what it does to him.**”

Four people said that a meeting nobody could call off was not a meeting, and five said it was the only kind there had ever been, and one of the five was the copyist and she said it in a working voice and not a loud one.

Then the woman of about thirty-four who keeps the key asked the question that was actually in the room, and she asked it in about eleven words.

“**Who tells a road that is four days off?**”

Nobody answered her for longer than it takes to draw a cart round.

“**Nobody,**” the badge-man said. “**A road that is four days off by a made road is not told, and there are two of those inside a week of here, and the way they are told is that somebody who is going that way says it in a doorway, and there is no clause about that and there cannot be one, because a clause about telling a road four days off is a clause about a man remembering, and a man remembers or he does not. I am putting that in the open at the second hour on a market morning so that it is in the air of this market and not in anybody's coat. I am not going to do anything else about it.**”

She said that she had not expected an answer and had got one, and that she was going to a place four days off in about two weeks and would say it in about four doorways herself, and nobody asked her whether she was paid for it and she was not.

Aren Kest stood at the low end of the boards for the whole of that morning and answered two people out of four, and both of them were people who had asked him for a figure. There was a third one he did not answer, a man he knew, who wanted him to say that the works would hold another four years, and it might have been true and it would have been worth saying, and Aren Kest stood with his stick against the platform edge and let it go past him, because a man who says the load-bearing thing in a market street for his own hand is a man who has asked to be the middle, and he had worked that out in a room with nine people in it about four months ago and it had cost him a road.

He did not go up the outside stair and he did not go into the room. He had come to the market that morning to be a man in a street, and there was a woman thirteen stairs up who had not asked him for anything, and those two facts had been standing in the same place for about a week and neither of them had moved.

The man of about fifty-eight with a badge stopped about half way through the morning and looked at the empty form on the boards and then at the copyist, and said that he had not asked her to refuse it and that he had asked her to say what the refusing would cost, and she said that it had cost him a sheet and that was the whole of it, and he said that this was the cheapest thing anybody had said in this lane in about a month and that he had no idea who had paid for the sheet and did not intend to find out.

Then the man of about fifty-two's chair was looked at, because it was still under the table from the ninth of the nine days where he had put it himself, and about nine people looked at it and four of them said his name and nobody said it twice.

The badge-man said that the man had four days of road in front of him in the middle of a month and that a man with four days of road in front of him in the middle of a month is not a man anybody sends for, and that he had not sent for him and would not send for him, and that this was the correct arrangement and that he had said so on the ninth day and was saying it again in a market because markets are where things are said twice.

The copyist said that nothing had come from the end of that road since the middle of the month before, and she said it without stopping what she was doing, and she said that it was the eighth time she had said it and that she would say it a ninth and a tenth, and that a count of how many times a person has said a thing is not a measurement of anything.

Then, at about the fourth hour, two people who had not been at the nine days came up the lane and stopped at the boards, and one of them was a woman of about thirty-four and she had not been in this lane in about four years, and she said out loud, in front of about nine people, that she had come to have a road put in a column.

Nobody told her she could not. The badge-man did not tell her she could.

“**You can come on Saturday,**” he said, “**and it will be in the open, and you can say it there, and I am not going to tell you here that you will like what happens, because I do not know what will happen and neither do you, and that is the only honest thing anybody can offer you at about the fourth hour on a market morning.**”

“**Is there a place where a person says a thing and it is written down?**” she said, and the copyist said that there was not and that there had not been one for about four years and a half, and that this was the only answer she had and that it was not a good one. The woman of about thirty-four said that she had come four days to say one sentence and she was going to say it and go home, and the copyist looked at her for a moment and then went back to the boards, and five people in that market did not turn round and four did.

**And the cost of that Saturday is a column and it is nobody's, and a form with four ruled lines and nothing in them and a meeting with nothing at the top of it and a chair under a table are not in it and none of them has a price.**

**a wicker basket at 3, a sheaf of withies at 7, a peck of onions at 10, a rope of tar at 16, a bound of skins at 17, a trundle of sheep at 20, a cheese at 30, a barrow-load of salt cod at 31, a hide at 34, and a handbell at 39.** Three, seven, ten, sixteen, seventeen, twenty, thirty, thirty-one, thirty-four and thirty-nine is two hundred and seven pence. **A mark is forty-eight pence and two hundred and seven pence is four marks and fifteen over.** The sheet went home in the coat it arrived in, with its four ruled lines and nothing in them. At about the fifth hour a man came with a broom and swept the boards from the low end to the high, and the four lines came up in the grit and went out with it, and he did not stop for any of it.
