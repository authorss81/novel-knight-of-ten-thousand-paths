# Chapter 0606

**Wolfmonth the nineteenth, YR 320, a Sunday, the ninth open crossing, the second hour, a lime kiln on the slope, a boy of about eleven who can say the count, and a woman of about forty who will not be the first to say a number.**

The road was not made for about a mile and a half of its length and the mile and a half was the whole difficulty, and it was passable on the days when two people turned up at the two ends of it, and on the days when one of them did not turn up it was not a road and it was not anything else either, it was ground with a wall on one side and a drop on the other.

At this end of it there was a lime kiln on a slope, and the woman of about forty who ran it was called Nell Craddock by about nine households and by nobody else, and she had run it alone since her mother died, and it took stone up past the bad mile and a half in a sledge with a rope and came back down with lime, and the lime paid for the firing and the sledge and the rope and nothing else, and about nine houses owed her for a season and about four of them had paid and about five had not and she had a slate for it.

The man of about thirty-two with a limp came up the lane from the top of the bad stretch on the ninth open crossing in two years, and about four days ago a woman of about sixty at the other end had told him it was a crossing day and had said no month and no day and no hour of any kind out loud, only that the water was off the stones and it would be off them until the sun was over the ridge.

He was at the second hour of the part of the day that the sun was over the ridge. There was a boy of about eleven at the kiln with a barrow and there was a woman of about sixty at the top of the slope with the other end of the rope, and there was a man who keeps a lane four days below, who had walked up once in the last year and had not come the other four times, and nobody had sent for him.

Nell Craddock was turning lime out of the kiln's draw-off with a barrow and did not stop for him.

“**You will want to know the number,**” she said.

“**I would like to know the number,**” Aren Kest said.

“**Then you can ask the boy,**” she said, “**and the boy will give it to you straight off, because it is in him and he does not know that it is a thing anybody has to be careful with.**”

The boy of about eleven said it without stopping at the barrow. Nine open crossings. Four shut weeks. The longest shut week was the one in the middle of the wet, and it had been eleven days, and there had been a shorter one of three days in the summer, and the other two had been a fortnight and a week. He gave the order they had happened in and he gave it as a list of things that had happened rather than as a thing anybody was counting, and he got it right, and nobody had written any of it down at any point in two years.

“**Ask him again in a year,**” Nell Craddock said. “**He will still have it. He is eleven. He will have it for about nine more years and then he will be a man of twenty who was asked a question by a stranger on a bad stretch and will not remember it as a count.**”

“**Then somebody should write it,**” Aren Kest said.

“**Somebody should,**” she said, and turned the barrow, and did not go on, and the boy of about eleven was already back at the kiln mouth and had stopped listening because he had given the whole of it away and there was nothing left.

He asked her why she had not said the number herself, and it took a while to get an answer out of her, and the answer was four sentences long and she said it in her own voice and not in a voice anybody had taught her.

“**Because I am the one who goes,**” she said. “**And the first number anybody says out loud about a place is the number that place has for the rest of its life, and it is said by whoever says it first and it is not chosen by anybody afterwards, and I have watched that happen to a ford and I have watched it happen to a mill, and the ford has been called shallow for nine years and it is not shallow and the mill has been called busy and it has been idle for two.**”

“**You could say a different one,**” Aren Kest said.

“**I could,**” she said. “**And then I would be the first person in nine counties to put a figure on a road that four days of carting away has never been able to find, and every clerk in between would have to use my figure, and if my figure was wrong then the road would be the wrong size for ever and nobody would be able to prove it, because there would be no other figure.**”

“**Somebody local has to say it,**” Aren Kest said, and heard himself say it, and knew as he said it that it was the finding of the whole of that day and that he had not earned it and that a woman with a barrow had earned it two years ago by not saying it.

“**Yes,**” Nell Craddock said. “**Somebody local has to say it, out loud, in front of people who will remember, and be wrong if they are wrong, and be believed about everything else for the rest of their life. That is what a number is when it is a place and not a sum. And I have four houses that owe me for a season and a kiln that wants burning before the frost and about nine households who will be perfectly certain in ten years that the road I said the number for is the road they remember, and I am not going to be that, because I have to walk it.**”

That was the whole of what she said about the number and she said it once, and then she went back to the barrow, and the lime came out of the draw-off in the wet grey slabs it comes out in and she broke them with the back of the spade and that is not a thing you do well in a hurry.

He did not go up the bad mile and a half. He sat on the kiln wall above the draw-off and looked down the slope, and the road went off the slope in three turns and lost about a mile of height, and the ground at the bottom of it was a river flat of broken stone and the river was off the stones the way a woman of about sixty had said it would be, without saying when.

The woman of about sixty came down at about the fourth hour and stood at the top of the barrow run and looked at him and did not come closer than that, and she had been the other end of that road for about thirty-one years and had not once in thirty-one years been asked by anybody what the road was.

“**You are the man whose name is on a page,**” she said, and it was not an accusation and it was not a question, and it was the first time in four days that anybody had said anything to him about a piece of paper, and he had not known that anybody four days off knew there was a piece of paper.

“**I have not seen it,**” Aren Kest said.

“**I did not say you had,**” the woman of about sixty said. “**I said your name. I heard your name said in a market town by a woman who was in a lane and I have carried it up here and I have not put it anywhere, and I am telling you that I have said it out loud, which is a thing I have not done about anything in about nine years.**”

Then she went to take her end of the rope.

He went back down the bad mile and a half in the last of the light, on the low side, which is the way a loaded sledge goes down and the wrong way up, and the sledge went up in the morning with a stone on it and a boy of about eleven at the front of the rope, and he walked in front of the sledge for about a quarter of a mile because his legs knew how to do it and nobody asked him to and nobody thanked him for it, and the woman of about sixty walked behind with her shoulder against the back of the load and said nothing at all for the whole of that quarter of a mile.

At the top of the bad stretch he turned round and looked at the nine open crossings that were eight that day, because the ninth had been spent getting here and getting back, and at the count as the boy of about eleven held it, which was a thing in a child and not in a book, and at the woman of about thirty-eight who had said a number could be given by somebody else and had been about four days wrong about what a number is for, and at about four hundred people in a market town who hold one true thing about him and about four hundred who hold another, and none of it was a list.

**And the cost of that Sunday is a column and it is Nell Craddock's, and nine open crossings and four shut weeks and a count that lives in a child are not in it and none of them has a price.**

**a barrow wheel at six, a pair of gloves at eight, a rope splicer at ten, a bushel measure at twelve, a kiln rake at twenty, a lime hod at twenty-two, a whetstone at twenty-five, a sledge shoe at thirty-eight, and a wicket gate hinge at forty-one.** Six, eight, ten, twelve, twenty, twenty-two, twenty-five, thirty-eight and forty-one is one hundred and eighty-two pence. **A mark is forty-eight pence and one hundred and eighty-two pence is three marks and thirty-eight over**, and two ends of a road, about a hundred paces of a district, nine counties, about nine hundred sheets a month, about a hundred and four in one band, about four hundred and nine parish rolls a month, a fourth head ruled and empty, four hands of writing, a register of seven questions, a day-book of nine entries, a drawer about eleven inches deep, about four hundred returns and a half leaf face down are thirteen figures of distance and of count and not one of them is a price, and the second hour, the fourth hour, a Sunday, four and a half years, about seven months, about three weeks, a fixed day, two years and the ninth open crossing are nine figures of time and not one of them goes in at either end of this column. **A woman of about forty who runs a lime kiln and a boy of about eleven who holds a count and a woman of about sixty who has been the other end of a road for about thirty-one years and a man who keeps a lane four days below and Aren Kest and a copyist of about twenty-six and a man of about thirty-four with a sheet and a woman of about twenty-nine who keeps a room and about nine households that owe her for a season are nine figures of persons, and the third of them has never in thirty-one years been asked what the road is and the ninth of them has never been asked whether they would rather not and the column asks neither of them.** Nobody thanked anybody and nobody was thanked and nobody asked the boy a second time.
