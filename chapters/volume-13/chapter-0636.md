# Chapter 0636

**Thawmonth the eighteenth, YR 321, a Sunday and not a market morning, one and a half days off on a made road, a bridge over a drain where the waterworks country takes the water off, six men and a cart, and a fourth course that is not laid.**

There is no market in that town on a Sunday and the room over a weigh-house was shut and stayed shut, and in the lane about four hundred paces long nobody was arguing about anything, and nine people who had been arguing for three days were not in it.

A day and a half off, on a made road where the waterworks country takes the water off the road and puts it into a channel, there is a bridge over a drain that a cart's off-side wheel went into in about February and that had been silted and weeded for two months because there is no money in nine counties for a bridge that is not on anybody's list and no rule in nine counties for a man to mend one and be paid for it.

There were six men and a boy of about seventeen and a cart, and they were not there because anybody sent them.

The boy of about seventeen was asked who had told them to come, on the ground, at about the second hour, while the silt was coming out of the drain in barrows, and he said that nobody had.

“**We came off our own road,**” he said. “**That bridge takes the water off about four miles of made road and it has been holding four miles of made road up since February and every one of us has been standing in it. The last time it was cleared was when my master's father was in and nobody wrote that down either.**”

“**Then who paid for the cart?**”

“**A carter's boy paid for the cart out of his own wage and he is going to be poor about it,**” the boy said, “**and he will get it back when the road money comes round, or he will not, and he did not tell me to tell anybody that either.**”

The man of about forty-four who keeps a road in the waterworks country was in the market town arguing about a burden clause and had not sent them and did not know they had gone until a carter told him four days afterwards. He said afterwards, in a lane, to about four people, that the crew had gone because the drain under that bridge was the drain that took the water off his own road, and that a man who keeps four miles of made road does not sit in a room in a market town while four miles of road is being held up by a hand's depth of silt, and that is the whole of his politics and he has never had more.

The man of about fifty laid it himself. He would not let anybody else lay a course, in the way he does not, and he did not explain that and nobody asked, and four of the six men standing there that morning would have said afterwards that they had seen a man lay stone properly perhaps twice.

He cleared it first, with a hook and about nine barrows of silt and weed, and the weed had gone right down into the joints of the two courses that were left, which is the thing that makes a bridge like that fail and not the weight on it, and he worked it out joint by joint from the top down with a bar and his own fingers and no hurry at all.

The boy of about seventeen barrowed it all out into barrows and tipped it on the low side of the road where it would go down the ditch and off, and about nine barrows went out and the last one was mostly water and the boy said it did not count.

“**It counts,**” the man of about fifty said. “**Everything that comes out of there counts and none of it is written down. In about thirty years somebody will find this bridge with three courses in it and a bed under them that has been scoured since before you were born, and they will say it was laid bad. It will be a true thing that will be said about a thing that was laid well.**”

Then he cleared it down to the stone and not to the mud under it, because the mud under it is the bed and the bed is not his and is not anybody's that morning.

Then he cut the string and laid three courses in lime.

“**He laid them the way the middle course of that crossing went in about nineteen years ago,**” the boy of about seventeen said afterwards, to the cart, “**which is to say perfectly, and at the same speed, and he looked at it for a while and said nothing.**”

Then he sat down on the parapet with his back against the stone and did not pick the trowel up again.

There was a fourth course to lay. Everybody there knew there was a fourth course to lay, and there were six men and a boy and a cart and about two hours of daylight left, and one of the men asked him what he was waiting for and he gave the load answer first because it was the short one and he had given it before.

“**Four is on top of three and three is on top of two and two is on top of the bed, and you have put the weight of a course onto the one course in that bridge that has nothing holding it. I will not lay it. That is the first reason and I have given it four times now and it is still true.**”

“**Go on then, put four in,**” the man said, “**four is better than three.**”

“**No,**” the man of about fifty said, and then he did the thing he had come to do, and he got the back of a trowel and turned it over and pointed with it at the three courses in front of him and he took his time about it.

“**And that is the reason I have never given anybody, because it took me thirty years and it is not about stone. They will not have it.**”

Nobody said anything.

“**There is no return in nine counties in which a man may write down how many stones he laid. I have asked three times in my life. I was told twice that there is no head to rule it on and once that there is no rule because nobody has ever thought of one. And I have gone on laying them anyway and I have been right every time, and being right has got me a hole in about thirty years and a road out of a market town that people say is not safe in the dark.**”

“**That has nothing to do with the fourth course,**” a man said.

“**It has got everything to do with the fourth course,**” the man of about fifty said. “**Because in thirty years a man is going to come to this bridge with a bar, and he is going to find courses in it, and he is going to be able to count them, and if there are three then he is looking at three and he can go and ask the men who were here and about four of them will be dead and the fifth will be somewhere else. If there are four, then there is one course in that bridge that nobody can account for. He will have no way at all of finding out who put it in or what was under it or whether anybody looked at the joint. And that course will be the one that is going to go, because it will be the one nobody has an argument about.**”

He put the trowel down.

“**And I know what nine people are doing about nine days' ride from here,**” he said, “**because a boy came past this bridge on Wednesday and said there was a man of about thirty-four writing clauses out in a room and that one of them was about a person who can be named and found. And I have been laying stone for thirty years and I have wanted a stone you could find since before any of you were in a room. So you write your rule about your person. I am not going to argue with it. And I would like somebody in the nine counties to write down a course of stone.**”

Nobody thanked him for the three either. The wages were counted out at the seventh hour on the parapet by the cart, six men and a boy, and the money went into seven hands, and the boy counted his own twice before he put it away, and nobody said it was well done and nobody said anything about it at all.

Aren Kest was four days off at that time, at a set of works under an abbey, with six roads on it doing the ordinary work of six roads, and he heard about the bridge on the Monday morning from a carter who was buying a basket, and he did not go and look at it, and he did not ask to see the masonry, and nobody in that market town told the bridge-man about it either, and the two of those facts are the whole of what happened between them in about four and a half years and neither of them is described in any document.
