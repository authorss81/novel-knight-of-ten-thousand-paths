# Chapter 0623

**Hearthmonth the twenty-sixth, YR 320, a Thursday and a market morning, four days off on a made road, a new sill in three courses at the crossing where the water came over, a cart of stone, a boy of about seventeen, and a fourth course that is not laid.**

The room over the weigh-house was open that morning because it is a market morning in a town of about two thousand people four days back, and the room does not know what is happening four days off and neither does anybody else in it, and the man of about thirty-four with a sheet was at the counter at the second hour with a schedule in front of him that nine counties could not yet pay for, and none of that had anything to do with what happened on a made road that day.

Four days off, at about the second hour, four men and a boy and a cart were at the crossing.

They were not repairing the works. The man of about fifty who lays stone had said on the first morning that the works was not theirs and the bed under it was not theirs, and the man from the road office had said on the second morning that it was a matter for the works and not for a road, and both of those were correct, and in the meantime the water had come over a road with a number on it, and a road with a number on it is a thing nine counties pay for whether or not anybody agrees whose bed it is.

The job was to take out what was left of the old sill across the scoured reach and put three new courses in, in lime, from new stone, and to do it in about two hours of daylight because the cart could not come again until the ground dried.

The man of about fifty laid them himself. He would not let anybody else lay a course and he did not explain why and nobody asked, and about four of the men standing there that morning would have said afterwards that they had seen a man lay stone properly perhaps twice, and about none of them could have told you what he did differently, and that is what a well-done thing looks like to people who are not looking for it.

He worked from the ends in. He cut the string for the first course before he broke a stone. He mixed the lime with sand in the open air and not in a heap, because lime mixed in a heap goes off in the middle and sets like a stone, and he said that out loud once, to nobody, while he was doing it.

“**A man will mix it in a heap because it is quicker and by Thursday it will be a lump,**” he said. “**And then in a year somebody will say the bed was laid bad and it will be true and it will not have been anybody's fault, and that is worse than a fault.**”

The boy of about seventeen cut stone and carried the hod and did the other half of the work of a man, and was paid at the end of the day out loud on a sound stone by the low parapet, and his wage went into his own hand and straight into a pocket, and nobody said it was well done and nobody said anything about it at all.

The middle course went in at about the fourth hour. It went in the way the middle course of that crossing had gone in about nineteen years before, which is to say perfectly, and it went in at the same speed and with the same string and the same lime, and it was a good course, and the man of about fifty looked at it for a while after he had struck it off and said nothing.

Then he sat down on the parapet and did not start the fourth.

“**That is the top of it,**” he said, when a man asked him what he was waiting for.

“**There is a fourth course to lay.**”

“**There is,**” he said, “**and I am not going to lay it, and I will tell you why and then you can go and be annoyed somewhere else.**”

He got the back of a trowel and turned it over and pointed with it at the three courses in front of him, and he took his time about it, and the man of about fifty who lays stone is not a man who is asked to be careful and is not sure whether he is right.

“**That one,**” he said, pointing at the bottom, “**is bedded on the channel. It cannot go anywhere. That one,**” he said, pointing at the top, “**is bedded on the one under it, and the weight of the water sits on it every hour of every day, and if that one moves it drops on to the bottom one and the bottom one has the channel under it. The one in the middle has nothing on it that would fall and nothing under it that would hold it. It is a good course. It is the best laid of the three. It is the one that will go.**”

“**Nothing falls on it,**” a man said.

“**Nothing falls on it,**” the man of about fifty agreed, “**and nothing under it holds it, and in about thirty years somebody will find a hole in it at about the second hour of the night, and there will be a foot and a half of water on a road, and they will say the bed was laid bad. And nobody will ever ask me about the middle course, because a middle course is not a thing anybody thinks about. A bottom course is the ground. A top course is the water. It is the one in between that has to be its own argument, and it has not got one.**”

“**Then lay four,**” the man said. “**Four is better than three.**”

“**Then what do we tell them?**” a third man said, and he had not said anything for two hours.

“**Four is on top of three,**” the man of about fifty said, “**and three is on top of two, and two is on top of the ground, and you have just put the whole weight of a fourth course on the one course in the whole of that sill that has nothing holding it. I have laid three and I will go and do the same sill again in a year if anybody asks me and I will not lay the fourth, and if somebody with a schedule and a column of money wants four courses then he wants a man who will lay four, and he can have one, and it will not be me, and I am not going to lie about it.**”

Nobody thanked him for the three either. A man of about forty who keeps the third road was there and he had a bar and a bucket and about four hours of his own road in front of him, and he had said that he would be back the following morning, and he was not thanked and did not expect to be.

Aren Kest was there from the second hour and did not lay a stone, and it is worth being exact about why, because it is the whole of what he did and he did it on purpose.

He could have laid stone. He had laid stone, twice, on a made road four days down, in the dark, with a hook and a barrow and nobody watching. He was thirty-two with a limp in his right leg and a ferrier's stick and about four seconds more going up a bank than coming down one, and a new sill in three courses is not work for a man with a bad leg on ground that is wet to the ankle, and everybody at that crossing knew it, including him.

What he did instead was carry the string for the man of about fifty and hold the other end of it, and read nothing, and say nothing at all for about three hours, and the man of about fifty asked him once whether he wanted to say something and he said no, and the man of about fifty said that was the right answer and told the boy of about seventeen to stop looking at the man with the stick as though he were going to be asked to do something.

At about the seventh hour the cart was loaded with the stone that was left and the ground had dried enough to take it off, and the wages were counted out on a sound stone by the low parapet, four men and a boy, and the money went into five hands, and the boy counted his own twice before he put it away.

The man of about fifty who lays stone wrote nothing down, and it was not because of the man of about thirty-four with a sheet, who was four days back in a market town and did not come and would not have wanted to be told about a morning's work in a lane. He wrote nothing down because there is a road, and the road has a number against it, and a keeper's day is not a return and there is no return in nine counties in which a man may write down how many stones he laid, and the man of about fifty had asked for that return three times in his life and had been told twice that there is no head to rule it on and once that there is no rule because nobody has ever thought of one.

“**They will not have it,**” he said, to the boy, gathering his tools, “**and they are right not to have it, and it is going to be a hole in about thirty years and I am not going to be here.**”

“**You laid it,**” the boy said.

“**I laid three,**” the man of about fifty said, “**and if a man comes in thirty years with a bar and looks at the middle of it he will find three courses in lime and a clean bed and he will not find anything wrong, and that is the best thing I have ever done and nobody will ever know and that is the correct arrangement and I am not going to be asked about it again.**”

**And the cost of that Thursday is a column and it is the man of about fifty's, and three courses in lime and a fourth course not laid and about two hours of daylight and a boy's wage in a pocket are not in it and none of them has a price.**

**a spade at thirty, a mattock at twenty-seven, a crowbar at thirty-two, a hod at one, a line of chalk at forty-one, a bucket at thirty-seven, a wheelbarrow at thirty-five, a barrow wheel at eleven, and a hand barrow at nineteen.** Thirty, twenty-seven, thirty-two, one, forty-one, thirty-seven, thirty-five, eleven and nineteen is two hundred and thirty-three pence. **A mark is forty-eight pence and two hundred and thirty-three pence is four marks and forty-one over**, and a made road four days off, a new sill in three courses, a scour about a foot and a half deep at the low side, a length of channel cut by somebody dead, a low parapet, a road with a number against it that is nineteen years old, a cart, a load of stone, about four hours of a keeper's own road in front of him, a string, a trowel, a fourth course that is not laid, and about thirty years are thirteen figures of distance and of count and not one of them is a price, and a Thursday, the second hour, the fourth hour, the seventh hour, a market morning four days back, the first morning, the second morning, nineteen years ago, three times in a life and about two hours of daylight are ten figures of time and not one of them goes in at either end of this column. **A man of about fifty who lays stone and Aren Kest and a man of about forty who keeps the third road and three other men at a crossing and a cart and a copyist of about twenty-six and a man of about thirty-four with a sheet, four days back in a market town, who did not come and a man of about thirty-one from a road office with a schedule and a man of about forty-seven with a page and a woman of about twenty-nine who keeps a room and a boy of about seventeen are eleven figures of persons, and the second of them held a string for three hours and said nothing at all, and the first of them laid three courses and refused the fourth and told a boy of about seventeen about it, and the sixth of them has a column of money and has nowhere to rule a line.** Nobody thanked anybody and nobody was thanked and the money went into five hands and nothing went down the lane in a coat.
