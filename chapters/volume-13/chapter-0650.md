# Chapter 0650

**Greenmonth the twenty-fifth, YR 321, a Thursday and a market morning, the second hour in the market street and then a room over a saddler's, a hearing with no bench in it, about nine people, and a woman of the fruit valley reading a sentence out loud that was never written down.**

The badge-man said the standing notice in the street at about the second hour, and then he said the one thing about it that was not standing, and nine people heard both.

“**The hearing stands in the street on the first of every month. Today is the twenty-fifth of Greenmonth and today is not the first of a month, and I am saying it anyway, and I am saying it because this is the last morning of a thing that began in this street in Thawmonth and I am not going to let it end on a day nobody comes. There is no bench in it. There has never been a bench in it. That is not a complaint about the furniture.**”

Then he said where it was going, and the woman who keeps the room said it with him and then said the part he had left out.

“**Not the room over the weigh-house,**” she said. “**It is up thirteen stairs and there is a woman of about sixty at the low end of the boards who was told on the first of a month that anybody could come and she is not going up thirteen stairs to be told it again. The room over the saddler's is on this lane and it has no stairs and it has floor enough for about nine people in it. I have kept the room over a weigh-house for about four years and a half and I am telling nine counties that on this one morning it is not the right room, and nobody is to write that down as a thing about the room.**”

Nobody wrote it down and nobody thanked her.

The hearing went up the outside stair of the saddler's and stood in the inquiry's rooms with about nine people in them, and there was no bench, and there was a floor, and the floor had been washed at some point in about four and a half years by somebody nobody in that room knew and nobody asked about and nobody was thanked for.
The stair of the saddler's is nine steps and it is inside the shop, so everybody who came in that morning came in out of the dark through a doorway about four feet wide, one at a time, and had to stand in the shop for a moment while the light got into their eyes before they could find the bottom of the stair. Nobody minded. There was nowhere in the room to put a coat and about nine coats went up on the back of the door and stayed there, and somebody's hat came off and went on top of a coat and stayed on top of the coat, and the door was not shut the whole morning because the door being shut was a thing somebody would have to decide.

A half leaf lay face down on a table at the far end and nobody turned it over and nobody said what was on the other side of it, and nobody in that room could have said how long it had been face down and nobody asked, and there was no arrangement about it in nine counties and there never had been one.

Aren Kest was in the room for about a quarter of an hour with his stick against the frame of the door and his good leg on the boards. It was his room. He had slept in it eleven nights and he had a spare blanket folded on the sill because the shop below shuts at the seventh hour and the room gets cold after that, and he had the key, and he had been in it every night since the twenty-second of Mudmonth. He had a quarter of an hour of it. The floor is the inquiry's and he is not the inquiry, and the room belongs to a thing that was standing up in it, and a man who owns the room and is not the owner of the thing in it is a visitor in his own doorway, and there was nobody in nine counties he could have said that to.

The copyist of about twenty-six brought the register up in her apron and put it on the table beside the leaf, and the register stood at nine questions with no finding under any of them and nothing had gone under any of them in about four years and a half. She put it down square with the edge of the table, which she did everywhere, and she did not open it, and her hand stayed flat on the cover for about a second longer than putting it down requires.

Then the woman who keeps the room read the standing of the hearing out loud to about nine people in a room with a door on a lane, and she read it as a person reads a thing she has kept, and not as a person reading somebody else's words.

“**The hearing stands in the street on the first of every month and it has no bench in it and it cannot say that a sheet is false. The book on this table has nine questions in it and no finding under any of them and nothing has gone under any of them in about four years and a half. The fourth line of the outgoing sheet is ruled and it is empty and it is not going to be filled and I am the person who keeps the sheet and I have said that out loud about four times in about a year. The three clauses are in nine hands and there is no copy of anything, and I am not the tenth and I am not going to be. There is no head in nine counties with which to call a meeting, and the man who says that most often has a badge and no head, and that is not a fault in him. Two roads sent writing instead of a person and both letters are on a file in the room over a weigh-house and neither of those roads has been visited. One road went quiet in the middle of nine days in Thawmonth and nothing has come from the end of it since the middle of Mudmonth, and nobody knows which road it is, and nobody has been sent, and nobody is going to be sent. That is all of it. That is what about nine counties have.**”

Nobody thanked her.

Then the badge-man said the thing nobody had said, which was that the meeting was not finished, and he said it in about nine words and then stopped and let the silence be the end of it.

“**It is not finished. There is no rule about who calls the next one and there is no head to call it with, and I am not going to make one, and about four months from now somebody is going to want a thing out of this and there will be nobody to ask. I have thought about that for about a month and I have not got an answer and I am not going to give nine counties a bad one this morning because it is the last morning.**”

“**Then who does call it?**” the man of about forty-four who keeps a road in the waterworks country said, from the middle of the floor, and he said it as though he had expected to be told and had asked anyway.

“**Nobody,**” the badge-man said, “**and I have said that about four times in about a month and you are going to hear it a fifth. There is no arrangement for the next one and I am not going to build one this morning in a room with a door on a lane, and if the six of you in here take it into your heads to do it between you then you will have done a thing in about nine days that took nine counties a year, and I would ask you not to.**”

Nobody argued with him and about four people in that room said nothing and five looked at the floor.

Then he named who was going to speak the sentence aloud, and he said where the words had come from and he said it in about nine words and nobody contradicted him.

“**Berta Lomax of the fruit valley has had these words since the seventh of Mudmonth at about the fourth hour in a market street, and nobody gave them to her and nobody sent them to her and nobody wrote them down, and the woman who said them is standing in this room and will say so herself. About nine people hold them. Nobody holds them for anybody else.**”

The woman of about thirty-six who keeps a road in the county where a list of names was burned stood up then, and she did not say it to the badge-man and she did not say it to Berta Lomax, and she said it to about nine people in a room with a door on a lane, and she said it in the four sentences she had been saying since the fourth of the nine days, in the same words, at the same speed, and she said that they were hers and that she had said them in a lane in Mudmonth and that she had not said them to anybody since except out loud in rooms and streets where somebody asked her to, and that a sentence of hers being read out in a room was a thing she had been asked for on the eighth of the nine days by a person who was in this room, and that she was not going to say that person's name in front of nine people because a name in a room is a head at the top of a thing, and that was all of it, and she sat down.

Nobody thanked her and nobody argued with her and the copyist looked at the table for about a second and a half and did not say a number out loud, which for the copyist of about twenty-six was the loudest thing anybody could have done in that room.

Then the badge-man gave Aren Kest the standing notice to carry, and did it in the plainest words anybody used all morning, and what he asked for was not a post and he said so.

“**What is it, then,**” Aren Kest said, from the low end of the boards, not meaning it as a question and getting an answer anyway.

“**Not a post. I have no post and there is no post, and I have said that in this street about nine times this month. I want you to say the words in nine lanes, one each, in the next two days, standing at a gate, in the open, to whoever opens it. And when somebody asks whether the hearing is on, you say that it is on, and when somebody asks you what it is, you say that it is a hearing with no bench in it that cannot say a sheet is false, and you say it about nine times and not nine hundred.**”

“**I will do that,**” Aren Kest said.

“**And you are not being given a post by a man with a badge,**” the badge-man said, “**and I will not hear that it is one, and if anybody in nine counties says that the man who can be sent for has been given a post to be sent for, I will stand in this street and say that it is not one, and I have not got a head and a badge is not a head.**”

Nobody thanked either of them.

Then Aren Kest went out of the room and down the nine steps of the saddler's stair and stood at the low end of the counter boards with his stick, and the woman who keeps the room did not look at him and he did not look at her, and four people in that room noticed and five did not, and that is the whole of what happened between them in a fortnight and nobody in nine counties has it in a document and nobody in nine counties is going to be given one.

Outside on the boards the market was going about its own morning and the man who swept them was not there, because it was a Thursday and the sweeping is done on a Saturday, and about nine people who had come to argue with a clause that was going to be read found themselves standing about four paces off with nothing to do, and four of them went to look at the saddler's window and five stood still. A carter put a load down about nine paces off, which was where a carter had put a load down on the Thursday the two things were answered, and he did not stop, and the badge-man did not go out to him, and the two of them had known each other for thirty-one years and neither of them made a thing of it.

Aren Kest stood with his stick and his good leg on the stones and thought about the two rooms over the saddler's. He had a key to them and a spare blanket on the sill and eleven nights of sleep behind him, and in about four minutes a sentence that had never been written was going to be read out loud in his own doorway, and the floor he was thinking about was the inquiry's and not his, and the shop below shut at the seventh hour, and there was nobody in nine counties he could have told any of it to, and he was not going to stand on the boards and be a man who is waiting for a thing to happen to him in a room he owned. He stayed where he was and watched a carter's boy untangle a rope and thought about lime, and neither of those was a decision and both of them were the same decision.

Nobody thanked anybody.

**And the cost of that Thursday is a column and it is nobody's, and a hearing with no bench in it and about nine people standing in a room and a floor and a key and a half leaf face down on a table and an unfinished meeting are not in it and none of them has a price.**

**a broom at 13, a cake of soap at 21, a jug at 25, a sheet of clean linen at 27, a footstool at 29, an oil lamp at 33, a bag of lavender at 34, a table at 39, a candlestick at 40, and a wax taper at 41.** Thirteen, twenty-one, twenty-five, twenty-seven, twenty-nine, thirty-three, thirty-four, thirty-nine, forty and forty-one is three hundred and two pence. **A mark is forty-eight pence and three hundred and two pence is six marks and fourteen over.** The lamp on the table over the saddler's was not lit, because it was not the fourth hour and nobody in that room had ever been in there at the second hour to want it lit, and the room was cold enough that about four people said so out loud and about five said that a hearing ought to be held in a cold room, and the badge-man said that it was the first sensible thing anybody had said in a room in about a year, and then somebody said that the hearing had never once been in a room at all, and that was true, and about nine people in a market street agreed with him.

Then Berta Lomax of the fruit valley stood up in the middle of a room with a door on a lane and about nine people in it, and read the clause about the right to refuse out loud, and she read it slowly, and she read it in the words it was said in, and nobody stopped her and nobody helped her and nobody thanked her, and about four people in that room had heard it once before and about five had not heard it at all.

“**A road may refuse to name a witness. If a road refuses, then nothing happens to that road. The man who said the road might be shut has not shown that it may be shut, and the road is open. No road has to say out loud that it has refused, and nobody is going to be able to get inside it afterwards and find out.**”
