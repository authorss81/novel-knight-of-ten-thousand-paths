# Chapter 0604

**Wolfmonth the eleventh, YR 320, a Saturday and a market morning, the second hour to the ninth at the culvert under the made road at the town's gate, three courses of sill, a boy's barrow, and a name at the top of an instruction that is not a return.**

The market was on the street and the gate was at the end of the made road, and between them there was about a quarter of a mile of road that nine counties paid for and about two thousand people used every market morning, and at the low end of it the water went under the road in a culvert that had been there longer than anybody in that town and had three courses of sill laid in lime across its bed.

On the Thursday a cart had gone in on its off-side wheel at the mouth of it and put a wheel-track a hand's depth into the silt, and the silt had packed the grating, and the water had come up out of the culvert and gone across the made stone and spread on the low side, and on the Saturday morning it was about a hand's depth across the road at the low edge and thin enough in the middle that a cart could cross it and a person on foot could not say what the crossing was.

The town had known about the grating since the first of the month. A boy of about seventeen knew it better than the town did, because he swept the gate for a man who swept it for a woman who had swept it before her, and on the Friday he had gone up the lane and found the man with the limp, who eats at the inn and is in on market nights and out on market mornings, and had said that the water was coming over the road at the gate, and had not said anything else, because he was seventeen and had a whole market morning in front of him.

Aren Kest went down to the culvert at the second hour with his stick and a barrow.

He was not asked. Nobody in that town asked him for anything and about four hundred of them said where he slept and about four hundred said a different thing about him, and the two sets did not overlap, and none of that had any business at a culvert and all of it came with him down the road.

He looked at it for about a minute and a half and then did the only thing he was asked to do, which was to say what was wrong with it, and he said it out loud, to the boy and to nobody.

“**The grating is not the fault,**” he said. “**The grating is weed and a wheel-track and it will clear. Under the weed there is a sill laid in three courses, and the middle course is not broken, it is gone, and the lime at the joint above it is clean, and the joint below it is clean, and a course does not leave between two clean joints on its own.**”

The boy of about seventeen asked him how a course leaves.

“**Somebody took it out,**” Aren Kest said, “**and put a new one in, and put the new one in dry.**”

He said that and then stopped, because there is a point past which a man who has been asked what is wrong with a thing is a man who is doing a second thing, and he knew where the point was and he knew it in about a minute, and about nine days ago he had stood in a lane and told a woman that a place could not speak, and he had had the days to ask her and had used them on something else, and he did not intend to do a smaller version of it in front of a boy with a barrow.

So he said the rest of it out loud, because saying it was not the second thing. The second thing would have been saying who.

“**Somebody has laid a course dry across the bed of a culvert that had already gone,**” he said, “**and a dry course laid over a bed that has gone is a lid, and a lid holds until there is scour under it, and then it does not hold.**”

Then he cleared the culvert.

It took the morning. There is not a great deal to describe about clearing a culvert and he did it well, and the description of it is that a man with a bad leg and a hook and a barrow and a boy of about seventeen with a barrow took out about nine barrows of weed and silt and set them on the low verge in a line, and the water came down about four inches in the first hour and about two more by the seventh, and by the ninth hour it was going under the road again and the made stone at the low edge was drying in the sun and there was a line of wet across it like the edge of a table.

A man of about fifty who lays stone came at the fifth hour and stood on the low verge and looked at the barrows and then at the bed.

“**I know,**” he said. “**We have known since the first of the month.**”

“**Then why has nobody said so?**” Aren Kest said.

“**Because it is not our road,**” the man of about fifty said. “**It is a road office thing, and the road office is four days off, and the grate is ours and the bed is theirs, and I have said so to about four people and none of them has been the road office.**”

“**Who laid the new course?**” Aren Kest said.

The man of about fifty looked at him for a while and then said that three men from the district had been there three weeks ago, and that they had lifted the silt and put in a new course and packed it with rubble and gone, and that they had had an instruction, and that he had not seen the instruction and had not asked for it, and that it had the shape of a sheet of paper with a name at the top of it, and that he did not know whose name because he had not read it, and that a man who has not read a thing cannot say what is in it.

That is a true thing and it is the whole of the reason the middle course of a culvert at the gate of a market town is not in anybody's knowledge, and the man of about fifty said it without any pleasure in saying it, and then he said the other thing.

“**We were told to keep the water off the road,**” he said, “**and we did, and the road is dry, and I am not going to be told about the bed of it by a man with a stick at the fifth hour of a market morning.**”

“**Then I have said it and you have heard it,**” Aren Kest said, “**and it is the bed under a road, and it is not mine and it is not yours, and I have no way of putting it in front of the right man except by saying it out loud on a made road, which I have now done, twice, to two people, neither of whom is the road office.**”

“**Then it is said,**” the man of about fifty said, and picked up a barrow and helped him empty the last of it, and neither of them thanked the other and neither of them said that the other had done it well.

At about the fourth hour, in the middle of the wet work, with his left hand in the silt up to the wrist and his right on the barrow handle, his left palm was warm.

He did not look at it. He has never told anybody about it, and it did not stop him, and he went on with the hook, and the boy of about seventeen said nothing about it because the boy did not notice, and by the ninth hour his hand was cold again and there was nothing to report.

At about the seventh hour the man of about thirty-four with a sheet came past the gate, on his way to somewhere else, and stopped, and looked at about nine barrows of silt in a line on the low verge and the water going under the road again.

“**How many?**” he said.

“**Nine,**” the boy of about seventeen said, before Aren Kest could answer, and the man of about thirty-four with a sheet wrote nine on the sheet he had with him, because a number of barrows is a number, and a number is the only thing that street gave anybody that day that could be put in a book, and he wrote it and did not say whose work it was, and the number went back down the lane with him in his coat.

The woman of about twenty-nine who keeps the room over the weigh-house was not on the street that morning, and the man of about fifty-eight with a badge said in the lane that the hearing stands and did not come down to the gate, and the market went on around about nine barrows of silt until the carts had them cleared or the carts had moved them, and nobody knew which.

At the ninth hour Aren Kest stood at the low verge with his stick in the mud and looked at about four hundred paces of made road with the water going under it again, and he knew more about the bed of that culvert than anybody in nine counties, and he had said it twice out loud, and the road was dry, and he was further from the work than he had been at the second hour.

The boy of about seventeen asked him whether he would be asked to come back.

“**I do not know,**” Aren Kest said, and it was the first straight answer he had given anybody in about a month, and the boy took it as one and went home.
