# Chapter 0646

**Greenmonth the fourth, YR 321, a Thursday and a market morning, about the third hour at the gate of the market town where the water goes under the road, a silted drain and three courses of sill with the middle one gone, and a man who lays stone who will not lay a fourth.**

The drain at the town's own gate had been silted and weeded for about three weeks and a cart's off-side wheel had gone into it on the low side on a Tuesday, and nobody had sent anybody, and the reason nobody had sent anybody is that the drain is on nobody's list and there is no rule in nine counties by which a man may mend one and be paid for it.

It was not a big piece of work. That was the thing that made the morning strange. Nine men could have had it out and in by the fourth hour, and the whole of the difficulty was in about three feet of a drain, three feet under a road, at the gate of the town, with the water coming in from four miles of made road and going under the town behind it. The weed in it was not weed any more. It had been silt and weed together for three weeks and it had gone to the consistence of a cold wet cake, and it came out of the drain in slabs that you could stand on.

Six men, a boy of about seventeen and a cart came off their own road at about the third hour and did not stop to explain themselves to anybody, and the man of about forty-four who keeps that road was four days off in the market and did not know about it until a carter told him on Friday.

The boy of about seventeen said the whole of his reason in about nine words while they were clearing.

“**The drain takes the water off about four miles of made road and it has been holding four miles of made road up since about Tuesday, and the last time it was cleared was when my master's father was in and nobody wrote that down either. We are not on anybody's round. We came because it is ours.**”

The middle course of the sill was gone and the top course had dropped about an inch and a half into the gap, and the man of about fifty who lays stone cleared it down to the stone and not to the mud under it, and he propped the sides with two uprights of rough stone and a bar of oak, and he took the fallen top course out whole and set it on the bank, and he said that a course that has dropped once and is put back is a course that will drop again.

Lime is the thing nobody in a street understands and the only thing that matters at three feet down. It is cold to make and it is hot to work, and it goes on wet stone and it holds a course up off its own back in about three weeks, and every man who lays stone has the heel of his hand burned about twice a year and does not mention it. The bed went on in two courses of lime and sand, and the man of about fifty who lays stone laid it with his own hands and not with a hod, and it took a long time, and nobody hurried him, and there was nothing to hurry him with.

Then he laid three, in lime, on a flat bed, and he laid them well.

A course of dressed sill stone is a foot deep and a yard long and it weighs more than two men want to lift at the wrong moment in a day, and it goes down on the bed with a sound the whole lane can hear, and it goes down true or it does not. The three went down true. The joints were tight enough that a thumbnail would not go in and the face of the wall was straight down the length of the drain and you could lay a straightedge on it and find nothing.

About the fourth hour he sat down on the bank with his back to the cart and did not lay the fourth, and about nine people who had come down the lane to watch a man mend a drain stood about a yard off, and he gave the reason nobody had asked him for, and it was not the reason he had given four other times.

“**Three and a bed is what a lime bed will take in that ground. I have laid four courses on a bed that had not been through a winter and I have watched it come up in about four months and take the third with it, and then there is a hole in the gate road and a hole that nobody laid and no man can account for, and the hole is mine because I put a course in on the strength of a morning. I am not arguing with the nine of you about who accounts for a course. I am telling you what lime does.**”

Then he got up, and he did not argue with anybody about it, and he did not explain it twice.

What he did instead was take the stone for the fourth course, about a yard of it, and carry it out of the way to the top of the bank and lay it down in a line on the grass where anybody coming off the road could see it, and put the mallet on top of it, and said that it was a course and not a lie, and that a man in about four months would be able to stand there and know there had been a fourth one and that it had not gone in, and that this was the whole of what he could leave anybody.

Nobody thanked him.

He washed the lime off his hands in the bucket and then he went back and looked at the props, and four people who had come to watch went and looked at the props, because he had not explained the props and there was nothing to explain. The two uprights stood in the silted sides with the oak bar across the top of them and a foot of lime under each, and if a man came back in about four years there would be two holes in the bank that showed where they had been, and the man who came back in about four years would be able to see that two men had stood there and had held something up while a third one cut stone out of a hole.

Nobody wrote that down either.

Aren Kest passed stones from the barrow to the mason's hands for about an hour, one at a time, and he did it on his good side with his stick stood against the barrow's frame, and the mason said nothing about it and four of the other five looked at it and two of them carried twice as fast afterwards.

He had laid stone with his own hands about four years ago, on a job that took nine days, before any of this. He knew what the hour was worth. It was the hour in which a man is not a man with a limp and a stick and a name in a clerk's hand; it was the hour in which he was two hands and a back and the whole of the arrangement was somebody else shouting for a stone. He had not known he missed it until the mason's hands came out for the second course and the first stone went on without a word between them, and he stood there with an empty barrow for about a second and a half before he understood why his chest had gone tight.

A man of about fifty who lays stone, refusing to lay a fourth course in front of nine people on a morning when every other man in the lane would have laid it, was doing the one thing Aren Kest had done in a room with nine people in it about four months ago, and was doing it in front of the same nine people, and had cost the same, and the difference was that the man with the mallet was going to be there in four months to be asked about it and Aren Kest was not going to be asked about anything at all, ever again, by anybody in nine counties, and had made sure of that himself.

He said none of this. He passed the next stone.

Then the wage, which is the part everybody came for and the part that took longest, and the first question about it was asked by a carter at the low end of the bank who wanted to know, out loud, in front of about nine people, who was going to pay for six men and a boy, and nobody answered him for about as long as it takes to get a load round.

“**Then who pays the boy?**” he said, when nobody had answered him. “**Not the cart, there is no cart to speak of, and the boy had a day and a half of it before light. Somebody here is going to put a hand in a pocket or somebody is going to tell me in a week that the boy was idle.**”

The man of about fifty-one who keeps a road in the glass country on the high flats said that the road money was not his to spend and that it came from somewhere about nine counties away and that a man could not take a penny out of it for a drain at somebody else's gate, and the man of about thirty-one from a road office said the same thing in different words and added that a road office could not pay for work it had not ordered.

The woman of about twenty-nine who keeps the room said that the room had fourpence a year and that it was fourpence a year because the deed said so, and that she was not going to pretend fourpence a year was a fund, and that she had said this in a lane a week ago and had not expected to be saying it in a market.

Then the man of about thirty-four with a sheet asked, out loud, in front of about nine people, how much was wanted, and about nine households at the gate road each put in what they said they could and the amounts were said out loud as they went in, because the copyist of about twenty-six stood there and made them say it, and the amounts ran from two pence to nine and one household put in nothing and said so out loud and was not argued with and was not asked twice.

The boy of about seventeen was paid a day and a half, because he had been on the bank before light, and he counted it in his own hand and then said out loud that he would rather have had the work, and he was given the work as well, and he did not say anything else about it and four people said they would have said something about it at his age and five did not.

The carrier who brought the cart said that the cart was his and that the wheel came out of the mud and not out of the stone and that he was not claiming for the cart, and the man of about thirty-four with a sheet wrote nine words about it and it went down the lane in a coat, and the carrier read the nine words and said they were right and said that nobody was going to pay him and that this was the correct arrangement.

Then the woman of about thirty-six who keeps a road in the county where a list of names was burned, who had come down the lane to look at a hole and had not said anything about it for about an hour, said one sentence and then went away.

“**A hole at a gate is a hole at a gate and it is not a rule and the two are not connected. Somebody will come along in about four months and put those two together so that the mending looks like an argument, and I have watched that done in about two counties and it is how a piece of work stops being a piece of work.**”

Nobody argued with her, and four people in that lane had not thought of it and five had.

Then the woman who keeps the room came down the lane with the boy of about eleven who holds a count, and the boy was carrying a board and a bit of chalk, and he chalked the two figures on the board that the four pence a year does not reach, and the man of about thirty-four with a sheet looked at the board and did not write the figures on his sheet and said aloud why, which was that a figure on a board in a lane is a figure that two people will remember differently in about a week.

At about the fifth hour the man of about fifty-two was not at the drain and had not been asked to come and nobody sent for him, and the road he would not name had sent nothing since the middle of Mudmonth, and the copyist said the fact out loud in the middle of the wage, as she says facts, and nine people heard it and nine were holding stone and did not stop.

Then the water came through, all of it, in about a quarter of an hour, and it came through the way water comes when it has been held up, which is to say all at once and harder than anybody expected. The drain filled to within two inches of the sill and then found the gap and went down the new bed, and the new bed took it. The cart came out over the sill without touching the middle course, and the man of about fifty who lays stone watched it come out and said nothing, and went up the bank to wash his hands, and the fourth course of stone lay in a line on the grass with the mallet on top of it where anybody could see it.

**And the cost of that Thursday is a column and it is nobody's, and three courses of sill and a fourth course laid out on a bank where anybody can see it and a gate road held up for about four miles and nine households that put in from two pence to nine are not in it and none of them has a price.**

**a barrow of river sand at 7, a hod of lime at 15, a bar of oak for a prop at 26, a basket of steel wedges at 27, a chalk line at 28, a bushel of sharp sand at 30, a bucket of water at 32, a mason's hammer at 34, a dressed sill stone at 40, and a second dressed sill stone at 41.** Seven, fifteen, twenty-six, twenty-seven, twenty-eight, thirty, thirty-two, thirty-four, forty and forty-one is two hundred and eighty pence. **A mark is forty-eight pence and two hundred and eighty pence is five marks and forty over.** The water kept going down the drain all afternoon and did not come up, and the six men and the boy went off up the lane about half an hour before the carts did, and the man of about fifty who lays stone stayed on the bank with the fourth course in front of him and the mallet on top of it for about as long as it takes to have a pipe of tobacco and then went home, and about four people came back up the lane after dark to look at it and it was all still there.
