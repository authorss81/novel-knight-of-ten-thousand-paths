# Chapter 0614

**Hearthmonth the ninth, YR 320, a Monday and not a market day, the second hour to the ninth at the culvert under the made road at the town's gate, a man of about fifty who lays stone and said he would come after the frost, three courses of sill, and a thing that is not to be written down.**

The frost had been and gone twice by the ninth of a month and the ground had gone with it and come back, and the water at the gate had been up and down in the night, which is when a culvert does its work.

Aren Kest was at the low verge at the second hour with a barrow and a hook, and the grating was clear and the water was going under the road, and the road was dry, and the low edge of the made stone had a line of wet across it about a foot and a half back from the edge where a wheel goes.

The man of about fifty who lays stone came at the fifth hour with a hod and a barrow of his own and a line, and he did not come earlier, and he said so before he put the hod down.

“**I said I would come after the frost,**” he said. “**I have had about nine days to decide whether to come at all, and I have come, and I would like that said in that order so that nobody in this town thinks I hurried.**”

“**You did not hurry,**” Aren Kest said.

“**No,**” the man of about fifty said. “**I have not hurried because the grate is mine and the bed is theirs, and a grate that is stopped is a road that is under water, and I am not going to spend the rest of my life explaining a thing to a road office four days off that will not come and look.**”

Then he looked at the bed, and it took him about two minutes, and he did it without saying anything, and he went down on the low side on his hands and knees with his head under the arch where a man of about fifty-eight cannot get, and he came out and wiped his face on his sleeve.

“**Your lid is gone,**” he said.

The boy of about seventeen, who sweeps that gate for a man who sweeps it for a woman who swept it before her, was the one who had told him about the water in the first week of a month and had said nothing else, and he was there with a barrow because he was always there on a Monday with a barrow.

“**Gone,**” Aren Kest said.

“**Gone, and the bed under it is not,**” the man of about fifty said. “**Come here and look at this. This is not a hole somebody dug. Nobody dug anything. That is a hole the water made, and it made it in about nine days, and it made it because there was a course laid over the top of it and a course laid over the top of a place the water is going wants nothing on top of it at all, it wants the stones gone so that it can take the bed as well.**”

He put his thumb in it. The scour was about a foot and a half across and about a foot and a half deep at the low side and shallower at the high, and the bed below it was hard and clean and had not moved, and the two courses that had been under the new one were where they had always been and had not shifted an inch, because they were laid in lime and the lime had gone hard in about four years and they are keyed to the sides of the culvert and not to the bed.

“**So the middle course is the one that goes,**” the man of about fifty said, and he said it the way a man says a thing he has known a long time and has never had cause to say.

“**Why the middle,**” the boy of about seventeen said.

“**Because the top course has the road on it and nobody can take it out without the road knowing,**” the man of about fifty said, “**and the bottom course has the bed under it and it is keyed in lime to the sides. The middle one has nothing above it that would fall and nothing below it that would hold it. It is the part of a thing that nobody looks at, and it is the part of a thing that goes, and I have been laying stone for about thirty-one years and I have never once been asked to look at a middle course.**”

Then he dug it out.

There is not a great deal to say about a man of about fifty digging out about a barrow and a half of rubble and a barrow and a half of stone in the cold with water coming at him from the low side, and he did it well, and the description of it is that it took the afternoon, and that the boy of about seventeen and the man of about thirty-two with a limp took out the stuff in barrows to the low verge in a line about nine barrows long, and that the man of about fifty went back to sound twice and stopped both times, and that at about the seventh hour he began to lay.

He laid three courses in lime, and he would not lay a fourth, and he said so before he laid the first, not after.

“**Three,**” Aren Kest said.

“**Three,**” the man of about fifty said. “**There were three and there are three again. I am not going to lay a fourth over a bed I have not seen all of, because a man who lays a fourth course over a bed he has not seen is doing exactly what those three men did, and they will not be here in nine years to be asked about it and neither will I, and this culvert will go in the third hard month and the water will find the next thing and there will be a fourth course, laid dry, by somebody who has been told to keep the water off the road.**”

“**I have not asked you to lay a fourth,**” Aren Kest said.

“**No,**” the man of about fifty said, “**and I am telling you before I start rather than after, because afterwards you would have had a reason, and I would rather you had the reason now while you can do something about it and are not going to.**”

He said the second true thing of that Monday at about the eighth hour, with his hands in lime to the wrist and the boy of about seventeen holding the hod, and he said it to the culvert and not to anybody.

“**I have laid stone for about thirty-one years,**” he said, “**and the worst job I ever saw was not one where the stone was wrong. It was one where the instruction was right. Somebody wanted the water off the road, and the water was off the road, and the road was dry, and it was dry for about three weeks, and nobody anywhere ever went and looked at the bed, and I have thought about that about once a month for the last month and I am not going to say anything else about it, because I have not read the instruction and a man who has not read a thing cannot say what is in it.**”

By the ninth hour the water was going under the road again and the made stone at the low edge was drying in the sun and there was a line of wet across it like the edge of a table, and it was the same as it had been on the second hour, and about nine barrows of stone and rubble were in a line on the low verge in the cold.

“**Is that going to be written down anywhere,**” the man of about fifty said, and he said it to the boy of about seventeen, and it was the first question he had asked all day.

The boy said that the man of about thirty-four with a sheet had written a number of barrows on that street once in a month and taken it away in his coat, and the man of about fifty said one word about that and the word was good, and the boy did not hear it as good and the man of about thirty-two heard it.

“**No,**” the man of about fifty said. “**I do not want it in a book. If it is in a book, then in about nine years there is a man who comes along and says it was always three courses here, and he will be talking about a lid that somebody else laid, and he will be right about the three courses and wrong about everything else, and the wrong part will be in a book and the right part will be in my head, and in about nine years my head will not be here.**”

The boy of about seventeen was paid a penny, in his hand, at the ninth hour, and he put it in his own pocket and did not give it to anybody and nobody saw him do it except the man of about thirty-two, who looked at the ground.

“**That was well done,**” the boy said, about the work, to nobody, and neither of the two men answered him, and the man of about fifty paid him and did not say that it was well done, and that is the whole of what the boy got out of a Monday and it was a penny and his own two hands.

At about the ninth hour he was still at the low verge with the stick in the mud, and about four hundred paces of made road had the water going under it again, and there was not one person in nine counties who knew as much about the bed under that road as he did, and about five weeks ago he had said it out loud twice to two people, neither of whom was the road office, and there was now a third fact about it that nobody had put him on to and that he would not have believed if it had been put to him: the middle of a thing is the part nobody looks at, and the middle of that culvert had gone twice inside about five weeks for the same reason, and the second time had been laid by three men who were told the right thing.

**And the cost of that Monday is a column and it is the stone layer's, and three courses of sill and a scour a foot and a half deep and a penny into a boy's own pocket are not in it and none of them has a price.**

**a used barrow wheel at one, a hod hook at seven, a bushel of lime at ten, a bucket at fourteen, a line peg at seventeen, a mason's hammer at eighteen, a scupper at twenty-six, a whetstone at thirty-one, a pair of gloves at thirty-two, a length of chain at thirty-seven, and a stone to sit on at forty-one.** One, seven, ten, fourteen, seventeen, eighteen, twenty-six, thirty-one, thirty-two, thirty-seven and forty-one is two hundred and thirty-four pence. **A mark is forty-eight pence and two hundred and thirty-four pence is four marks and forty-two over**, and three courses of sill, a cart's off-side wheel, the made road at the town's gate, about four hundred paces, a scour about a foot and a half across, nine barrows in a line, nine counties, about nine hundred sheets a month, about a hundred and four in one band, about four hundred and nine parish rolls a month, about eleven inches of drawer, about four hundred returns and a fourth head ruled and empty are thirteen figures of distance and of count and not one of them is a price, and a Monday, the second hour, the fifth hour, the seventh hour, the eighth hour, the ninth hour, about thirty-one years, about nine days and about seven months are nine figures of time and not one of them goes in at either end of this column. **Aren Kest and a man of about fifty who lays stone and a boy of about seventeen with a barrow and three men from the district who are not in this county and a man of about thirty-four with a sheet who does not come on a Monday and a woman of about fifty who keeps a road with a slate in her apron and a man of about fifty-eight with a badge and about nine households at the low side of that gate are eight figures of persons, and the third of them earned a penny and the fifth of them wrote down a number once and the fourth of them laid a course dry and none of them is going to be asked about it.** Nobody thanked anybody and nobody was thanked and the number of barrows was not written down.
