# Chapter 0645

**Greenmonth the first, YR 321, a Monday and not a market morning, the second hour in the market street, the room over a weigh-house shut behind its own door, and the hearing standing in the open with about nine people in it who keep no road at all.**

The room over a weigh-house was shut, because it is shut on every day but a market morning, and this was a Monday, and the woman of about twenty-nine who keeps the room had said in a lane on the first of Mudmonth that she would not have it opened for nine people who asked nicely and she was not going to have it opened for about nine people who had not asked.

A Monday morning in that street is not a market morning and nothing in it pretends to be. There were no carts at the boards and no barrows and nobody selling anything off a barrow, and the stone that a market morning covers up was lying open in the middle of the street, grey and pitted, with the old nails of forty markets still in it. Two carts went through at a walk with loads of ordinary work on them, a load of lime and a load of sacking, and they went through without stopping, because a Monday is a day for carts that have already been paid for. The light was thin and green, the first cold bright morning of the month, and about nine people stood in it with their hands in their sleeves and were not uncomfortable and were not enjoying it.

There is a thing that happens in a street on the first of a month that nobody has standing to stop it, which is that about a fifth of the people who come have never come before and do not know whether they are allowed to be there. Four of them stood at the edges that morning and stayed at the edges, and about nine of them knew somebody, and the badge-man spent the first two minutes saying one sentence over and over in nine different ways, which was that anybody could stand there and that there was nothing to stand in and that nobody was going to ask anybody for a reason.

The hearing stood in the street anyway, on the first of every month, where it has stood, with no bench in it and nothing at the top of it, and the badge-man said the standing notice in the words he uses and nine people heard it and nine more were doing something else.

Then he did the thing he had never done on a first of a month, and he said afterwards that he had thought about it for about three weeks and that the reason he had never done it was that nobody had ever asked him to.

“**I am going to say the three clauses out loud in this street, and I have never said them out loud in a street in my life, and the reason I am doing it is that the hearing is the one thing in nine counties that a person who keeps no road can stand in. About nine of you have never been in the room over a weigh-house. About nine of you keep no road and are on no sheet and are owed nothing by anybody. This is the only morning of the month where that is any use to you, and I would like to use it.**”

Nobody thanked him.

He said the three clauses out loud in the market street on a Monday morning, slowly, and four people heard all three and five heard the first and the second and lost the third, and he started the third one again for the five and did not start the first one again for anybody.

Aren Kest had heard the three clauses four times in four years and a half and had never once heard them said in a street. He stood at the low end of the boards and listened to the badge-man say them out loud to a Monday morning in the open, and what he found, and found it earlier than he expected to find it, was that the sentences were better in the open. In a room they were a thing people were careful with. In the street, said that loud and at that pace, they were just sentences, and about four people who were not keepers and had no business there understood all of them without being helped, and he thought that if the whole of the last year had been spent finding out how to say a thing in a room, and it had, and it had nearly cost him a road, then somebody ought to have gone out of the room about a year earlier and said them on a Monday.

Then the woman of about thirty-six who keeps a road in the county where a list of names was burned said the thing the rule does not cover, in a street, to about nine people who keep no road, which is the worst possible place to say it and the only place she had ever been asked to say it in.

“**I said this in a room in Thawmonth and I am saying it in a street in Greenmonth and I am going to go on saying it in streets. This rule covers roads that can be written to. Every one of the three clauses wants somebody found. A notice wants a person at the end of a road. A witness wants a person named by the people who work the road. A burden wants a man who can be found afterwards. And there is a road that nobody in nine counties can write to, and it went quiet in the middle of the nine days and nothing has come from the end of it since, and not one of the three clauses touches it.**”

Four people in that market stopped.

“**And the meeting has told you what it is going to do about that, and I will say the answer out loud so that nobody can say afterwards that it was kept back. The answer is that it is going to do nothing. Not a rider and not a form and not a column and not a name. Nothing. And that is the correct answer, because every one of the four of them I have watched in about four months make it worse, and I am not going to be the fifth. And I want it said in the same breath that this is not a small thing and I will not have anybody in this street say it is. In my county a list of names was burned and the men who burned it did not hate anybody and it was a sheet, and a sheet with good manners on it is still a sheet, and I have three good manners here in nine hands and the hole is exactly the size of the hole that was in my county before.**”

Nobody argued with her and nobody told her she was wrong and nobody told her it was a small matter.

Then a woman at the back of about forty-five, who had not said anything and had not been spoken to, asked the badge-man for a piece of it, and asked for it the way a person asks for a loaf.

“**Can I have one of the three?**”

The badge-man said no at once, and he gave the reason at once, and he did not soften it and he did not apologise for it.

“**No. Not because there is no copy. There is not a copy, and that is the whole of what the three of them are for, and you can be asked about any one of them by anybody at any time, including the ninth of us, including the woman who keeps the book, including me. I cannot give you one because a piece is a thing one person holds and can be asked about, and if I gave you one then within about a month there would be two people in nine counties holding one and neither of you would be asked about it, and in about a year there would be a drawer of them in a room, and a drawer is where a decision goes. I am not doing it to keep a thing from you. I am doing it because a rule that can be handed round is a thing nobody is responsible for.**”

The woman of about forty-five said that this was the first time in about nine years that anybody had told her the truth about why she could not have something, and she said it without heat, and four people heard her.

The man of about thirty-one from a road office said that a road office had a press and a ream and a man who could set a thing in a hand in about a day, and the copyist of about twenty-six answered him before he had finished the sentence.

“**You have a form,**” she said. “**A form has a head. A rule that lands in a form has a head, and the head is what makes it a decision, and I have spent about four years and a half watching a room that had a head and could not be argued with and I am not going to do the arranging of that for nine counties on a Monday.**”

He said that he had not been offering, and the copyist said that he had, and four people agreed with her and five said nothing, and she counted them out loud, because she counts things out loud.

The woman of about thirty-four who came four days for a sentence stood at the low end of the boards and said it again, once, in the words she had used in the lane, and nobody answered her and nobody stopped her and nobody asked her to come back inside.

Then a man of about thirty-eight who keeps a road said the other thing that happened that morning, and he said it to the badge-man and not to anybody else, and he said it because the room was shut and the market street was the only place left and about nine people who keep no road were standing in it.

“**A road of mine sends a note to this room about four times a month. It is not a road, it is a place where a road is used, and the note says the same four sentences and the four sentences never change. I have never asked them to change and I have never asked them why. About nine people keep the three series and not one of my four sentences is in any of them, and the reason not one of them is in any of them is that the series are right and mine does not ask them anything. I am not blaming anybody. I am standing in a street on the first of a month saying it out loud.**”

Nobody wrote it down and the man of about thirty-four with a sheet had no satchel that morning and the copyist said that nothing would be written about it and that this was the correct standing and that she had been keeping it for about four years and a half and that she would go on keeping it and that she was not going to be thanked for it.

Aren Kest was at the low end of the boards with his stick against the edge of the platform for the whole of the hearing and heard all three clauses said in a street for the first time, and when the woman of about forty-five asked for a piece he looked at the ground. It was not shame. He had been the first one, nine days ago, in a room with nine people in it, asking for a thing that could be given to him and being refused in a way that turned out to be correct, and he had gone out of that room and stood about four hundred paces from it and worked at something else for a while. He looked at the ground because he could not think of one thing to say to a woman of about forty-five that would be worth her having been told the truth for, and he was a man who was good at exactly one thing on a market morning, and it was not that.

Afterwards he said that this was the second time in about nine days that a person had asked for a thing in a room and been refused in a way that was correct, and that the first one had been him.

“**Does the room have to be opened on a Sunday for a hearing?**” the badge-man said, and the woman who keeps the room said no, and four people in that market said that they had been in nine counties thirty years and had never once known a room to be opened for them on a day it was shut.

Then the badge-man said the hearing stood in the street on the first of every month and not in the room, and that the room was shut on a Monday and would be shut until Thursday, and that four of the about nine people who had never been in the room said they would come again on a first of a month and five said they had somewhere to be.

One of the four was a woman of about sixty who said that she would come on a first of a month and would bring somebody with her, and the badge-man said that there was no arrangement for that and that anybody could come, and she said that she knew there was no arrangement for it and that she was going to come anyway, and four people in that market laughed and five did not.

**And the cost of that Monday is a column and it is nobody's, and a hearing with no bench in it and nine people in it who keep no road and a piece of paper that was not given to anybody and a door on a lane with a rule standing behind it are not in it and none of them has a price.**

**a bundle of faggots at 6, a lantern at 10, a hearth brush at 23, a pot of tallow at 25, a rope of chalk at 27, a hand shovel at 32, a brush of tar at 33, a water butt at 35, a kettle at 38, and a sack of sand at 40.** Six, ten, twenty-three, twenty-five, twenty-seven, thirty-two, thirty-three, thirty-five, thirty-eight and forty is two hundred and sixty-nine pence. **A mark is forty-eight pence and two hundred and sixty-nine pence is five marks and twenty-nine over.** The woman of about sixty went off up the lane to find out who she was going to bring, and she was gone about four minutes, and she came back with her arm through the arm of a girl of about fourteen who had not come on her own account and was looking at the boards as though she had been dragged to something, and the badge-man said nothing about it and the woman of about sixty said out loud that the arrangement was no arrangement and she was going to do it anyway, and about nine people saw the two of them standing there in the thin green light and about five of them understood the whole of what the first of a month was for.
