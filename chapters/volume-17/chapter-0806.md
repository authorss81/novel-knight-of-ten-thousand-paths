# Chapter 0806

**Hearthmonth the twenty-second, YR 323, a Tuesday and not a market morning, the room over that weigh-house shut behind its own door and the hearing not sitting and no market in that street, the fourth hour on a floor four days off with seven people on it, twenty-six days after a sheet went up on those boards, and the second of three things said out loud in one street in one week by two people who are not him.**

There were nine people in that street at the ninth hour and a man planing hurdle rails against the wall of the third door. The bolt had been on the room over that weigh-house since noon on the Saturday before, and nobody had gone up the four hundred pace lane that holds it.

Nine doors down the cloth lay over the bench with eleven weeks of wet on it and no mark of any kind on the cloth itself, which is the one thing about it nobody in that street has ever thought to check.

Four days out, ninety-three drove have gone up that lane since the second of Mudmonth and the same ninety-three went up five days ago. Five days is now the interval at which that figure changes, and a figure nobody hears change is a figure nobody in nine counties is counting.

Eight of the nine yards of wall at the head of that made road are back in and the gap in it is nine feet long and four feet high, and one man has been carrying the stone two miles in his own hands for six hundred and fifty days, which is about nine hundred journeys, and nobody in nine counties has asked him for the figure.

Four days off, on a floor above a byre, seven people said a number at the fourth hour. It was the two hundred and seventy-first morning.

“Twice.”

Four. Four. The number had been four for two hundred and sixty-three mornings and had been eight before the water.

“He has not been down here since Wolfmonth,” said the man with the bad hip. “Not once. You know why.”

“He does not come because he does not want to be counted, and he is not wrong to want that.”

“He is not wrong. He is just the only one of the seven who has worked out what the counting is for.”

“Which is what you have just said.”

The woman at the door put her hand on the frame and looked at the two of them for a while and then said that a man who has worked out what the counting is for and stays away from it is a man who has understood the arrangement better than the arrangement deserves, and that this was not a compliment to the arrangement.

The register in that room has ten ruled questions, no findings under any of them, and twenty-six days of gaining none. The day-book stands at eleven entries. Nothing stands under the fourth ruled head and no fifth has ever been cut, and the copyist of twenty-six ruled no head and has been told about none of it. The press stands open and undated, and behind it a leaf of the day-book carries nine figures and a date and the name of nobody at all.

The stone sill of that weigh-house door carries four lines of chalk under the hood that keeps the rain off it. They mean four. They have been dry and sharp there since the forty-ninth of Mudmonth and nobody has read them out loud to anybody. Nine people went past that door that morning and four of them looked down and five did not.

The ninth mile stone is the height of a knee, and the clean ring round the foot of it has filled with grit for about the fourth time since the forty-ninth of Mudmonth. A ring that fills is a ring somebody does about once a fortnight. Four hundred yards past it the eleventh mile stone carries nine hundred names cut into the side of it going back two hundred years, and nobody in nine counties can read more than nine of them, and about two hundred years is twenty generations of people who cut a name into a thing and none of them ever came back to read one.

Then, at about the tenth hour, the man planing those rails at the third door was asked one question about his own trade.

He was asked it by a person who wanted to know what a hurdle was made of and what it cost to have one made. He was forty-four or thereabouts and he did not put the plane down.

“**I have made four hundred hurdles in his life and every one of them went up where the person who wanted it put it, and a line with a name on it is the end of that.**”

Wait. He caught it himself and laughed at it and said it again.

“**I have made four hundred hurdles in my life and every one of them went up where the person who wanted it put it, and a line with a name on it is the end of that.**”

Nine people heard all of it. Five did not.

Nobody argued with him about any part of it. Nobody said anything back to him for the length of time it takes to turn a cart twice over, and the nine of them went on with what they had in their hands.

A carter who had come in for loading asked him what he took for a hurdle, and he told him, and the carter said his was dearer, and he said it was dearer, and the carter said he had never once been told what he took for a hurdle, and he said that was the first time and he would not say it twice.

A man who had been listening from the second door came across and put a question to the hurdle maker that nobody else in that street had thought to ask.

“Then what happens to the hurdle.”

The man with the plane looked up. “What do you mean.”

“If a line has a name on it and the name is a man, and the man is gone or is ill or does not want it, then who takes the hurdle down and puts it somewhere else, because I have been to houses where a man has died and the hurdles are still exactly where he put them and nobody will move them.”

“That,” said the hurdle maker, “is the first sensible thing anybody has said to me this month, and I have not got an answer to it, and I have been putting hurdles up for twenty-six years and I have never once been asked what happens to them after.”

“So you will not put a name on a line.”

“I will not put a name on a line because I do not know what a name on a line is for, and a man who does not know what a thing is for should keep his hand off it, and that is not politics. That is carpentry.”

Nobody argued with him about that either, and nine people went on what they were doing, and the man at the second door stood where he was for a while and then went back inside without saying anything, which was the first time anybody in that street had seen him do that.

The man of thirty-two came down the four hundred pace lane at the ninth hour and stood in that street and heard the second of the three things said and said nothing about it and did not answer it and did not say that it was wrong. He kept it to the eleventh hour.

He had meant to hear three things in one week out of three people who are not him, and he had heard two.

Standing there with the leg going he did the arithmetic that he could not stop doing, and it came out the same as it had come out twice before. A woman of thirty-nine will not speak for nine households because when she says it, they will all say she said it. A man of forty-four will not put a name on a line because four hundred hurdles went up wherever the man who wanted one put it, and a name on a line fixes it in one place and takes it out of his hands.

Neither of them is the sheet. Neither of them is for or against the offer. They are two different reasons, held by two people who have never met, in one street, in one week, and they cannot both be carried by a county of two thousand people. If you build on the first you have a county that cannot speak for itself. If you build on the second you have a county that cannot be held still.

Thirty-seven days were left on the sheet. The roll has been on those boards one hundred and seventy days with nine empty lines on each of three copies and the fourth of them this county's.

The rest of that Tuesday went on. The near wheel of a cart came off at the low end of that street and the carter got it up again with a bar in the time it takes to turn a cart twice over, and the man planing the rails got his plane off the third door and set it down on his knee. The two women at the third door could not agree about a bolt of cloth for the four minutes it takes to boil a kettle, and came out of the shop with it wrapped and neither of them speaking.

The light went off the west bank and found the upper edge of those boards, and the lamp at the high end of them was not lit, because the ninth hour of a Tuesday in Hearthmonth is dark and there is nothing off the wet slate of them to need it. Five went out into five trades and four stayed, and the light went off the wet slate.

At the causeway four days south the hand lamp goes on the coping every Monday afternoon in the winter, and the twenty-second of Hearthmonth is a Tuesday, and the lamp was not put there. The water was off the causeway and the hand bell on the gate post is the size of a fist and was not rung, and four hundred people live on the far bank of that water and have never once been across in about nine years.

The five frames of brood in the walled yard are under nine straw skirts and the board is still on the skirt stones at the gate, and the young man of nineteen has been in and out nine times with a honey pail and has not touched a frame. Nothing in that yard is on any board in nine counties and nothing in it is on any line anybody has written this month.

Between the eleventh mile stone and the top of that hill there are two miles of wall and orchard owned by nine households, and a man who has made four hundred hurdles in his life is the only person in this county who can say what a hurdle is for, and nobody has asked him.

Behind the ninth of the nine stones there are nine hundred yards dead inside its own width, four hundred and seventeen days old, and nobody has ever been past the ninth of them.

He came out of that market street at the eleventh hour and went up the lane, and the made road past the ninth mile stone was not on it. The hurdles are still across the top of that cart track, and the thumb of lime on the shoe of the top one is powder, and a man who makes four hundred of those has not been up there to see it. Five things on that board and thirty-seven days left on the sheet and the fourth line of the roll still empty on all three of its copies.

**And the cost of that Tuesday is a man of forty-four saying thirty-seven words out loud once in one street about four hundred hurdles in his life and a line with a name on it, and nine hundred people not hearing it and nobody arguing and nobody agreeing, a sheet twenty-six days on a board and thirty-seven days left in it, three questions on one frame that do not agree with one another, three copies of a roll with nothing on any of them and the fourth line empty on all three, seventy-seven days of a bleed on the inside of one wrist that nobody in nine counties knows about, one thing gone quiet three hundred and sixty-nine days ago and alive and shut and unnamed and unheld, nine hundred yards of grass four days out that will not come back, the use of one ear, the use of one leg, and a year, none of which is in any of it and none of which has a price.**
