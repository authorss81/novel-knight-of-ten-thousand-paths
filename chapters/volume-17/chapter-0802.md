# Chapter 0802

**Hearthmonth the first, YR 323, a Tuesday and not a market morning, the room over the weigh-house shut behind its own door and the hearing not sitting and no market in that street, the fourth hour on a floor four days off with seven people on it, five days after a sheet went up on those boards, and the twenty-third first of a month in a row arriving at five things and standing at none of them.**

There was a hard white frost on the slate of the boards at the low end and nine people in that street at the ninth hour, and the frost had got into the ruled lines of the roll so that the empty lines on the topmost copy looked ruled twice. Nobody had gone up the four hundred pace lane. The bolt on the room over the weigh-house had been on since noon on the Saturday before.

Nine doors down the cloth was over the bench, and the frost had got into the weave of it and given the hem a hard edge that would have cut anybody who caught it barehanded.

Four days out along that made road, the first of a month came and went, and the ordinary part of the first of a month is that nine hundred people along a made road count what is in the cart and find out that it is not as much as it was. That is the whole of what the first of Hearthmonth did on that road. Four hundred people along it did not know that a sheet had gone up at the far end of it on Friday.

The twenty-third first of a month in a row had come to those boards and found nothing standing at them. Nine people saw it come. Nine saw it go.

The sheet had been on that frame five days. Fifty-eight days of it were left, and eight weeks was what fifty-eight days was, and a date gets nearer while it is on a board whether anybody looks at it or not.

Then, at the ninth hour, at the boards, a man read the two sentences of it out loud.

He was not asked to. He had come off the made road with a handbar over his shoulder and he stopped, and he read it the way a man reads a thing he has seen once before, which is to say without stopping.

“**It wants a name.**”

He waited, as though the street had asked him something and this was the beginning of the answer.

“**A name is a person. I have not got one for it and I do not know one in this street.**”

Nobody asked him to read it again. A woman with a basket on her arm said that it wanted one name and there were nine counties, and he said he knew how many counties there were, she said she was not asking him to know, she was asking whether he had anything, and he said no, and she said right, and went on. A carter's mate said his father had turned down work at the causeway once and that this was not the same as this, and the man with the handbar said it was not, and that was the end of it, and the carter's mate looked at the sheet for a while longer after that than a man who has agreed with something usually does.

Nine people heard all of it. Five did not. Nobody asked him his name either, which he would have given.

Four days out, on a floor above a byre, seven people were standing on it at the fourth hour.

The woman of twenty-nine had the second lamp at the foot of the stairs lit and had gone back down to the middle of the room and left the two of them to it. Two hundred and fifty-six mornings. The number had been four for two hundred and forty-eight mornings and had been eight before the water and had not gone back, and the six mornings lost in the nine days of water in Mudmonth were gone and were not being recovered by anybody.

“Say it twice,” said the woman at the door, from the dark. “Not because I have not heard you. Because the second one is the one they will write down.”

He said it twice. Four. Four.

“The old man heard that,” said the man with the bad hip, who was fifty-one and had a brother over on the other road. “He was outside. He does not count himself any more and he is right not to.”

“He counts himself.”

“He counts himself and he does not say so. That is a different thing and I have never worked out which of the two it is.”

Seven people on a floor and none of them had ever written the number down. That was the arrangement. It had been made in the first year and it had never been broken, and the reason it had never been broken was that nobody outside that floor had ever asked any of them for it, and a thing nobody asks for does not need a record.

The register in the room over the weigh-house held ten questions with no finding under any of them and gained none. The day-book stood at eleven entries. The fourth ruled head was empty and no fifth head was ruled. The copyist of twenty-six had not come down those stairs on a Tuesday and had not come down them on a Tuesday in four and a half years. The press stood open with no date on it. At the back of the day-book a leaf carried nine figures and a date and the names of nobody.

“Say nothing about this anywhere,” said the man with the bad hip. “Not to the officers and not to anybody with a board.”

“I have never said anything about it.”

“No. You have never said anything about it. That is not a thing to be proud of, it is a thing to be careful with. Somebody will offer you a piece of paper one day and the paper will want a figure on it, and you will be the man who has one.”

“Seven,” said the woman at the door. “The sheet wants a name and we have got seven of them on this floor and not one of us is the person it wants. Say that again in six weeks and it will still be true and it will be a worse thing to say then.”

The lamp went out at the sixth hour and the seven of them came down off the floor one at a time so that the stairs would not know how many of them there were.

The man of thirty-two came down the four hundred pace lane at the ninth hour. Four hundred paces of a lane and four hundred yards of a made road, and the hot band across the knee by the seventh hour on a Tuesday, because a Tuesday carries a ninth of what a Saturday carries and he had nowhere to stand and nothing to lift.

He stood at the boards. He read the sheet himself. He did not touch it and he did not touch any of the three copies of the roll.

He had come down to see whether a county which had refused two papers for a year would do anything at all about the first of a month, and nothing about the first of a month happened, and five days before the first of a month a sheet had gone up on that frame in the ordinary post, and nine people had heard it read once by a man who did not know a name for it.

He thought about the man with the handbar for longer than he expected to. He had come here four and a half years ago to stop being asked where a figure came from, and in four and a half years the whole of what he had learned about the way a county speaks was that it will speak through a stranger with a handbar if you leave one standing long enough, and it will say the true thing, and it will say it to nine people, and it will not say it again.

He could have told the man with the handbar that there were four names in that street he could have had by the tenth hour. He stood there and did the arithmetic of it and then he did the other arithmetic, the one about a name taken off a person in a market street, and he had done that arithmetic once already five days ago and it had come out the same way twice.

Before he left the boards he was asked a question by the woman with the basket, the one who had asked the man with the handbar for something to hold.

“You were here Friday.”

“I was.”

“You read it.”

“I stood at it.”

“Did you think about putting a name to it?”

He had. He had thought about it on Friday and again on Friday night with the leg up on a stool, and the answer had not changed in either place, and he found that he could say so out loud without any difficulty at all, which was new.

“Yes. And I am not going to, and if you ask me why I will give you the true reason and not a good one.”

“I am asking,” she said.

“Because the moment a name goes on that line it stops being anybody's to take back. That is the true reason and it is not a good one, and I have had four and a half years to find a better one and I have not.”

She put the basket down on its side, which she did not do for nobody.

“That is the first true thing anybody has said on this street about that paper,” she said, “and you are the fourth person to say it and the first one to say it twice.”

The rest of the day went on. The near wheel of a cart came off at the low end of that street and the carter got it up again with a bar in the time it takes to turn a cart twice over. The two women at the third door argued about a bolt of cloth for about four minutes by the clock and then went into the shop and came out with it wrapped. The inn at the high end had two of its four beds full and kept its tally-stick by the door of it and put no board out on the made street. Nine people went along that street at the fourth hour who had not been in it at the ninth.

The light went off the west bank and found the upper edge of the boards, and the lamp at the high end of them was not lit, because the ninth hour of a Tuesday in Hearthmonth is dark and there is nothing off the frosted slate of them to need it. Five of them went out into five trades. Four stayed on at the boards until the lamp went out.

Four days out, eight of the nine yards of wall at the head of that made road are back in and the gap in it is nine feet long and four feet high. One man has been carrying the stone two miles in his own hands for six hundred and twenty-nine days. The ninth yard has not been started and the mallet is on the bank two miles off with the line of stone and has not been picked up.

At the head of the cart track the nine hurdles are still across it, and the thumb of lime on the shoe of the top one has gone to powder. The last lime cart came up that track three hundred and forty-eight days ago and the road behind the hurdles has been shut three hundred and twenty-nine days. He did not go up it.

Nine feet across and nine hundred yards long, behind the ninth of nine stones set at the same height as a man's knee, there is grass that has been dead inside its own width for three hundred and ninety-six days. Nobody has ever been past the ninth of the stones. On the flat beyond them nine households graze beasts on a rota they have kept for nine years and nobody has looked over the stones to see it.

Between the eleventh mile stone and the top of that hill there are two miles, a mile of stone wall on the high side and a mile of orchard on the low side, and the owners of the nine households between them have kept that mile for nine years. A form with one line for a county is eight lines too short for them and nobody has asked any of them what they would put on the rest.

The two women at the third door, the inn with two beds, the man with the handbar going home down the made road without a name, seven people on a floor four days off who said a number twice each.

He left that market street at the eleventh hour and went up the four hundred pace lane and did not go up the made road past the ninth mile stone. Three questions on that frame and none of them answered. Eight weeks left on the sheet. The fourth line of the roll empty on all three of its copies.

**And the cost of that Tuesday is a twenty-third first of a month in a row arriving at five things on a board and standing at none of them, one man reading a sheet out loud once to nine people and nine hundred people along eleven miles of made road not hearing it, a number said twice in the dark on a floor four days off for the two hundred and fifty-sixth morning and written down nowhere, three questions on one frame that do not agree with one another, three copies of a roll with nothing on any of them, fifty-six days of a bleed on the inside of one wrist that nobody in nine counties knows about, one thing gone quiet three hundred and forty-eight days ago and alive and shut and unnamed and unheld, nine hundred yards of grass four days out that will not come back, the use of one ear, the use of one leg, and a year, none of which is in any of it and none of which has a price.**
