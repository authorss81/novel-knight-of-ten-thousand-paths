# Chapter 0804

**Hearthmonth the fourteenth, YR 323, a Monday and not a market morning, the room over the weigh-house shut behind its own door and the hearing not sitting and no market in that street, the fourth hour on a floor four days off with seven people on it, eighteen days after a sheet went up on those boards, and a man with a wain who has said one true thing out loud in that street once.**

There were nine people in that street at the ninth hour and a wain standing at the low end of the made road with its tailboard down, and a hard grey sky doing nothing. The bolt had been on the room over the weigh-house since noon on the Saturday before and nobody had gone up the four hundred pace lane that holds it.

The cloth was over the bench nine doors down, and a man carrying a ladder past it caught his foot in the hem and swore at it and did not once look down to see what he had caught it in.

Four days out, ninety-two drove have gone up that lane since the second of Mudmonth and the same ninety-two went up four days ago. A drove that has not moved in four days is a drove somebody has stopped counting, and the only person in nine counties who still counts it is the man who counts it.

Eight of the nine yards of wall at the head of that made road are back in, the gap in it nine feet long and four feet high, and one man has been carrying the stone two miles in his own hands for six hundred and forty-two days. Nine feet of gap in a wall is the width of the made road and the width of the broom, and nobody in nine counties has asked him to close it.

Four days off, on a floor above a byre with the cow door open on the cold, seven people stood up and said a number.

It was the two hundred and sixty-fifth morning. The number had been four for two hundred and fifty-seven mornings and had been eight before the water.

“Twice,” said the woman at the door.

He said it twice. Four. Four. The man with the bad hip wrote nothing, because nothing is written, and the six mornings lost in the nine days of water in Mudmonth are gone and are not being recovered.

“They will ask somebody one day,” the man with the bad hip said. “They always ask the man who is easiest to find and not the man who knows.”

“Then we will be seven men who are easy to find and no man who knows.”

“That is a worse answer and it is the true one.”

The register in the room over the weigh-house holds ten questions with no finding under any of them and gains none. The day-book stands at eleven entries. The fourth ruled head is empty and no fifth head is ruled. The copyist of twenty-six has not come down those stairs on a Monday and has not come down them on a Monday in four and a half years. The press stands open with no date on it, and at the back of the day-book a leaf carries nine figures and a date and the names of nobody.

On the sill of that weigh-house door, under the hood where the rain does not reach, there are four lines of chalk. They mean four. They have been dry and sharp there since the forty-ninth of Mudmonth and nobody has read them out loud to anybody in four and a half years. Nine people went past that door that morning and four of them looked down.

Then, at the ninth hour, the man with the wain was asked one question about his own trade.

He was asked it by a person who wanted to know what a man did with four miles of made road every week of his life, and he answered standing with his hand on the tailboard.

“**I run a wain between the causeway and this town once a week and I pay for the loading of other people's carts at the low end of the made road.**”

Nine people heard all of it and four had not. A man at the second door said that was not much of a trade, and the man with the wain said it was the trade there was, and that the loading was the part nobody counted, and the man at the second door said his grandfather had loaded at the low end for nothing, and the man with the wain said so had his, and that was the end of it and it was not unfriendly.

He was asked then what to call him, because somebody wanted to know who to ask a second time, and he told them, and it was twenty-four words, and nine people heard it.

“**It is Ninian Crowe and I have said the thing about those four miles once and about nine hundred people have not heard it.**”

Then, half an hour later, in the middle of that street, with the loading for that week finished and his own cart still standing there, he said one more thing out loud. Once. In his own trade. To nine people. It was twenty-seven words and nobody stopped him at the first one.

“**Those four miles are the smoothest four miles of made road in nine counties and they were not smooth before somebody with a warrant came up them.**”

Nobody argued with him about any part of it. Nobody in that street said anything back to him for as long as it takes to turn a cart twice over, and nine people went on what they were doing.

A woman at the third door said she had heard that same thing said about that same four miles by somebody else in the summer. He said he knew who, and she said it was not the same because it had been said in the summer and this was said in the winter, and he said that was fair, and she went in.

Four people in nine counties have said since that the true thing about a road is the easiest kind of thing to say out loud in a street, and nine hundred people not hearing it is the ordinary way a county hears anything at all. Five have said that a man who says a true thing once and is not argued with has begun to think that not having been heard is a kind of safety.

He is right about that. It is not safety. It is the thing that happens instead of safety when a county has no way to be argued with.

The man of thirty-two came down the four hundred pace lane at the ninth hour and stood at the boards and heard all three of those things said and said nothing about any of them. He kept it to the eleventh hour. He had meant to see whether the people who had called that sheet the best offer anybody in this county had ever been made would say a second thing out loud, and a man with a wain did say a second thing out loud, and it was about four miles of stone and not about the sheet at all.

That had been the shape of it for eighteen days now. Four people had said the sheet was the best offer anybody here had ever been made. A woman had said she would not speak for nine households. A man with a wain had said his four miles were the smoothest in nine counties. None of the three was a refusal of anything. All three were refusals of something else.

He stood there and did the thing he does, which is to take the true part and turn it over and ask whether it can be carried. A wain that goes up four miles every week and a county that cannot answer a printed sheet are not the same size, and that is the whole difficulty, and there is no arrangement of words that makes them the same size.

Forty-five days were left on the sheet.

The man with the wain came past him on his way out and stopped, and he did it the way a man stops when he has already said the hard part and has one more thing in him.

“You have not said anything.”

“No.”

“Not to me and not to anybody.”

“Not to you and not to anybody.”

“Then what are you going to do with the four miles?”

“I am going to keep running them,” he said, “which is what I do with them, and that is the whole of it, and I would like you to know that I know it is not enough.”

Crowe shifted the handbar off his shoulder. He said that in four and a half years the man with the stick had never once been wrong about a road and had never once been asked about one, and that he had assumed those two things belonged together and had just been shown that they did not.

“You said the sheet was the best offer anybody here has ever been made,” the man of thirty-two said.

“I did not say that. A wright said that.”

“You have not argued with it.”

“No. And I have run four miles on it every week for a year and a bit, and the four miles are better than they were, and I have never once told anybody whose doing it was, and if you ask me why I will not tell you that either.”

“Why not?”

“Because then it is a thing one man did and not a thing the road is, and I want it to be a thing the road is.”

He went up the lane and the wain went down the made road with its tailboard up and neither of them said anything else, and that was the third thing said in that street that week that two people were not going to be thanked for.

The rest of that Monday went on. The near wheel of a cart came off at the low end of that street and the carter got it up again with a bar in the time it takes to turn a cart twice over, and nobody stopped on his account. The two women at the third door took a bolt of cloth apart in the doorway over four minutes by the clock, counting the threads at the selvedge against each other, and went in after it and came out with it wrapped. The inn at the high end had two of its four beds full and chalked the two occupied ones on its tally-stick by the door and put no board out on the made street, because there was nothing left to fill.

The light went off the west bank and found the upper edge of the boards, and the lamp at the high end of them was not lit, because the ninth hour of a Monday in Hearthmonth is dark and there is nothing off the wet slate of them to need it. Five of the people at those boards went out into five trades and four stayed.

At the causeway four days south the hand lamp goes on the coping every Monday afternoon in the winter, and the fourteenth of Hearthmonth is a Monday, and the lamp was put there that afternoon. The water was off the causeway and the hand bell on the gate post is the size of a fist and was not rung. Four hundred people live on the far bank of that water and have never once been across in about nine years, and the four miles the man with the wain runs are four of the miles between that lamp and this market street, and four hundred people on a far bank have not heard him either.

Four days out, the ninth mile stone is the height of a knee and the ring somebody had cleaned round the foot of it is a foot wide and half an inch deep and had taken grit into it again. Four hundred yards past it the eleventh mile stone has nine hundred names cut into the side of it going back two hundred years, and nobody in nine counties can read more than nine of them.

The five frames of brood in the walled yard are under nine straw skirts and the board is still on the skirt stones at the gate, and the young man of nineteen has been in and out of that yard nine times with a honey pail and has not touched a frame and has not added a sixth.

Behind the ninth of the nine stones at the end of that nine-foot line in the grass there are nine hundred yards dead inside its own width and standing outside it, four hundred and nine days old, and nobody has ever been past the ninth of them.

He went out of that street at the eleventh hour, up the four hundred pace lane, and did not go up the made road past the ninth mile stone. The nine hurdles were still across the top of that cart track and the thumb of lime on the shoe of the top one had gone to powder in the wet. Five things on that board and three questions on it and the fourth line of the roll still empty on all three of its copies.

**And the cost of that Monday is a man of thirty-eight saying twenty-seven words out loud once in one street about four miles of made road and nine hundred people not hearing it and nobody arguing with him and his beginning to think that not having been heard is a kind of safety, a sheet eighteen days on a board and forty-five days left in it, three copies of a roll with nothing on any of them and the fourth line empty on all three, sixty-nine days of a bleed on the inside of one wrist that nobody in nine counties knows about, one thing gone quiet three hundred and sixty-one days ago and alive and shut and unnamed and unheld, nine hundred yards of grass four days out that will not come back, the use of one ear, the use of one leg, and a year, none of which is in any of it and none of which has a price.**
