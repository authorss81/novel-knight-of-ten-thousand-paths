# Chapter 0803

**Hearthmonth the twelfth, YR 323, a Saturday and a market morning, the room over the weigh-house open and the hearing not sitting, the ninth hour with four hundred-odd people in that street, sixteen days after a sheet went up on those boards beside three copies of a roll and not over them, and a question asked at the low end of that street about a trade, and then a second question about what to call the woman who answered it.**

The street was full by the ninth hour and the five things were on the boards at the low end of it, and the bolt had come off the room over the weigh-house at first light and the copyist of twenty-six was up those thirteen stairs.

Nine doors down the cloth was where it had been all winter, and the corner of it had lifted where the damp came up that wall and had not been put back.

Four days out, ninety-two drove have gone up that lane since the second of Mudmonth, ninety of them on the Thursdays before the twenty-sixth of Frostmonth and ninety-two by the tenth of Hearthmonth, which was the Thursday this week. Nobody in this market street has ever watched one go up. A drove is the only thing in this county that climbs a hill by a figure that nobody can check.

Eight of the nine yards of wall at the head of that made road are back in and the gap in it is nine feet long and four feet high, and one man has been carrying the stone two miles in his own hands for six hundred and forty days, and the ninth yard has not been started through a whole winter and a whole summer, and the mallet is on the bank two miles off with the line of stone.

The woman of twenty-nine had the second lamp at the foot of those stairs lit at the fourth hour and it went out at the sixth, and nothing about the four lines of chalk on the sill went in with it. The register held ten questions with no finding under any of them and had gained none in sixteen days. The day-book stood at eleven entries and had gained none in sixteen days. The fourth ruled head was empty. No fifth head was ruled, and the copyist of twenty-six had ruled no head and had been told about none of it. The press stood open with no date on it. At the back of the day-book a leaf carried nine figures and a date and the names of nobody.

The room over the weigh-house does not run the practice on a market morning, and so for sixteen days now there has been no fourth hour in that room at all, and the number last said out loud on that floor is four, and it was eight before the water, and it has not gone back.

On the sill of that weigh-house door, under the hood where the rain does not reach, there are four lines of chalk. They mean four. They have been dry and sharp there since the forty-ninth of Mudmonth, and about four people in nine counties have seen the thumb-drag across the end of the top line and read it for a moment as the first stroke of a letter, and nobody has read the four lines out loud to anybody, and the stick of chalk is in a press drawer upstairs and he has never asked her for it.

Then, at about the tenth hour, at the low end of that market street, outside the door of the office, the man of fifty-four who keeps that weigh-house floor asked a woman one question about her own trade.

He did it the way a person asks a question he has already thought the answer to. He asked her what the office did with the paper that came into it.

She was sixty-one or thereabouts and she had a hand on the door frame and she answered without hurrying, because she had been asked a question about her trade by somebody standing still.

“**I keep the weights and the ribbon and the county's one seal and I keep them in a drawer, and I have not weighed a thing in nine years.**”

Four people heard all of it and five did not. A man sorting wool by the third door said he had not known the county still had a seal, and she said it had, and he said what for, and she looked at him and did not answer that, and he went back to the wool.

He asked her then what to call her, because he wanted to know who to ask a second time, and she told him, and it was sixteen words, and four people heard it.

“**Nell Thrush, and I would like the paper this county is copying to be worth something.**”

He stood there a moment longer than he needed to. He had come down the four hundred pace lane at the ninth hour to see whether the four people who had said the sheet was the best offer anybody in this county had ever been made would say it a second time. One of them did, on the sixteenth day, in a different trade, to a different nine people, and nobody argued with that one either, and he had been standing at the boards waiting for the rest of it.

Nine days ago a woman of thirty-nine had told him she would not speak for the nine households she washed for. Seven days ago a wright had said the sheet was the best offer anybody here had ever been made and had not said it twice.

He had come here four and a half years ago to stop being asked where a figure came from. What he had instead learned is that a county answers a paper the only way it can, which is one person at a time, in a trade, to whoever happens to be standing there.

He thought about asking Nell Thrush for a thing she keeps in a drawer. He thought about it properly, standing at the boards with the leg going, and he understood that the reason he wanted to was that a seal would make the paper worth something, and that she had told him so herself, and that she had told him so because she is sixty-one and has kept a county's one seal in a drawer for nine years and has never once been asked what a seal is for.

Nobody in nine counties is going to ask her that this year.

He said thank you to her, and she told him not to, and he asked her the second question while he had her, which was whether the seal had ever been asked for since she took it.

“Once,” she said. “A man from the county office wrote to me about a bridge. That was the year before I had it. He wanted to know what the county's mark was so that he could put it on the bridge paper.”

“And what did you tell him?”

“I told him to come and take it off the wall and look at it.” She said it without heat. “He did not come. So I have had it nine years and been asked for it once by post and there was nothing on the bridge paper, and the bridge is still standing.”

“Nobody has asked you in person.”

“Nobody has asked me in person in nine years, and you have not asked me either, and I have been waiting to see whether you would.”

He had been about to leave it there. That was the thing he would have done on Friday.

“What is a seal for,” he said.

The woman of sixty-one looked at him for long enough that he understood he had crossed a line he had told himself on Friday he would not cross, and understood at the same time that the line had been crossed in the direction he had wanted it crossed, and that it had been crossed four and a half years late.

“It is for putting a mark on a thing so that the thing cannot afterwards be somebody else's,” she said. “That is all it has ever been for. It is not for authority. It is so that a person reading a paper can tell whose word it was.”

She went inside and shut the door of the low room behind her and did not lock it, and he stood on the step afterwards in the wet and understood that nobody in nine counties had asked her that until the tenth hour of the seventeenth of Hearthmonth, and that he had asked it and had not been thanked for it, and that on this page the asking is the finding and it is not the answer.

At the back of that office there is a low room with a door in front of it. It was shut behind its own door all morning and nobody went into it. Inside it, in a drawer eleven inches deep, the county's one seal is still lying with its ribbon round it and a set of standard weights beside it, and the drawer was not opened once.

At the front of that office there is a counter, and behind the counter there is a man who was born in that street. Nobody went in and nobody asked him what a gate is for.

The roll has been on those boards one hundred and sixty days. Its nine lines are empty on all three copies and the fourth of the nine is this county's. It is not filled in, and it is not torn up, and it has not been taken down. Folded inside the covering paper at the back of the second copy there is a sheet folded in four with one sentence on it in a hand that is not the hand of any clerk this county has ever had. It says that nobody in this county is paid to keep a road, and that a form a county cannot fill is not to be filled by writing somebody else's name on it, and that a county which will not govern ought to say so in writing to the people it is not governing. Nobody in nine counties has said who wrote it, and nobody is going to be asked, and nobody has offered to say.

The man of thirty-two came down the four hundred pace lane at the ninth hour and stood at those boards and read the sheet again and did not touch it, and he kept it to the eleventh hour. Forty-seven days left on it.

The rest of that Saturday went on without him. A barrow went over on its axle at the low end of the street and nine people stepped round it without stopping, and one of them righted it before the carter came back. The two women at the third door had words over a bolt of cloth for the better part of four minutes by the clock, and one of them said a price out loud that four people heard and five had not, and they came out with it wrapped between them. The inn at the high end was full to the eaves and its chalk board stood out on the made street from first light, and a carter from the coast asked for a bed and was told the price twice.

The light went off the west bank and found the upper edge of the boards, and the lamp at the high end of them was lit, because the ninth hour of a Saturday in Hearthmonth is dark and there is something off the hard slate of them to need it. Five of them went out into five trades and four stayed on at those boards.

At the causeway four days south the hand lamp goes on the coping every Monday afternoon in the winter, and the twelfth of Hearthmonth is a Saturday, and the lamp was not put there. The water was off the causeway and the hand bell on the gate post is the size of a fist and was not rung, and four hundred people live on the far bank of that water and have never once been across in about nine years, and nine hundred people along eleven miles of made road have not heard what is nailed to a frame at the other end of it.

Four days out, the ninth mile stone is the height of a knee and the ring somebody had cleaned round the foot of it on the Saturday before is a foot wide and half an inch deep and had taken grit into it again. Four hundred yards past it the eleventh mile stone has nine hundred names cut into the side of it going back two hundred years and nobody in nine counties can read more than nine of them.

Between that stone and the top of the hill there are two miles of wall and orchard, nine households owning a mile of stone on the high side and a mile of orchard on the low side, and nobody has asked any of them what they would put on a form with one line for a county.

The five frames of brood in the walled yard are under nine straw skirts and the board is still on the skirt stones at the gate, and the young man of nineteen has been in and out of that yard nine times this week with a honey pail and has not touched a frame and has not added a sixth.

He went out of that market street at the eleventh hour with the leg grinding and did not go up the made road past the ninth mile stone. The hurdles were still across the top of that cart track, and the thumb of lime on the shoe of the top one had gone to powder and gone to nothing in particular, which is what lime does. Five things on that board and forty-seven days left on the sheet, and a drawer eleven inches deep in a room nobody went into, and the seal still in it.

**And the cost of that Saturday is a sheet sixteen days on a board and forty-seven days left in it, four people saying out loud in four trades to nine people each that it is the best offer anybody in this county has ever been made and about five who have not worked it out and nine hundred along eleven miles of made road who have not heard it, a woman of sixty-one keeping the county's one seal and its ribbon and a set of standard weights in a drawer eleven inches deep in a room nobody went into and wanting the paper to be worth something and not being asked what a seal is for, three copies of a roll with nothing on any of them and the fourth line empty on all three, sixty-seven days of a bleed on the inside of one wrist that nobody in nine counties knows about, nine hundred yards of grass four days out that will not come back, the use of one ear, the use of one leg, and a year, none of which is in any of it and none of which has a price.**
