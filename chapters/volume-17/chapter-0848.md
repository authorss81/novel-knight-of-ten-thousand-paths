# Chapter 0848

**Rainmonth the twelfth, YR 324, a Thursday and a market morning, the room over the weigh-house open and the hearing not sitting and about four hundred people in that street, one hundred and ninety-six days after a sheet went up on those boards, one hundred and thirty-three days past the date at the foot of that sheet, and one question asked at the low end of that street about a trade and answered, and a name given that was not asked for.**

About four hundred people were in that street and the five things were on the frame at the low end of it and the bolt had come off the room over that weigh-house before the light. At the third stall from the boards a woman of about thirty-one was standing behind a board on two trestles with about nine letters on it in nine hands, and she reads other people's letters out loud there for nothing.

She had read about nine of them this year and about nine people hear all of one and about five do not, and she does it on a market morning and has done it for about four years. Nobody pays her and nobody has ever asked her whether she would do it again and about nine people in that street have worked out that those two facts are the same fact.

A man of about thirty-eight who runs a weekly wain between that causeway four days south and this market town, and who pays for the loading of other people's carts at the low end of that made road, asked her one question, kindly, in his own trade, at about the tenth hour. About nine people were near enough and about four of them heard all of it and about five heard the end of it.

“**I ask it because I carry what people write and I never know what is in it, and about nine letters have gone up this road this year and I have carried every one of them and I did not open one, and I wanted to know what a person gets out of reading other people's words out loud for nothing.**”

It was sixty-one words. She said them into the space in front of that board and about four people heard all of them and about five heard the end of them, and about four hundred people in that market street went on doing what they were doing.

Then the man of thirty-two with the stick asked her what to call her, because he wanted to know who to ask a second time, and she told him, and it was Prue Sillick.

“**It is Prue Sillick, and I am at the third stall from those boards on a market morning with nine letters on that board, and nobody pays me and nobody has asked me to read one again.**”

It was thirty-seven words. About nine people heard it and nobody repeated it and nobody wrote it down, and the name went into no document of any kind in this county on that morning and has gone into none since.

What she wanted was to be asked to read one again. About nine people in nine counties have not asked her to read anything twice, and about four of them have said out loud that not asking twice is the only respect anybody in this county has shown her, and about five have said that a thing nobody has asked for twice has not been refused twice either.

What she had begun to think about was that a person who reads for nothing is a person who has not been asked to say what is worth money. About four people in nine counties have worked that out from about nine minutes of listening at the low end of that street and about five have said that the thought is not a complaint and is not a claim and is not a thing anybody in this county is going to do anything about.

He did not ask her a second question and he has not, and about four people in that street worked that out inside about nine seconds and about five have gone on thinking about it since. There were about nine other questions available at that board that morning and he left every one of them where it was, and the reason was not kindness.

He had a reason for that, and it was that a second question would have made it a thing he was doing to a woman in front of about four hundred people instead of a thing he had done once, in her trade, kindly, with about nine people near enough. And he could not have said that out loud in a market street, and about four people in nine counties have said that the second question is the one that makes a person into a thing somebody did, and about five have said that a name is not that.

She is not in a room and she is not on a paper, she has not been given an office or a rule or a vote, and she has not said one word in front of about four hundred people except the two things printed above. Nobody in this county has asked her to read either of the two sheets that came up that made road in eight days, and about four people in nine counties have said that a person who reads other people's letters for nothing is the last person anybody should hand a second one to, and about five have said that she is the only person in this county anybody would think of first.

The roll has been on that frame three hundred and forty days with nine empty lines on each of three copies and the fourth of the nine this county's. Nothing has been written on any of those nine lines on any of those three copies and nothing has been taken off that frame, and no name in nine counties has gone against any road on any paper. The notice nailed above those three copies is four hundred and sixty-one days past its own date and has shut nothing.

The date at the foot of the sheet that was nailed up beside the roll and not over it went past one hundred and thirty-three days ago and nothing in nine counties is shut on it. The count of things on that frame is five and the count of questions on it is three, and the three questions have not agreed with one another since the twenty-sixth of Frostmonth, and a name given at a stall is not one of the five things and is not on that frame.

This county's copy of the hand paper has been on the flap of that stall for a hundred and sixty-one days with nine lines on it and a trade on each and no name on any and no seal on it. About four hundred places in nine counties are writing the same paper out by hand and not one of the four hundred has ever met another of them, and nobody in nine counties has been asked whose trade is on any of the nine lines of this county's copy.

A line with a trade on it can be argued with and a line with a name on it cannot. That is the finding, and it is four months old and it has not been acted on, and about four people in nine counties have said that a woman who reads other people's letters for nothing has about nine trades in front of her every market morning and about nine names not on any of them, and about five have said she has not been asked which of the nine is hers.

The low room at the back of that office was shut behind its own door on that Thursday and it has been shut behind its own door on every morning since the seventh of Longlight. It did not stand open on any market morning after the seventh of Longlight and nobody stands in that doorway. The drawer eleven inches deep in the wall of it was not opened once in a hundred and ninety-six days, and the county's one seal is in there with its ribbon round it and a set of standard weights beside it, and nobody in nine counties has asked what a seal is for.

Four days out, a hundred and eighteen drove have gone up that lane since the second of Mudmonth YR 322, and the hundred and eighteenth went up on Rainmonth the twelfth at about the sixth hour and stood in the field inside the stone wall and came back down at about the fifth hour. Two men went with it, and nobody in nine counties has ever watched one go up.

One man has been carrying that stone about two miles in his own hands for eight hundred and twenty days, and the gap in that wall is about nine feet long and about four feet high, and the ninth yard has not been started and does not start. The mallet is out on that bank with the line of stone and nobody has picked it up. The third course of sill at the culvert at this town's own gate is unlaid.

Nine hurdles are still set across the head of that cart track foot to foot, and there is a hurdle shoe on the top one with its thumb of lime gone to powder. The last cart of lime came up that track five hundred and thirty-nine days ago and the road behind the hurdles has been shut five hundred and twenty days, and nobody has been up that track since the second of Goatmonth, and no hurdle has been lifted and no bar has been tried.

At the causeway four days south the hand lamp goes on the coping at the low side every Monday afternoon in the winter, and the twelfth of Rainmonth is a Thursday, and the lamp was not put there. The water was off that causeway and the hand bell on the gate post above that coping is the size of a fist and was not rung, and about nine hundred yards of kerb has gone down on that far bank since the first of Mudmonth.

Behind the ninth of the nine stones there are about nine hundred yards of grass nine feet wide that has been dead inside its own width for five hundred and eighty-seven days. The dead grass stops at the ninth of them, and nobody has ever been past the ninth of them and the far side of them has never been shown to anybody, and that omission is a decision and not an oversight.

Under about nine straw skirts in that walled yard there are five frames of brood and no sixth has gone in, and the yard wall is about nine feet high and the gate about four. The young man of about nineteen has carried a pail in and out of that yard nine times this week and has not opened a skirt. About nine hundred yards of a standing broom on that east rise lies about nine feet wide and will not close over.

There are four lines of chalk on the stone sill of that weigh-house door and the hood over it is where the rain does not reach. They mean four and they have been dry and sharp there since the forty-ninth of Mudmonth, and about four hundred people went past that door that morning and about four of them looked down, and nobody has read them out loud in about four and a half years.

The second lamp at the foot of those thirteen stairs was not lit that morning, because the room it belongs to was open and it is lit on the five mornings a week the market does not come. The register holds ten questions with no finding under any of them and gained none in a hundred and ninety-six days, and the day-book stands at eleven entries and gained none, and the fourth ruled head of that page is empty and no fifth head is ruled anywhere in this county.

That press stands open and undated in the room over the weigh-house, and the four copied pages lie on its top shelf above the half leaf, which is face down. A leaf at the back of that day-book carries nine figures and a date and the names of nobody, and the name given at a market stall on that Thursday went into no leaf and no register and no day-book and no head.

The rest of that market morning went on the way a market morning goes on, and about four hundred people in that street went on buying string. A man of about forty-five set a trestle against the front of the second stall at about the tenth hour and put nine weights on it in a row and took them off again and put them back four inches along, and two women at the third door took a bolt of cloth apart in the doorway over about four minutes by the clock.

The light went off the west bank about as long as it takes to turn a cart twice over and found the upper edge of those boards, and the lamp at the high end of them was lit, because the ninth hour of a Thursday in Rainmonth is dark and there is wet slate on that frame to need it. Five of about nine people at the low end of that street went out into about five trades and about four stayed on at the boards.

A cloth lay over a bench nine doors down from those boards and stayed where it lay on the morning a name was given at a stall. The half leaf in that press is face down and was not turned over on the day of a naming. None of the three series in that room is worked out from either of the other two, and the band figure appears nowhere in this county.

The cost of that Thursday was the leg, which took about nine miles of a made road and about four hundred yards of a lane either side of it and went from an ache to a needle on the last mile, and the ear on the side the stick is on, which is gone and is the side she spoke from.

And there was, on the inside of one wrist, a bleed two hundred and forty-seven days old that had not faded by anything he had done to it, that nobody in nine counties has asked him about, that is in no document this county keeps, and that has never been said out loud in a room in front of one other person. A name given at a stall in a market street does not reach it, and about nine people in that street would know the name and about four hundred along eleven miles of made road would not.

Four lines on a sill are not a cure for it. Nor is a page in a woman's hand on the back of a day-book. Nor is the sentence in an unclaimed hand on the folded sheet inside the covering paper of the second copy of that roll. Nor is a page that has been authenticated, which is not consent. Nor is a place that is still held, which is not a victory, and a damaged engine is not a rescue. Nor is a bleed that has not faded in two hundred and forty-seven days.

He left that market street at about the eleventh hour with the leg grinding and did not go up the made road past the ninth mile stone and did not go in at the low end of that street. Five things were on that board and one hundred and thirty-three days past the date and the fourth line of the roll empty on all three of its copies, and nobody was thanked for any of it.

**And the cost of these six weeks is a woman of about thirty-one who reads about nine other people's letters out loud at a stall on a market morning for nothing and who wanted to be asked to read one again and who has begun to think that a person who reads for nothing is a person who has not been asked to say what is worth money, and who was asked one question about her trade and answered in fifty-nine words and gave her name in thirty-one and has been given no office and no rule and no vote and no sentence in front of about four hundred people. The drawer eleven inches deep not opened once in a hundred and ninety-six days and the county's one seal and its ribbon and a set of standard weights still in it and nobody asked what a seal is for. Nine trades on nine lines of a paper nobody can seal and no names on any of them. Two hundred and forty-seven days of a bleed on the inside of one wrist that has not faded by anything anybody has done to it and that nobody in nine counties knows about, one thing gone quiet five hundred and thirty-nine days ago and alive and shut and unnamed and unheld, about nine hundred yards of grass four days out that will not come back, the use of one ear, the use of one leg, and a year, none of which is in any of it and none of which has a price.**
