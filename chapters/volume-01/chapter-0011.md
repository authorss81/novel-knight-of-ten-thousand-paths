# Chapter 0011

The first of Greenmonth was the second day he had been on his feet and out of the room, and the town had decided in the meantime that it did not need him.

That was the shape of the first hour. He came down the stair from the cooper's rooms with his notebook in his coat and the water still standing in the boot of the left one, and the street was a working street doing what a street does on a day that is not a market day: the foundry's boy going along the channel with a barrow, the eel-man's boy scraping a board, two women at the cooper's door with a cart and a disagreement. The river had come down four fingers in four days and the wharf road was carrying a cart, so the town had stopped looking at the Sable entirely. The Old Toll crossing, the empty cradle, the Crown's paper nailed to the post — none of it was a thing a town looks at on a Tuesday in Greenmonth. It was a thing a town looks at on a market morning, and there was no market until the fourth.

Aren Kest put his hand in his coat and counted what he had without taking it out, because he had done it enough times in eleven years to do it through cloth.

A mark and a quarter in the bag, in three pieces and a great many small coins. Eleven days of his own wage, which the trust owed him and would owe him until there was money in the trust, and there was no money in the trust because of nine days in Mudmonth and a column in an account book that a magistrate had read out in a room over a salt merchant. A bond for six marks against a tool-chest the guild's clerk had priced at three and a half, and a clerk's remark that was going to live in his head for the rest of the year: *a man who signs six and has three and a half is a man who has signed on faith, and faith is not a currency we take.*

And a bell under a grey wax seal in a storehouse by the fish weir that he had put six marks of his own body on the paper for and could not get within a yard of.

What he wanted, on the first of Greenmonth, at the eighth hour, in the cold, with the water going down four fingers a day, was a job he could be paid for.

He got as far as Weir Street before anybody offered him one, which he considered about right.

Tovan's yard was open and the door of the workshop was off its top hinge and propped with a churn, which is how a bridge carpenter's yard says *come in if you have nothing better to do*. Inside there were four men because there were always four men, and three of them were on a job and the fourth was sharpening a chisel and would be for another hour. Oak shavings stood about four inches deep in the middle of the floor where the bench with the shave horse had been dragged out and put back eleven times since Mudmonth.

"You look like a man who has been told to enjoy himself," Tovan said.

"I've been told I look it."

"You look worse. Sit down before you go over. There's no market and the trust's got nothing, so if you've come for the scour hole you can take it up with the trustees, because I did the scour hole and I did it at a loss and I would do it again and I am not doing it twice."

"I know."

"Then what."

"Pin," said Aren.

Tovan put the chisel down.

"Pin."

"There's a hull on the Sedge slip with a shear pin gone. Nine feet of water in her garboard at the turn and she's been riding on the frame since Thawmonth, and a hull-hand in Merefen puts a new pin in an afternoon and charges four shillings for the iron and keeps the fitting. The man who owns the hull wants it by Thursday and does not know there is a difference between a pin and a rod and has been telling three people in Merefen that a bridge man could do it cheaper." He shrugged. "It's a day's work and it's got my name in the fitting and it pays a shilling and the iron, and that is a job, and I would like to do it today."

Tovan looked at him for a while with his arms folded, which is what a brother does when he is deciding whether to say a thing.

"You've been up two days," he said.

"I've been up two days."

"You were on a board under a blanket four days ago. I carried the water."

"I know what I was four days ago." Aren set the notebook down on the bench, which was the oldest gesture between them and had never once required words. "How are you?"

"I'm four days behind on my own pay and I have four marks in the chest that are the crew's money by Friday, and I'm not asking you for them, and you are not to ask me for them, and if anybody in this town asks you where a road warden gets four marks you are to tell them the truth, which is that he does not, and you are to say it in that order and not the other way about."

"I wasn't going to."

"You were thinking about it on the road up the bank and you stopped thinking about it about forty steps back, which I can see because you do a thing with your jaw." Tovan picked the chisel up again and turned it over. "There's a factor coming back this week. He's been twice. He's got eleven tons of setts standing in the mud two miles up the western channel and a quarter of his year's margin in the water with them, and he is the only man in this trade at present who would rather be spoken to honestly than be promised a date, which is a thing I say about him in front of my crew and it is nearly true."

"He was in the account column."

"He was in the account column and the column has no figure against his line, which is the truest thing about that column." Tovan tested the edge of the chisel on his thumb and was satisfied and went back to the work. "Go and do your pin. If you're going to be a man who can be paid, be one on a Tuesday, and we'll see what the fourth does."

That was the whole of it. There was no other conversation, and neither of them had expected one, and Aren went down Weir Street with a job in his hands and the strange lightness of a man who has been given a day's work by somebody who owes him money and has chosen to pay him in that instead.

---

Sedge Reach Bridge had four timber bays and was holding, and would go on holding until somebody put oak in it, which was the entire of its situation and the entire of his.

The scour hole under the nose of the third pier was the size of a washtub. He had measured it on the sixth of Mudmonth and it had been the size of a bread loaf then, and Tovan had put two oak cribs in it at a loss and the thing had held through a flood, which meant the repair was good and the cause was still there. A hole under a pier is not a hole. It is a symptom, and the thing that made it is four feet to the north and eleven weeks ahead, and the fix for the fix was eleven tons of stone and a fortnight with the road shut and a crew of four paid by somebody.

He stood on the deck and looked down into it for a while, because looking was free.

The work waiting for him on this bridge was not available to him, and it was worth being exact about why, because a man who says *nobody will pay me* is a complaint and a man who can say *here is the sum that is missing and here is who has it* is doing arithmetic. The trust's money had gone into nine days of shut road, thirty-one stalls of pitch, a barge held off two days, and a market held late and thin. What was left in the trust on the first of Greenmonth was enough to pay a man for cutting a shear pin and not enough to pay a man for putting his arm in a scour hole to a spring tide. That was not cruelty and it was not politics. It was a town that had lost three weeks of takings in a fortnight and had done the arithmetic correctly, and the arithmetic said the scour hole waited for Longlight, when the river dropped and the stone came off the western bank in summer prices.

He wrote it in the notebook. *Third pier, nose, hole at a washtub, was a loaf on the sixth. Sound crib work, cause eleven weeks out. No money for stone till Longlight. Mine to do when there's money, which isn't my decision.*

Then he went and did the one thing on that bridge that was free, which was the bench mark.

There is a mark that has been in the Sedge Reach abutment since before anybody now living was born. It is a small cut face, no bigger than a thumbnail, cut into the third course of stone at the height a man in a brown coat decided on one afternoon in a year nobody in Merefen can name, and every level taken on that bridge for two generations has been taken off it. Road wardens do not make them. Guilds make them, because a guild that keeps a bench mark keeps a datum, and a datum is the one number a town cannot argue about for twenty years.

Aren had found on the morning of the first that the mark had fresh lime in it. Somebody in the guild had cut and re-cut it in Mudmonth, which is a thing a bench does after a flood, and the re-cutting had put it a quarter of an inch low, which is nothing to the eye and everything to a level, and it meant that every height anybody in Merefen had taken off that mark since the storm was a quarter of an inch wrong, in the direction that makes a deck look better than it is.

He got the level out of the guild's chest — which was in the guild's own hut, under a tarpaulin, with three other instruments and a mouse nest in the case — and he took a level off the mark twice, and read it, and wrote it in the notebook, and then took it back to the guild's hut and put the chest where he had found it and made a mark on the lid with chalk.

That was a free afternoon's work of the best kind there is: nobody wanted it, nobody had asked, and if he had not done it the next twenty years of levels in this town would have been a quarter of an inch optimistic and every man who took one would have blamed the river.

He was putting the chalk back in his pocket when the guild's own man came along the deck, a heavy slow man with a ledger under his arm who had the settled expression of somebody who has come out to stop a thing happening.

"That mark is the guild's," the man said.

"I know whose it is. I took a level off it and put the chest back."

"You put a level off it."

"Twice. It's a quarter of an inch low. Somebody re-cut it in Mudmonth and cut it by the hand, not by the instrument." Aren held the notebook so the man could see the figure, which was a mistake, because a guild's man does not want a road warden's figures. "There's a date and my mark on the lid of the chest. That's how it's done."

The man looked at the lid, and at the figure, and at the mark.

"The bench sits on the ninth," he said. "I can put it in front of the bench if a road warden puts a paper in against the bench's own mark, and a road warden doesn't put papers in against the bench."

"I didn't put a paper in."

"You've told me it's low."

"I've told you what a level says." Aren closed the notebook. "You can put the paper in or you can leave it out, and either way the mark's where it is, and I'm the only man in this town with a chest that reads that fine, and in Longlight I'll be the only man in this town with a chest that reads that fine, and this afternoon I've made sure of it for nothing, which is how it ought to be done and how it isn't."

The guild's man stood in the middle of his own deck for a moment with the expression of a man being beaten in an argument he had agreed to have.

"Right," he said.

"Right."

He went down the steps. And that was the second thing on the first of Greenmonth that Aren Kest had done well and for nothing, and there were four or five more of them between the second hour and the dark, and he would have taken all of them if anybody had asked.

The factor came at the eleventh hour and found him on the wharf road with a rule and a chalk, waiting for a carter to come out of the mud with an axle he had promised for Thursday.

"Kest."

"Factor."

"I have been told you are the reason I am not moving."

"I am the man who isn't holding the road that your stone is standing behind. Those are two different sentences and only one of them is mine." He put the rule away. "Your setts are eleven tons on a bank two miles up the western channel, and the bank is going out from under the south end at the rate of a foot a fortnight, and if you wait for the ninth the south end goes and you lose the face of the bottom course, which is a third of the value and all of the trouble of getting it out."

"Which is what my second visit was about."

"Your second visit was about the deck. The deck is open to light loads and a cart with stone in it is not a light load, and I have said so in writing to the trust and been told that the trust has no money, which is at least honest of the trust." He looked at the factor properly, because a man who has stone in a field is a man who wants a date and will pay for a true one. "There is a way. Not from me. Hire the boat. Hire Wenna Koll's second hull and get two trips in a day off the bank's shoulder while the shoulder is still a bank, and the stone comes down in eleven days instead of never. It costs you a boat instead of a bridge and there is no road warden in the trade who can beat me on that because I am not offering."

"You are not offering."

"No."

The factor took his hat off and looked up the channel, where the water was four fingers down and falling and the wharf road was solid enough for anything with a wheel on it.

"You have been the worst of the road wardens to me for a fortnight," he said, "and you are the first one who has told me the true price of my own mistake. I will remember which of those two things you were." He put his hat on. "I will come back on the ninth, after your market, and I will ask you for a date, and if you have one I will take it and I will not come back a fifth time."

"Ninth is a market day."

"Ninth is a market day, and a market day is the only day in the month when a man with stone and a man with a bridge are in the same square, and I am not a fool." He went off up the wharf road, and stopped, and came back, and said: "My name is not in your account. I checked the column. There is a line with my loss on it and no name, and I want to say that I have looked at that column and I know what it is."

"It is the trust's account."

"It is the trust's account and there is no name on my line, and I have decided to let it stand, and I would like you to know that I decided it and did not forget to."

Then he was gone, and Aren stood on the wharf road in the cold with a rule in his hand and thought that there was no version of this town in which he was a man nobody trusted, and that he had been living in one anyway because the version he was in had a bell in it.

The market square is not a square. It is the space in front of the toll-house where the market assembles on the fourth and the ninth, and where the rest of the month it is a set of cobbles with a notice board on three sides of it.

He went past it at the second hour and stopped, because there was writing on the board that had not been there on the thirtieth of Mudmonth.

The toll-house clerk chalks the day's notices on the board every morning, and every clerk in every town does it the same way, in a small hand, with a rule between each line, and the rule is what makes it official. Under the notices for the first there was a line in a different hand. Not large. Not shouted at. Somebody had used the same size of chalk and had not ruled above it, which is how you tell a thing that has been put there on purpose from a thing that has been put there because there was time.

*Bell taken by the crown on the 19th, storehouse by the weir. Nobody asked. Ask Kest, he was there.*

Twenty-nine words, in a hand that had no rule over it, on a board that a clerk wipes every morning of his life.

Aren Kest stood in front of the market board for a quarter of a minute and then walked up the Old Toll road, because there was only one thing in Merefen he could do about it and it was to be nowhere near it.

The glasswright's slip was nailed inside a hut on the Old Toll road, in a frame of four split sticks, under a hood of oiled leather that somebody had put there to keep the rain out of a thing nobody in Merefen was ever going to buy.

*Two discs, Steppe glass, cut and annealed to a bridge pin. Four marks the pair, ready in a fortnight. Money before cutting.*

He read it four times, which is twice more than a man reads a price he has no intention of paying.

The Old Toll crossing was a quarter of a mile further up the road, and he walked it, and stopped at the abutment, and did the thing he had been not doing for two days.

The cradle was still empty. The iron was rusted to the stone by eleven years of weather and eleven of nothing, and under it the stone was swept clean, and the moss on the third course had begun, in the eight days since he was last on his back on that stone, to be the green it is supposed to be. A clean rectangle, nine inches by five, paler than the stone round it. And on the post, a sheet under a hood with a disc of grey wax on it the size of a crown piece, and a hand laid flat on a book.

He did not touch the seal. He had not touched it on the thirtieth and he was not going to touch it now, and the reason was not respect. The reason was that the moment a man's hand is on a seal, everything after that is a story about the man who put it there, and there is no version of that in which a fen town is served.

Below the cradle, three feet under his boots, was the sill.

He got down on it. Cut stone, laid by a man who knew the trade, the third and fourth lengths from the abutment repacked by him in the autumn and again in the week before the storm, three courses of setts and a lime bed, chalked with his own mark in the bed of it where the flood would keep it.

And in the middle of it, where the packing had been lifted and set back down out of line, a cross. Two lines crossing, the size of two hands, pale in the middle of the stone and dry, and the dryness of it was the thing that stopped him. The whole bridge was wet. The rain was coming off the arch above in a sheet and the parapet was streaming and the moss on the third course was wet to the touch, and the inside of that cross was dry. It had been dry since the twenty-seventh, which meant that whatever had struck the stone from the other side had left a place in it that rain had not got into.

He put his hand flat on it. He did it deliberately, and he waited, with his whole attention on the stone, for a long time — long enough that a cart went past on the wharf road and a woman called something to her dog and the cart went out of earshot. On the twenty-seventh he had put his hand on this stone with a fever on him and the place under his palm had been warm, and there had been a voice in the place behind his eyes that had said one true thing, and then he had come back. He had been waiting ever since for some sign that the arrangement went both ways.

Nothing.

Not a warmth, not a sound, not a fold in the air, not a seam. Stone under a hand, and rain, and a man of thirty-two on a bridge at the third hour of a Tuesday with his roadmark in his palm and nothing at all on the other side of it. He said the sentence aloud, once, to nobody, in the trade's words he had used in a magistrate's room a matter of two days ago, because a man who can read a thing out loud is not the same as a man who has stopped believing in it: *a roadmark is not a road.*

He had understood, in a filing room over a salt merchant, that a roadmark is not a road. He had not understood until that afternoon, on his knees in the rain, that a road can also be entirely real and have nothing on it.

He wrote in the notebook, in the wind, standing up:

*Old Toll sill, near abutment. The cross is a working and not a stain. Dry inside while the whole bridge is wet, so it is not a fill and not a wash. It is a scar, and it is mine, and the sill will take one more and not a third.*

Then he looked at the two lines and thought about what it would take to be allowed to do it again, which was glass, and glass was four marks, and there was no roadwarden in Merefen who could mend stone with glass, and there was no office in Merefen that would pay a roadwarden four marks for mending a stone they had already written down as not built.

The glasswright's lean-to was a hundred yards further up the Old Toll road, where the road leaves the river and starts the long climb to the Steppe, and it was a stone-and-timber shed with the whole front open to the weather and a furnace at the back that was not lit, because it was the first of Greenmonth and the last person in the world who wanted a thing cut was a road warden with a mark and a quarter.

The man inside was sixty-eight and about five foot four and was sitting on a stool with his hands on his knees, and he did not get up, and he looked at Aren Kest the way a glasswright looks at a man who has come into a shed with a wet coat on.

"Quarren," Aren said, because a man's name in Merefen is a form of payment.

"Master Quarren, if it's going on a page." He did not offer the stool. "It's on the post."

"Your slip's on the post. I want to know what a disc of glass is for, and I'll pay for the answer if the answer's worth money, and it will be, because I've got no money and I've asked."

Quarren's eyebrows went up about a sixteenth of an inch, which on him was an event.

"Two Steppe discs, cut and annealed to a bridge pin. Four marks the pair, ready in a fortnight, money before cutting. That's the trade's answer, and it's the right one, and the reason for it is a year."

"A year."

"A year. The glass on the Steppe comes out of the ground as rough, and rough glass is full of strain, and strain is a thing that has to be let out at a rate the maker does not choose. A man who cuts a disc and cools it in three days has made an axe head, and an axe head is very useful until the first cold morning, when it lets go and takes the hand off. It takes a year." He got up then, because he had got up to show him something, and he went to a rack against the back wall and took down a disc in a cloth and did not open the cloth. "That's one. That one's been in this rack since the first of Frostmonth and it will keep until the Longlight, and it is a year old before it was ever mine, and I have never had a use for it, and there is one other like it."

"Two marks?"

"Four the pair, and that is not me being clever, it is what a disc is. I cut it, I dress the pin, I fit the seat, and I stand behind the fit for as long as the stone stands, and a man who cannot pay me for that is a man I will be seeing in a court." He put the disc back without opening the cloth. "And before you say the obvious thing, which is that you have about a mark, I have read the trust's account. It is not the account of a town that is going to buy glass in Greenmonth."

"No," said Aren.

"Then you've come to be told the price, and you've been told the price, and it has not changed while you were standing in my shed, and it will not change, and I am not a man who enjoys that."

"I know."

"Then say your question, Kest, because you've had two and the first one was a courtesy."

Aren Kest stood in the open front of a glasswright's shed on a road going up out of a fen, with a mark and a quarter in a bag and a fever's worth of weakness in his legs, and asked the question that would matter later.

"Can it take a working?"

Quarren turned round and looked at him properly for the first time.

"Glass takes a working the way a bridge takes a cart," he said. "That is the whole answer and I am not going to dress it up for you. Put your promise into the glass and the glass will carry it, better than iron and worse than nothing, because glass has no grain and iron has a direction. Set one on your sill and it will hold whatever you set it in, for as long as the setting is honest, and I will cut you two that will do it for four marks."

"And when it fails?"

"When it fails it will fail the way glass fails, and glass does not fail the way a thing fails by breaking. It lets go along a line nobody drew, in a direction nobody chose, on a morning with no weather in it at all, and it lets go all at once and it is a thing that was holding when it let go." He set the cloth back on the rack. "That is why I will not sell it to a man who cannot tell me who is going to be standing on the stone when it happens. Four marks is not the price of the glass, Kest. Four marks is the price of not being the man who sold it. Ask me anything else."

"What do you want?"

That stopped him. He had the cloth square on the rack and his hands still on it and the look of a man who has been asked a question in the wrong register and has decided to answer it in the right one.

"I want this," he said. "I want the Steppe road to be a road and not a queue. Up there they cut rough in the dark, in winter, in holes, and it comes down to four workshops and a counting-house in the provincial town, and every year the count sets the price, and every year I get my glass in the spring and my money at the year's end, if I live, and I am sixty-eight and I have two journeymen and no sons and a house with a bad roof. And I have watched, for thirty years, men come down off the Steppe with more glass than they can sell and no other trade, and every one of them sold it to somebody who could not pay for it, because when you have glass and no money the only buyer in the world is a fool with a use for it." He let go of the cloth. "I would like to be able to stop. I would like to be sixty-eight in a town where the glass is a trade and not a favour, and I would like somebody to come and say to me, in ten years, that there is a bridge out there somewhere with my name on the seat of it and the maker's mark under it, and that the man who set it is alive. That is the whole of what I want and it is not in your hands and I would not take it if it were."

"Master Quarren," said Aren, "I cannot promise you that, and you know that I cannot promise you that, and I have just watched you refuse to sell to a man who cannot tell you who will be standing on the stone."

"I know exactly what I have done," Quarren said, "and I have done it eleven times, and there is no pleasure in it. Your post is nailed on the hut. The discs are in the rack. When you have four marks, bring them and I will not charge you to wait, and I will cut them to your seat, and I will ask you the question again and you will not have an answer, and we will both go on as we are."

---

The tower at Merefen stands on the Sedge Reach bank because the crossing wanted watching and the crossing has been there for two hundred years. Its light is a mirror on a pivot and it is worked by a boy of fifteen who lives in the top room and whose wages are two shillings a month and the right to sleep in the room.

Aren Kest did not go to the tower. A woman in the salt loft did, or rather a woman in the salt loft sent, and the result of it came down the Old Toll road on the back of a carter's boy at the seventh hour, while Aren Kest was standing on an abutment with a dry cross under his hand and no answer in it.

The boy had a note, and the note was four words long and it was not signed, and it said:

*Kest. Come to the loft.*

Underneath, in a different ink, in a clerk's small hand, as though it had been added afterwards by somebody who wanted it on the paper: *A man has been in the outer room three days running. He will not go home, and he has asked for you by name, and he says it is about the crossing.*

It was about the crossing. There was only one crossing anybody in Merefen meant.

Aren folded the note into the notebook and went up the bank towards the town, and the water went down the Old Toll channel under a bridge with a bell-shaped hole in it, and the town told itself the story of the bell on the way he went, and he had a day's work in his hands and a fee for a shear pin and a mark and a quarter in a bag and a sill with a cross in it that would not answer him, and eleven days of wages that would come, and a bond for six that would not, and a fortnight's wait he had already signed up for in a shed at the top of the Steppe road, and a night of work ahead of him that would be paid, and a name on a chalk board in the market square that he had not put there and could not take off.

The fever had cost eleven marks and four pence and it was still being invoiced, and the next item on the account was going to be his.
