# Chapter 0047

The arch was carrying a market.

That is the whole of what the far side of that city was doing at the fourth hour of the first of Fallowmonth, and it is the reason there were about two thousand people on the crown of it, and the number two thousand is a count of feet on a deck and nothing else at all: it is in the bench-clerk's book that night under a heading he invented for it, which was *on the day*, and he invented it because he had four hundred years of a bench's book and a column for nothing else.

The fair came to the arch twice a year and the first of Fallowmonth was one of them, and the toll-man on the north steps had a rope and a book and a farthing and had been taking the toll since the fourth hour, and the market under the second arch had about ninety stalls and had spilled onto the crown, which is what a market does when a market that has four days to run is told by a bench that the deck is open.

Nobody had told anybody to open the deck. The deck is always open. That is the difference between a market and a thing that has been closed, and it is the whole of what the oath had been worth for four generations.

Aren Kest was on a staging under the second pier at the fourth hour of the first of Fallowmonth with a lamp on a cord round his wrist, because he had gone down to have one more look at the scour at the low water and had told a bench-clerk on the thirtieth that he would, and the bench-clerk had written it in the book with the hour.

There were four men in a boat under him. One of them was a hull-hand of the Hushwater cooperative, a man of about fifty who had been on that water since he was nine and who had said two sentences to Aren Kest in nineteen days and both of them had been about a tar bucket. The other three were there because there was a market on the arch and a hull-hand takes a boat under a bridge on a market day for the coins, and it is not stupidity, it is the trade, and every man in the boat knew exactly where the pier was and exactly how deep it was and exactly what the scour hole under it was worth, because it was their river.

The other of the four men in that boat was a stranger.

He was thirty-one or thirty-five and nobody in the city knew where he was from and he had been at the fourth camp for a fortnight, cutting setts, and he had arrived on a boat out of the upper valley with a bag and a sett-cutter's callused hands, and eleven people had fed him in eleven days and the camp had taken him in, and in a city of four thousand people that had been put out of the world eleven years ago by an office two hundred miles away, a man who turns up and is useful and can cut setts is a man the city asks no questions about, and that is not a moral defect. It is what eleven years does to a place.

He had a name. The bench-clerk entered it that night and it is in the book, and it is in the fair hand, and it is a name and not a figure, and the book that holds it is the only book in this world that holds it, and the point of the whole of the last three days is that there is one.

---

The first thing that happened was not a sound.

Aren Kest had been in the water at that pier nine times in six months and twenty days and he had got used to the way a river reports itself through a stone, and at about the fourth hour and a quarter, with ninety stalls on the crown of an arch and two thousand feet on the deck, the stone of the second pier came up about half an inch, and set down, and came up again, and the second time it came up it did not set down.

It is a scour hole. He had told a bench that in a hall on the twenty-ninth in the trade's terms and in the fewest words he could find, and he had told them to put a date on it and a man on it and go back to the water in the spring, and it was not the spring, and the bench had renewed an oath about the crossing in front of two hundred people on the evening of the twenty-ninth, and there had been five days for somebody to close the arch and nobody had closed it because the oath said the crossing would be held.

**That is the whole of how it failed. Not the river. A promise made five days ago by nine men who meant it.**

He was on the staging, which is oak, four feet wide, and the arch was above him and the market was on the arch, and the water came up under the arch through the scour hole in a sheet about two inches deep and it was up to his knees in a second and a half.

The hull-hand of the cooperative said one word. The stranger said nothing at all, which Aren Kest noticed about four years later and which is the single thing about that night he would take back if he could have taken anything back.

And Aren Kest did the old move.

---

He did it in about two seconds and he watched himself do it, and the whole of what is left of the man he was in Mudmonth of this year is in the fact that he watched it and did not stop in the two seconds.

He got his left hand on the bar and his right on the staging stanchion and he put a working into the span.

A Bind is not a charm. That is the thing nobody in the north believes and everybody in this trade knows, and the two seconds in which he did it are not two seconds of light and are not two seconds of anything anybody in two hundred yards could have seen. What a Bind is, in the trade, is that a man who knows a load path can put his intent into a material and the material will take it. A man puts a promise into a sill and the sill carries it. A man puts a promise into a staging and the staging holds. It has no more in it than that and no less, and it is why four generations of men in this hall have sworn things into stone and why the things have held, and it is not a joke, and it is not a mystery, and it is why the guild is called a guild and not a society.

He put it into the span because he could see the load path.

And he could see it because he was the best man in the room, and the room was a bridge.

The arch was carrying a market. The scour hole had opened under the river side of the second pier and had taken about four and a half tons of stone out of the shelf in fourteen days, and the pier's foot was standing on a shelf with two notches in it the width of a knee, and the arch wanted to spread, and the spread went into the one place it had always gone, which was the foot of the second pier, and Aren Kest could see that the whole of the arch was about to put about thirty tons into a bed of river gravel that had four feet in it.

So he put his working into the span and held the spread in the stone where it was, the way you hold a thing by putting your shoulder behind it, and he did it in the trade's way, with his hand on the bar and his feet in the water and a word in his mouth that is not a word and is a place.

The force went into the pier exactly as it should.

That is the sentence. It is the sentence of this chapter and it is the sentence of the last eleven years of this man's life, and there is not one word in it that is wrong.

A Bind puts the load where the load path says it goes. That is what it is for. That is the only reason it works. And the load path of that arch at that hour went into the second pier, and the second pier was standing on a shelf of gravel with two notches in it, and the second pier was the shortest distance between the river and the load, and the second pier took it, because that is the load path, and a man who can see the load path can see exactly how much of it there is.

The stone cracked.

It did not crack the way a stone cracks when it is tired, which is a long slow thing you can hear. It cracked the way a thing cracks when it is given a job it was never built for, all at once, in one line, and the line went from the waterline of the second pier up through the ring and out through the face of the spandrel about nine feet above the arch, and the sound of it came down off the deck and into the water and under Aren Kest's boots, and the arch moved.

Two thousand feet came off the crown of the arch in about a second and a half, and the market under the second arch took the shock of it, and the toll-man's rope went across the north steps and took a man down, and the hull-hand of the Hushwater cooperative was struck by nine feet of coursed masonry at about the fourth rib and went into the water on his back and did not come up for about four seconds.

The stranger was standing at the boat's bow with a pole in both hands and the pole went, and the boat went over, and he came down under the arch foot at the south end and the timber of the boat came across his back and his legs, and he was under the arch with the water coming up through the scour hole in a sheet that was now four inches deep and rising.

Aren Kest got him out. That is in the record and it is the only thing anybody in this book is ever going to be allowed to say about him on that night: he went into a hole in the bed of a river under a loaded arch with his own working still in the stone and pulled a man out of it, and it took him eleven seconds, and the eleven seconds are the eleven seconds the river crews' saying allows for a whole crossing and he spent them underwater with a stranger.

The hull-hand came up on his own, at the ninth second, and swam for the staging, and got a hand on it, and the stranger got out on his own at about the fortieth second and lay on a staging for a while and then asked, in a completely ordinary voice, whether the arch was all right.

Then Aren Kest stopped.

He had about four minutes in which the load was still in the span, because the arch was standing and the pier had taken nine feet of crack and was not going to take a great deal more, and in those four minutes there was nobody in that hole with him but a man of fifty with a cracked rib and a stranger with a hole in his back.

Nobody on the crown of the arch had a bar. That is the thing he has to live with and he did know it at the time and he did it anyway and that is the whole of the man. There were nine men of the bench on that arch and there were about forty men of the fourth camp on it and there were two hundred other men and women standing on it, and not one of them had a bar, and not one of them had ever in his life looked at a load path, and there was nobody within four minutes' walk who did.

And the man who could see the load path had about four minutes and had put his own working into the span in the second second of the accident, and the whole of the arithmetic of this volume is in that sentence.

He took it out.

That is what took eleven seconds and it is the only part of the night he has never been able to describe to anybody including himself, and what he wrote in the notebook on the third day, in four lines, is:

*I took it out. In the second minute, with a cracked rib on my left and a stranger on my right, and the load came off the span and went back down the arch and into the second pier on its own, because the second pier is the load path and there is no arguing with the load path, and I did not take it out because it was going to fail. I took it out because it was mine.*

*And it went into the pier. And the pier had already got nine feet of crack in it from me.*

He did not argue. He did not stand on a staging in a hole in a river and explain to a hull-hand and a stranger what he was doing and why, and he did not apologise, because apologising to two men in a boat in the dark is a thing a man does with words and words are worth nothing in a hole in a river at the fourth hour with the tide making.

He climbed the staging and got out on the third pier and went into the hall at about the fifth hour, and the hall is over the arch, and there were about ninety people in it because the whole of the crown of the arch was inside the guild hall and the shutters were up and the light was in the gallery.

And he said the four sentences.

"That arch is closed. Both ends. Now, and by me, and I am not the bench and I have no vote and I am telling you and you will do it because there is a hole in the bed of the river and I am the only man in this city who has put a bar in one this year.

"The second pier is gone from the river side. It is not cracked. It is nine feet of crack in a line and it is standing on a shelf of gravel and it will not carry the deck and it will not carry a man and it will not carry a boat. The repair is a cutwater and the cutwater is nine months and there is a hole in the bed of the river and the bed is at the lowest water of the year and I am telling you that the second pier has to be looked at in the spring by two men with a pole and not by anybody tonight.

"There is a man of the Hushwater cooperative in a boat at the second pier with a broken rib and there is a man from the upper valley with a hole in his back who has been cutting setts at our camp for a fortnight and whose name is in your book because I made the clerk enter it, and he has no people in this city and no papers in any country and if you do not put him somewhere tonight then he is a man nobody has ever met lying on a bench in your hall, and I would like both of them carried up to the abbey and I would like it entered in the minute.

"And the last one, and it is the one I am going to have to say in front of two hundred people, and I am saying it now because I have earned the right to be listened to for about nine more minutes and after that nobody in this room has to listen to me again as long as they live. The oath was renewed on the twenty-ninth in this hall by nine men who meant it, and the oath said the crossing would be held, and I did not tell one of you on the twenty-ninth that a promise about a stone is not a promise anybody can keep, and I said a great many other things in this room on the twenty-ninth and I said one true thing and it was the wrong true thing. The oath is not what failed. The oath is four lines about who answers. It is the only good thing this city has. And I stood in a hall with two hundred people in it and I said *do not put a number on it tonight, put a date on it and a man on it* and it was the best sentence I have said in six months and it was a sentence to a bench about a ring, and tonight the ring was eight inches and the arch came down, and a bench of nine men swore on Monday that they would hold a crossing and a man from the north let them, and the whole of what happened after that is mine."

Nobody in that hall said anything for the length of a slow count of nine.

Then Alis Merrow stood up at the near side of the table, with his coat on and a bundle of string in his hand, and said: "The page has gone south."

Nobody understood him.

"The page has gone south," he said again, "on the line, at the fourth hour, with a woman on it, through a hole in a bridge in a county four hundred miles off, on a road that was opened by a carpenter and a ferrywright and a witness at each end, and it is gone, and it was made this morning, and it is the first page of that register that has left this city in eleven years, and the arch came down an hour after it left, and I would like the bench to note the order of those two facts and I would like nobody in this hall to make anything of it yet."

The man with the mallet put his hand flat on the stone in the middle of the floor, which is the crown of the third arch, and said: "Close it. Both ends. And get the boats out from under the second pier, all of them, and I want the hall shut and the shutters up and no man on the crown until somebody who is not me has looked at that pier."

"I have looked at it," said Aren Kest.

"You have looked at a hole in a bed of gravel under water at the fourth hour in the dark and you have been in this city six months and twenty days and you are a stranger in this hall," said the man with the mallet, "and this morning the arch came down while you were in it, and I am not going to be the man who says anything about whose working it was, because I do not know, and because if I did know I would not say it in this hall in front of two hundred people in a city with one crossing, and because I have a bench to sit in an hour with a hole in the river and I would like to be able to sit in it. Out. Both of you. And the woman on the boat with the rib, and the man with the hole in his back, and I will have the four of you back in this hall at the ninth hour and I will put it in the minute and I will not be told a thing by any of you tonight."

Nobody in this book is going to tell you that the work that followed was done by one man with a bar.

The pier of the great arch is eleven paces long at the top and about four at the water, and it has a scour hole under the river side of it about four feet across and eleven feet of the footing gone, and on the night of the first of Fallowmonth, at about the sixth hour, with the tide at the bottom of the year's range and the water still going out, there were nineteen men in and under that arch, and the work took until the eleventh hour of the next day, and here is who did it and in what order, and it is in the minute of the bench of the twenty-ninth of Longlight and it is the only page in the whole of this business that anybody reads twice.

The older members of the bench did the first thing and did it without being asked, which is that they took the pier apart from the top. A bench of nine has a man of fifty-eight who cannot lift a sett above his shoulder in a heat, and the two oldest men in it are fifty-eight and fifty-one, and they went up onto the crown of the arch at the sixth hour with bars and took forty courses of the spandrel down off the second pier by hand, because the only way to look at a footing is to take the weight off it, and that is nine months of work if you do it with a crane and it is a day and a half if you do it with forty men and a great deal of swearing.

Alis Merrow sent his counting house's three clerks and the wagon and the oil and the wedges and the elm, and he sent them with an entry in his own book, and the entry is four lines and the fourth line is *at the cost of this office, and this office is a creditor's office and the crossing is not ours and the cost is ours, and no toll of this arch has moved in eleven years and none of it will move today.*

The Hushwater cooperative brought the two boats and four hull-hands and a woman of about fifty with a thumb wet with ink, and she would not let anybody touch the crossing book, and she did not, and she was on the boats for nine hours and afterwards she wrote the day's crossings in it in a living hand, and the last line of that page is a line about a man being carried up to the abbey with a hole in his back and about a hull-hand being carried up to the abbey with a broken rib, and the two names are in it, and it is a crossing book and not a record of an accident, and that is on purpose, and it is the only document in this whole business that anybody in it kept for the right reason.

Captain Ruen came down the river at the seventh hour with a crew he did not have to have, and put them on the arches to take the footings out in a barge at the low water, and his daughter of fifteen was on the bank and was not allowed on the arch and stood there for nine hours anyway.

And Bellweather's own two stewards were the last two people to arrive and the only two people in that city who had a document between them, and they arrived at about the ninth hour of the morning with a sheet of paper done in string and a bench minute that a clerk had written out in a fair hand twice, and the two of them were a ferrywright from a hamlet on a drowned channel and a bridgewright of about sixty who had been on that bench for thirty-one years and had never once put his name to anything a stranger had asked for.

Sera Quen and the man with the mallet. The bench-clerk's question of the thirtieth — *who is the steward of this crossing* — was answered at the ninth hour of the first of Fallowmonth by two people putting their names at the bottom of a sheet, and the man with the mallet's name went in at the bottom and Sera Quen's went in beside it and not under it, and neither of them signed the other one's line, and the clerk entered both hours and read them back.

And the thing that came south on the road at the fourth hour of that morning was the first page of a register to leave the city in eleven years, and it was out of the city before the arch came down, and nobody in that hall ever worked out whether that had been the worst thing that had ever happened to them or the best, and Alis Merrow is still alive and still has the bundle of string and has never been asked.

The narrower oath was made up on the arch between the fifth and the ninth hours of the next day, in a room made of the fourth camp's drying floors with the doors up, with about two hundred people outside in the cold because a city that has lost one thing in eleven years and had a page carried out of it in the same hour cannot go inside.

It is four lines long and it is not four lines long. That is the whole of what happened in that room and it is the entire difference between this bench and every bench that has ever sworn an oath, and it took nine people and a clerk and two stewards and a woman with a bench order and about six hours, and the clerk's minute of it is the longest entry in the roll and it runs to nine sides.

It is four lines long, and the four lines are these:

*That the crossing at Alder Vale shall be marked twice a year at the two low waters, and the mark read back and entered;*

*and that where the mark of a ring, a pier, a cutwater or a footing has moved, or where the bed of the river under any part of the crossing has moved, the bench shall say so in a hall, with the number, and the hour, and the name of the man who read it, before the bench does anything else about it;*

*and that the bench shall not hold a promise about a stone, and shall be asked for no promise about a stone, and every person in this city who has been asked for such a promise in the last eleven years is released from it;*

*and that the stewarding of this crossing is held by a named person at each end of the road, and that when those two people cannot both be there, the crossing does not open.*

That is the narrower oath. It is not a promise that the arch will hold. It is a promise about a book, and a promise about who answers, and a promise about a door.

It was argued over for four hours and two lines of it are the ferrywright's and one of those two is the fourth one, and the man with the mallet said, in the middle of it, the sentence that the whole of Volume 01 turns on and that he said to a room of two hundred people on a drying floor with a hole in the river under it:

"We have spent four generations swearing to hold a stone. We have never once sworn to say out loud what we found. And there is no bench in this kingdom that has ever been asked for the second thing, because the first thing sounds like a bench and the second thing sounds like an office, and I am forty years too old to be told which of those two a town is.

"And a stranger from the north stood in this city six months and twenty days and he never once offered us a way to hold the arch. He offered us a way to find out, and we would not have it, and then we swore an oath about a stone in a hall, and the stone came down, and he was under it, and the only two things this city has that are worth anything are a bench order with a date on it and a woman who writes in a book. We have had those for four generations. We had them last night. We used the second one and we threw away the first one, and the reason we threw it away is that it had a number in it that frightened us."

The repair was stood in the open on the second day. Not a model. Not a drawing. The footings came out of the water in sections on a barge with a crane made of two baulks and a windlass and eleven men, in front of the arch, in daylight, in the cold, and about two hundred people stood on the bank and watched forty tons of stone come out of a river bed and go up on a wagon in the open where anybody in that city could walk up and look at it and put a hand on it.

It is called the fourth camp's standing stone in that city now, and there is a girl of about nineteen who shows it to strangers and recites the two hundred and eleven without being asked, and she is the girl from the north steps who sat on the third step with a basket of onions between her boots on a stranger's first morning in her city, and she is the great-granddaughter of the woman who keeps the Hushwater crossing book, and she still thinks the toll is a farthing too high and has said so to a Crown's man.

The road opened again at the fourth hour of the second of Fallowmonth, in the rain, and it was not the narrow road and it was not a schedule.

Four sets of hands on the anchor, and Aren Kest translating, which is the work he is actually for, and which is the only thing he did on that night that was any use to anybody.

The fever is three days and it is the same three days, and it began on the night of the first and it broke on the fifth at about the sixth hour, and there is not a fourth day of it anywhere in this book.

What it cost that the first two fevers did not cost is on the page of a notebook in a coat and it is four lines, and it is the only thing in this batch that he has never said out loud to anybody, and there is a page in a press over a salt merchant in which it is entered as a statement of a man and not an observation, because that is what she asked for and because he gave it.

*The verse. I have got the shape of it and about four words in the right order and the tune goes up and there is a place where it comes down. I had one hole in it from Mudmonth and I have found a second one and it is a bigger one and it is not a missing note, it is a place where two notes have been and now there is one note and it is the higher one, and the higher one is the one my mother used to end on, and I have had it in my head for nine months and I did not know it was there until about the fourth hour of the second of Fallowmonth in a room above a cooper in a fen county with a fever on me.*

*And the palm. It is not a scar any more and it is not a mark and it is a road, and I can feel which way a thing in this county wants to go, and I could feel it in that arch at the sixth hour with a man of fifty's rib broken under my right arm, and I have never been able to feel that about a road in this county before in my life, and there is no one I can tell.*

*Stage 0, and then not, and by qualification and not by award, and I did not get it by being right and I got it by being the only man in a hole in a river who could see the load path and putting it in a stone that could not take it and taking it out again in the second minute before two men died.*

*It is better than it was. That is the part I cannot get round and it took me until the third day to admit it, and I am writing it down here where it is only me: it is better than it was, and what I have lost is not the power. What I have lost is the belief that I am the one it should be spent on.*

*And the drift, which is a warning that was wrong and not a surprise, and which nobody in this county can check and everybody in this county has now had.*

*The road crews' saying in my country is three days out and three days back, and it is four generations of men and it is not a rule, and a man who has not three to give should not begin. The Gatehouse's own figure for a held road — I have been given it twice, and never in writing, and both times by a man who was wrong about something else and right about this — is three weeks.*

*I was out six days in this world's calendar, from the fourth hour of the twenty-sixth of Longlight to the sixth hour of this morning. Inside, it was six months and twenty days. Those are both true and the second one is not knowable from this side, and that is not anybody's fault and it is the whole of what the word drift means and I am writing it down because a man who keeps a figure like that in his head for four months is a man who will be asked about it in a room.*

*And the warning was three weeks and it was out by three weeks, and the three weeks is on this side and not on mine. Here is where it is: a bridge in a city four hundred miles off was shut on a Wednesday night in the middle of the month and the word of it came down Weir Street on a board on the twelfth of Fallowmonth and could not say which night, and the man who put it there could not have known there was a road, and he was right not to pretend he did, and there were nine of them who asked me about it, and I have not been in this county to answer one of them.*

*That is what a drift is. It is not a surprise and it is not a warning that failed. It is six days of a county and six months of a city and one Wednesday night that three hundred people in a fen were told about on a board and could not date. Next time I will write the figure down on the day I go, in a book, with the hour, and I will give it to somebody who is not me, and I do not know yet who that is and I have known that since the nineteenth of Mudmonth and I have not done it once.*

He came up out of the sill of the Old Toll crossing at about the quarter to six in the morning with a cold river and a wet road and a smell of the fen, and got as far as the third setting and sat down on it with his back to the parapet and stayed there for an hour and a half, and could not get up, and did not try again for about forty minutes.

The cradle was four feet away with nothing in it. The post was grey. The seal was where it had been and the wax had not run.

And the fourth course was open.

Not cracked. Open — a split the width of a hand that ran the whole depth of the course and up through the string course and out at the parapet, and the stone in the bed of the joint was crushed to gravel, and the two used discs that had been in that seat since the eighth of Greenmonth were not in it and not in the county: the seat had been used a third time at the fourth hour of the afternoon of the twenty-sixth of Longlight, the pin had gone, the bed of lime had been crushed to gravel, and a wagoner had prised the two discs out on the twenty-seventh and carted them away and said they were broken, and Tovan Kest had believed him because they were broken, and a bar of 3½ ounces of glass that had been used twice was not going to be used a third time, and the man who had dressed them had written a page about exactly that and given it away and would not charge for it.

And the block that Aren Kest had cut out of the parapet himself with a chisel and a drill in the rain in Greenmonth — four inches square, two feet deep, two used discs in it, a pin, an oak collar with three blows in the head of the pin — had moved about a quarter of an inch, and it had moved it in a way that stone does not move, which is that the two faces of the split were in register, which is not what a settling stone does and is exactly what a thing that has been pushed apart and put back does.

He got the notebook out with his left hand, which took a while, and wrote the hour and the state of the seat, and then he wrote the second line, which was not about the seat.

*Second of Fallowmonth, YR 312, sixth hour. Cold, no wind, felt it in the back before I came up. Four seconds and a half. Cannot stand.*

*And the stone in this county is warm. It is the second of Fallowmonth and it is a quarter to six in the morning and the dew has not come off, and the fourth course of the Old Toll sill is warm, and it has been cold every time I have put a hand on it since the eighth of Greenmonth and twice before that it was warm, and the first time it was warm was the nineteenth of Mudmonth at a quarter to seven in the morning and the second time it was warm was the sixteenth of Greenmonth at a quarter to seven in the morning.*

*And I know what the bench is going to do about this. The bench is four hundred yards up this road and the bench has a man with a rule in it and the man with a rule has never in his life compared two numbers, and he is going to take a reading off the mark at Sedge Reach in the morning because it is the second of Fallowmonth and the season, and he is going to write it in a book, and it will be a different number by about a quarter of an inch, and in a year somebody is going to build to it.*

*There is a mark in a fen county that says a Crown marks a stone in the north twice a year. It has been on a board in this town since Fallowmonth. And there is a bench four hundred miles off that marks a ring twice a year at two low waters. And they are the same description, and I have been in the room with both books in the same month, and the difference between them is a quarter of an inch of weather and a rule held by two men and a clerk, and I could not have told anybody in this county that in Longlight and I could not have told anybody in that city in the first week either, and I have told neither.*

*And a page went out of that city this morning with a name on it, and the arch came down an hour later, and this stone came open on the same night, and there are three documents in this county about a bell and a bench minute voted on a Saturday and a report with five limits in it, and there is a pair of books four hundred miles apart that are the same book, and the only person on this earth who has read both of them is sitting on a bridge at a quarter to six in the morning with a fever on him and a bar across his knees.*

*And I have told two people the same sentence in the same words this week and one of them wrote it down in a fair hand and one of them wrote a fact about a line in a rule, and neither of them is going to be able to defend me in October, and I have not asked either of them to.*

He was still sitting there when the cart came up the road at the second hour, and he heard the wheels on the setts before he saw it.

Tovan Kest got down and came the last eight yards on foot.

"Left," he said. "Not the right."

"I cannot stand."

"You are not going to." He got an arm under him. "I have got a book in the yard with a page in it, and I have read it four times since two o'clock this morning, and I am not going to ask you a single question about where you have been, and I am going to tell you now, in daylight, with the book in my hand, what is in it, because you are not going to be able to read it for three days and I am not going to be the man who holds it."

He opened it on the parapet and put it flat on the stone so that Aren Kest could see it upside down, which he could barely, and which was the correct way round anyway.

*Second of Fallowmonth, YR 312, third hour, with a lamp. The stone moved under the foot and the deck came up about a quarter of an inch and set down on the other side of itself, and the moving and the setting down were not one movement but two, and there was about a second and a half between them, and nothing fell.*

And under it, in the same hand, and Tovan Kest's voice reading it out loud on a bridge in the rain in Fallowmonth, and nobody in Merefen within four hundred yards hearing a word of it:

*And on the fourth course, at about the fourth hour, with two men and a lamp, the two pieces of glass that were in the seat are not in the seat. The seat is open the width of a hand the whole depth of the course and out through the parapet, and the faces are in register, and I have put a straightedge across it and there is nothing to throw.*

"I have written nothing else," said Tovan Kest, "and I am not going to write anything else, and there is no Crown's mark in my book and there is no paper in my book and there is one road in it and that road is yours, and I wrote it in Greenmonth because you did not ask me to and I have thought about it every day since."

Then he put his hand flat on the open book, on the page, the way a man does with a thing he has kept, and said the last thing, and it is the reason Tovan Kest has never once in nineteen years stopped being a carpenter and it is the reason the yard is worth having.

"And I do not know what any of that means and I am not going to pretend to anybody in this town that I do, and if a Crown's man comes up this street in a year and asks me what is in this book I have got a date and an hour and a fact and a description, and I have got a page with a road in it that I put in there because my brother came down Weir Street and told me where he was going and did not ask me for anything, and that is the whole of what I have got and I would like it noticed that it is more than this town has about anything."

He got him as far as the cart. It took a quarter of an hour and neither of them said another word for any of it, which is the correct amount, and Tovan Kest did not ask him a single question about a city four hundred miles off or about a page or about a woman on a line or about a bench that swore an oath about a stone five days before the stone came down.

And at the top of Weir Street a boy said a thing about the market board, and a woman said a thing about the bench, and the bench was going to sit in eleven days and every man in it was going to be asked to do the same thing twice, and the sixth line on the Market Square board, which had not been wiped for a fortnight, said *Ask Kest. He knows what the oath is. He has not been here since the twenty-sixth*, and nobody wiped it, and a bench in a guild house four hundred yards from a bell-shaped hole that had moss in it was about to find out what a paired anchor is.
