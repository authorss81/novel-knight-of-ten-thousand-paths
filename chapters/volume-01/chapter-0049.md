# Chapter 0049

The fever went the way the road crews' saying says it goes. Three days, the second to the fourth of Fallowmonth, over on the fifth at about the sixth hour, and on the ninth he was on his feet and out in the street by the eleventh, thin as a wiped slate, with a left palm that would not do what a palm is for and a right hand that had been useless for a day and a half and then was not.

He went and looked at the sill first, because that is what a man does.

The fourth course was open the width of a hand. It had been packed and set and it was open now, and it was open in a way that could be read, and a man who has spent four months learning to read stone could read this one standing up in about a minute and a half.

"A scar is not a crack," he said, out loud, to nobody, on a bridge, at the eleventh hour of the ninth of Fallowmonth, with his hands in his pockets. "A crack is the stone telling you it cannot go on. **A scar is a place a thing came through and can come through again.**"

He got out the notebook and wrote it in the flat hand with the hour on it, because the second half of the sentence was the useful half and it was going to be a very long time before anybody else in this county said it.

*A scar is a place a thing came through and can come through again. Two is easy. Three is not a number a glasswright will sell. This stone has been opened by a thing that came through it and it will open again, and the only question in this county about it is the ordinary one, which is what do you do with a road that has a scar in it, and the ordinary answer is you mend it and you keep it and you tell the men who come after you where it is.*

He put his left hand flat on it. It was cold, and there was nothing in it, and he had known there would be nothing in it, and he had come and put his hand on it anyway, which was the fourth day of a thing he had decided was a habit and not a trade, and he wrote that down too, in the second line, because a man who only writes down the figures that are wrong is doing a kind of arithmetic that gets him into a court.

And then he said the thing out loud on a bridge in a fen county, alone, in daylight, and it is the whole of what happened in that year and it took nine seconds:

"I know what it is. I know why it opens when it opens. And there is nobody in this kingdom I could say it to, and I have known that since the nineteenth of Mudmonth, and I have gone on being the best reader of this stone in this county for five months without a single one of them being able to help me, and that is not a mystery any more. That is a trade. It is the only trade I have. It is a man who can see a load path, and everything I can do in this county is built on it, and the price of it is that I can never tell anybody why I can do it, and the day I could tell anybody it would stop being a trade and start being a thing I own."

---

He was in the market on the tenth at about the eighth hour, which is where a man in Merefen goes on a Tuesday, and he found out what had happened to him while he was in bed, which is the ordinary condition of a road warden in a fen town, and it took four minutes.

It had not gone well and it had not gone badly and the reason was a bench.

A bench of nine had voted, on the second of Fallowmonth, while he was under a blanket in a room over a cooper, on the second of two visits a year to the Old Toll crossing, at a specification of two leaves in the guild's man with the levels' own hand, whose eighth line says that a seat so cut will be used and will fail and that the number of uses is not stated because it is not a thing this bench may know, and the guild's man with the levels had put his name on the specification and not the warden's, and the bench-clerk had entered the whole of it with the hour, and the reeve had entered that a bench of nine is being asked to do a thing twice, and the vote was carried against three marks and about six with two marks in the chest, and both of those entries were in the roll, and the roll is a thing a clerk wipes every morning and anybody can read.

So the town did not have a road warden who rang a bell. The town had a man whose brother had come down Weir Street on the fourth of Fallowmonth and been watched by four men and a woman with a sheet, and a bench had put a block into a parapet at its own cost, and nobody at all had asked the road warden.

"That is what a legal loss is for," he said to himself, in a lane, with a basket. "Not to be proved wrong. To be proved wrong by somebody else and to have the somebody else's handwriting on it."

The Market Square board had been wiped that morning for the first time in three weeks. The clerk had gone over the top four lines and left the bottom four alone, and the bottom four said what they had said in Fallowmonth, and the fourth of them — *Ask Kest. He knows what the oath is. He has not been here since the twenty-sixth* — had been wiped and re-chalked by a different hand, in a rounder hand, and under it somebody had put a fifth line, and the fifth line was the one that told him the town had changed its mind about him without ever once changing its opinion about him:

*Road warden was here. He did not say.*

---

Captain Rell came to him on the twelfth, in daylight, in a lane, and did not ask him to come up the street, and the reason he gave was that he had been up the street enough and a road warden who has been in bed for a week is entitled to be met halfway.

"I have to tell you three things," said Captain Rell. "They are the last three I am going to tell you in a lane and I am not going to pretend it is anything else. It is three, and they are in a room in a building two hundred miles off, and none of them is about a bell.

"One. A writ of enquiry into a record office was stamped on the ninth of Rainmonth, returnable the same day, and it has been answered, and the answer is in the spaces and lists nine hundred holdings and one lending dated the twenty-second of Longlight. I read the endorsement. I was not meant to and I have not got it in a file of my own and I am telling you the date and not the contents because I have decided that I have spent eleven years filling in forms and the one thing I am going to do before I go is leave one fact out of a conversation with a man in a lane.

"Two. There is a second writ of the same kind, on the reeve's office, and a third went down the county post on Thursday, and I do not know what is on the third, and my sergeant has stopped asking me what is on things and has started telling me what he has not been able to find out, and that is a change in a man of forty-four and I do not have a word for it.

"Three. The instruction of the ninth of Longlight is in force. It has not been superseded. It names no law and it requires no court and there is nothing in this county I can do about it and nothing in this county I can be asked about it, and on the second of Fallowmonth I stood outside a door in a guild house for eleven minutes while a bench of nine men met about a bridge, and I entered the hour, and the bench entered the hour, and we did not agree about anything, and the two of those entries are in two books about four hundred yards apart and they are the only two documents in this county that say the same thing on the same day."

"And you are still doing the correct thing."

"I am still doing the correct thing," said Captain Rell, "and I have discovered this month that the correct thing is a thing you can be *picked* for, and there is a notice nailed to my own wall about it, and I would like you to know that I have read it every morning since and I have not once been able to make myself stop reading it, and that is the whole of my private life at the moment and I would like it noted by a man who is a road warden and has a right to know things."

---

The four Crown's jobs came back on the sixteenth of Fallowmonth, because it was Fallowmonth and the county always came back in Fallowmonth, and the bridge season in this county starts in Fallowmonth and the county's surveyor had a schedule of forty-one structures and a shortage of men and a book with a ruled column on it.

The man in the provincial coat came down Weir Street at the seventh hour with a leather bag and four forms in it, and he read the heading of the column out loud in the gateway in front of four men, the way he had read it on the ninth, and he said he could not change it, and he said it in the tone of a man who has said it twice.

Aren Kest said no to it in about four seconds.

He said no to the schedule. The schedule of the season's inspections, forty-one structures, a penny a mile and a shilling a day and the county's cart, taken last year by a man who signed his name in a column headed *Keeper* forty-one times.

He took the post. The copying post at three shillings a week in the county survey office, initialling Crown returns, which he had refused flat in Longlight and had said out loud in a gateway that he would refuse again in a year and had then bought two marks and three quarters of answer in.

The reason was the same reason as the year before, and it was not the same reason, and he wrote it out in the yard's book and in the guild's roll and in a copy to the county surveyor, and dated it, and it is four lines and it is the single most useful thing he did in the year:

*That on the sixteenth of Fallowmonth, YR 312, the road warden of this county refused the county's schedule of inspections and took the copying post, and the reason is the same reason as in Longlight and it is not the same reason.*

*In Longlight: a schedule is a record and a post is an office, and an office has a name on it and a man in an office can be sent for and can be held to, and a schedule with a column headed* keeper *on every line is forty-one documents that say a man is an officer of something.*

*And now: a schedule is forty-one more lines in a book two hundred miles off with his name in a column, in the fourth month of the year in which his name is in a claim's schedule of holdings on a separate sheet with a separate number, and a keeper of that office has told him in a filing room that the separate number is the only reason he is not a party, and he has taken that on the word of a woman who has never once been wrong about that office and he has not verified it and he cannot, because the thing it is about is in a building two hundred miles away and the woman is right and he is not able to check her.*

*So: he refuses the column and takes the office. He is not refusing a Crown's money out of principle. He is a man with a road-marked palm and a sister in a claim in a county file and no office at all, and a man with no office is a man nothing can be asked of, and he has been nothing that could be asked of for eleven years, and there is a form in this county with a column for exactly that.

*And the difference between this year and Longlight, and it is the whole of the difference: he has taken one Crown job and not two. In Longlight he took one and refused two. In Fallowmonth he takes one and refuses one, and refuses the one that has a column, and takes the one that has a wage, and the reason he has not refused both is written in the yard's book and it is that a man who takes no Crown money at all in a county of this size in the fourth month of a year in which the Crown has put a writ on a record office is a man who is being given no work in order to be visible, and being visible is a thing that works in one direction only.*

"A post is a cage," said the man in the provincial coat, in the gateway, "and I would like you to understand that I am telling you that as a man who has watched six men take that post in nine years and four of them stopped coming in and one of them was a good clerk and one of them was a fool."

"Then I shall be the fifth," said Aren Kest, "and I shall be the one who comes in, and the reason I shall be the one who comes in is that there is a page in a book in a filing room over a salt merchant with my name in it as a thing that was set, and I would like there to be a second page somewhere with my name in it as a thing that was done every Tuesday, and I would like the two of them to be able to be read together by a hostile man in a room in about eleven years, and a man who is visible in two places is a man who is legible and legibility cuts both ways and it is worth the trouble anyway."

---

The bench had to be asked to do it twice.

That came on the twenty-first, at the ninth hour, in the long room over the wharf, and it was not an argument; it was the reeve reading four lines out of a book and a man in a chair saying that the thing could not be done and cannot be got round.

The Old Toll crossing's maintenance is three marks and about six the campaign, twice a year, seven marks and no farthing. What goes in the book is four marks and six a year.

The bench had carried the second visit on the second of Fallowmonth against three marks and about six with two marks in the chest, and it had carried it because a bench that stops carrying a thing in the middle of the year has stopped being a bench and started being a place where things were once done, and the yard has about a fortnight and a half left in it and it is spoken for, and a factor's stone contract is finished and a county town's yard wall is going slow and will be finished about a week before the water is at the low of the year, and the fourth pier's iron shoes are in a dead man's cart behind the mill.

"So we are asking a bench to spend seven marks," said the reeve, "out of two shillings, in the fourth month of a year, in a town that has lost its wharfage and its market and its tolls, and I have costed the loss of the wharfage and the market at four or five marks a year and a market day and I am not going to say it a fourth time in this room. I record only that the same bench has been asked twice and has voted twice and the second time it did not have the money and the first time it did not know the question."

"And the question is now known," said the man in the chair, "because there is a hole in a bridge in this town. Not a crack in a pier four hundred miles off. A hole in a bridge in this town, with the deck open on both ends and the closure taken on the second of Fallowmonth at about the fifth hour by a stranger who is not the bench, and about four hundred people saw it, and there is no money to mend it and there is a bench of nine that has been keeping four hundred yards of the oldest bridge in the county at four marks and six a year out of its own chest for four months."

"And that is the second time," said the reeve, "and the honest sentence is that the second time is more honest than the first, and that a bench that keeps asking to be asked is a bench that has understood something and I would like somebody to enter that it is the first time I have thought that of this room."

The resolution was carried and it is the shortest in the roll: *That the bench will keep the Old Toll crossing in repair as it has kept it, at the cost of this bench, out of its own materials and its own men's time, and the bench has asked for it a second time and has not been able to name a date on which the money comes, and the minute is to say that the bench asked twice and was refused once by a chest and is going to ask again in the spring, and that the asking in the spring is not to be treated as a new matter.*

---

He told one person.

He went up the stair at the seventh hour on the twenty-second of Fallowmonth, two flights, and a landing with a chalked joke on it, and the joke was older than the one in Longlight and had three names in it now, and he read it going up and did not say anything about it, and did not notice that he did not notice, which is a kind of progress and is the only kind there is.

Mara Vey had the claim on the table and a second keeper at the end of it and a sheet headed *the cost of a crossing, as known to this office*, which she had never re-opened since the sixteenth of Greenmonth and which she opened now herself, in front of him, and put the date on it, which is the first time in four years she has put a date on an old sheet.

"Read it back," she said.

He read it back.

Then he put his left hand flat on the table, palm up, in a filing room over a salt merchant, in front of two people, which he had not done in front of anybody in eleven years, and he said:

"I have crossed three times. I am not going to tell you why and I could not tell you if I tried, and there is nobody in this kingdom I could tell and I have looked, and looking took me two days and I did it in a lane like a man looking for a job.

"And here is what I have instead of a reason. On the second of Fallowmonth I came up out of that sill at a quarter to six in the morning and I put my hand on it and it was warm, and it has been warm three times in five months and it has never once been warm in Mudmonth, and I know the month it is in and I know the hour. I could not tell you what I know. I can tell you that I know it."

She did not ask him anything. She wrote, in the fair hand, on the sheet, with the hour, and she wrote what he said and not one word more, and then she read it back and made him listen to it, and then she wrote four lines of her own under it and made him listen to those as well.

"Entered at the seventh hour of the twenty-second of Fallowmonth, YR 312, on the word of the road warden of this county, in this room, with a second keeper present, and it is his statement and not my observation, and it is entered under the second rule and not the first, because I have not seen it and I cannot check it.

*That the said road warden states that he has crossed three times; that he does not state why; that he states that he could not state why if asked; and that he states that on the second of Fallowmonth, YR 312, at about the sixth hour, the stone of the fourth course of the Old Toll sill was warm to the hand, and that it has been warm three times in five months.*

*And that this office has not verified the said warmth, has no instrument for verifying it, has no jurisdiction, and is a keeper of records and not a bridge office; and that the said statement is entered because a thing a man will not say in a room is worth nothing to him and a thing he says in a room and cannot support is worth more than a thing he does not say, because it can be looked for; and that the day anybody looks for it and finds it is a day this office will be proved to have been right to have written down.*

*And that this office is not going to ask him again, and that if he wants to tell her anything else he will come up the stair, and that she will not ask, and that the not asking is entered so that in eleven years nobody is able to say that she extracted it out of a man who was ill.*

*And that this office is going to ask him one thing, in a room, on the record, and that she is asking it now, and the thing is: if it costs you something every time, and it costs more the better you get at it, then who pays it, and is the answer still you?"

He thought about it for a while.

"Yes," he said. "And it is going to keep being me for about a year, and then it is going to be a line and two hands and a date, and I am not going to be in it, and the day it is a line and two hands and a date I am going to be the man who translates and I am going to be a tradesman at a rate of pay on a contract like anybody else and I am going to be worth four times as much then as I am now, and I have written that down as an argument and I would like you to make me keep it."

---

He did not tell anybody else, and there is a line in a notebook in a coat on the twenty-fourth of Fallowmonth that says so and that says why, and it is one of the four lines in that book that he wrote for somebody else to read in about eleven years:

*I have told one person and I have been asked one question and I have answered it and I have not slept properly since and I am going to be honest here because it is after the eleventh hour and there is nobody to be brave for. I did not sleep properly because she wrote it down. In eleven years a woman with a press is going to read that page and it is going to be the only evidence in the world of a thing I can do, and she wrote it down inside a week of finding out that I had hidden a letter from her for four days, and she did it anyway, and she asked me one question and not two, and the question was the right one, and I have not got an answer that satisfies me and she knows that and she wrote that down as well.*

*I am not going to tell anybody else and the reason is not trust. It is that a stage is a thing you qualify for and there is no room in this county in which to qualify for anything, and a man who tells six people in a market that he can cross will be a man with a reputation for it inside a month and a keeper of nothing by the spring, and by the spring I want to be a man who is on a contract at a yard rate and can be sent for and can be held to and has a page in three books and no name in a column.*

*And the other reason, which is the real one, and it is that it would be a speech. Everything I have ever been given for it is a speech. I have had four of them in eleven years and three of them were from men in rooms and the fourth was eleven hundred words in a letter and it was the kindest one and it cost a valley. I am not going to be the fifth. If this is ever to be anything other than a thing I can do, then somebody else is going to have to hold it, and a man cannot arrange that by telling people he is good at it.*

---

On the twenty-ninth of Fallowmonth, at the fourth hour, at the lowest water of the year on a bridge in a fen county, a ferrywright from a hamlet on a channel in another world put a slate on the parapet of the Old Toll abutment and turned it round, and there were four lines on it in chalk in a hand that had been cutting iron and setting it down for twenty years, and the two men and the two witnesses who were on the bridge that day were the whole of the reading of it, and one of them wrote it out in a fair hand in Merefen that evening, and the other wrote it out in a book on a north bank four hundred miles off, and the two copies do not agree on the wording and both of them are correct, because a slate is a thing a woman chalks and a woman chalks what she means.

*Road, held: one.*

*Carryover: one. It is a hand's habit and it is not a working and I do not know what it is and I am not going to pretend I do.*

*Return: made before every crossing, by two hands and a date, and a witness at each end who is not the man who keeps it, or it does not open. It does not fail. It does not open.*

*Answer: the far side decides. You cannot make a city answer. That is the term and it is not a defect and I would like the man in the north to stop looking at it as though it were.*

He read it three times and did not say anything about it, and got the notebook out, and wrote the hour and the state of the seat and the third line, which was about a woman on a ferry.

*Twenty-ninth of Fallowmonth, YR 312, fourth hour, lowest water. Read four lines off a slate in a hand I know and did not improve them and did not ask about the fourth one because I have known for six months and twenty days what the fourth one is and I have written it down in a letter to a man with a seal and I will not write it down in a notebook that is not a record.*

*And the last line of it, which is the one I am going to have to be able to live inside: **I am not the mechanism.** Two ends and a date and a person at each end, and everything in the middle is a way of holding the two ends to their promise, and the man in the middle is a tradesman, and the road was opened in this county on the first of Fallowmonth by a carpenter and a bench and a woman with a sheet, and it came out of a stone in a parapet in a fen and it did not come out of me, and there were two thousand people on a bridge on the other side and none of them knew I was under it and none of them needed to.*

Then the woman who keeps that road wrote it in the book on the north bank, in a living hand, with the hour, and it is the third entry under a date that had been blank for eleven years and it is the first one in that book that has a place and an hour and the name of a person who is not a steward on it:

*Twenty-ninth of Fallowmonth, at the fourth hour, at the lowest water. The road is to be opened on a named day and not on a date agreed between two men who both mean well. The day is to be written at the top of this page and in the book at the other end, and a witness at each end who is not the person keeping the road, and if the witness is not here then the road does not open and nobody is to say afterwards that it failed, because it did not open and that is a different thing and it is the whole of what this page is for.*

*And the north steward is a carpenter of a yard on Weir Street and he is not the man who cut the seat and I have checked, and the man who cut the seat is a tradesman and he is not a steward and he has not asked to be and he has been told in a room on a bridge that he is not the mechanism, and he did not argue, and I want that in this book in case anybody comes in a year and says the north sent somebody.*
