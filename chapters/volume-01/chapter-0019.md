# Chapter 0019

The province renewed its finding on the second of Greenmonth, and by the fourth hour of the eighth Mara Vey had stopped writing in the first column of the day-book.

She had ruled a second column on the second itself, after the light went, with a knife she had sharpened herself in Hearthmonth, and she had headed it in her own hand and not in a fair copy, because a heading in a fair copy is a rule and she had not yet decided she had the right to make one: *Entered under the finding of the second, and therefore a year weaker than anything above it.*

Six days and she had not once had to say the word *unverified*. That was the trouble with the word, and she had learned it in four years. Nobody in Merefen had come in and used it. Nobody in the provincial town had needed to. The finding had gone down on the second the way a tide goes down, without anybody doing anything, and everything she entered after that day was standing in six inches of water and would be a foot under by the second of Greenmonth next year unless a man with a seal in his hand decided otherwise.

The rule was on the wall of the outer room in fair copy, because she had fought somebody about it once, in her second year, and had won, and because a rule in a fair copy is a rule and a rule in her own hand is a taste:

*An anchor is recorded on the word of a sworn tradesman, in his own hand, and not on a clerk's word, and not on a witness's word, and not on the word of a man who has an interest.*

Underneath it, on the second of Greenmonth, in the same ink and in a different hand — her own, the late hand, the one that wrote at the end of a day when the earlier hand had gone — there was a second line, and it was the first rule she had ever added, and she had added it without a fight, which frightened her more than the fight would have.

*No combined figure is entered in this office.*

She had not written the reason under it. A rule on a wall is read by forty people a month and a reason is only any use to the person who has to keep it. The reason was four years old, and it was on the first page of the day-book in her own hand with a line ruled under two figures, and the line had never once been crossed, and neither figure had ever been added to the other on any page in the building, because the moment they were added she would have made a place, and a place was not a place. A place had a ford, and a tithe barn, and a schoolmaster, and nine people who argued about a boundary stone every Longlight.

---

"Ma'am. Come and look at this one again."

Sarn Oyelaran had been at the copying frame since the sixth hour with the volume the registrar had brought down on the second, and she had the page down on the sloping board and a finger above it and not touching it, which was a thing she had stopped doing three days ago and had started doing again this morning, and Mara understood immediately that it was because the page had moved from *an odd thing in a bound book* to *a thing I am being asked to keep*.

"Out of place," said Sarn. "Same as Friday."

"Altered," said Mara.

She had used the word *out of place* on the second, in front of a registrar, because *out of place* was a bindery accident and *altered* was a crime, and she had spent three days deciding whether she was entitled to the second word.

"I want to know what I am entitled to," she said, "and I am not going to find out by being brave about it. Say it as a binder would."

Sarn said it as a binder would. A roll kept in order since the founding was bound in the order it was written, and a page that arrives out of that order is either *misbound* or *inserted*. Misbinding happens. It happens when a clerk works a year behind and lifts a quire out of a press by the two top sheets. It is a fault of the hand and it leaves the ink and the paper the same age as everything round it. An inserted page is made of the same paper and can be got at the same age, and it is inserted because a man had it in his hand and a reason.

"And this one," said Mara, "is not misbound. A misbound page comes out of the order. This one was put in."

"How can you tell that from the paper?"

"I cannot tell it from the paper. I can tell it from the hand." Mara came and stood where she had stood on the second, and read the page again, all of it, at the speed that would have been a scandal in her inner room and was the only speed that worked in a copyist's frame.

It was a clearance entry of the **three hundred and second year**. It sat in a bound order between two entries of three hundred and four, and the entries on either side of it were in the fair copy hand of a roll that had been kept in order since the founding, and this one was in a different hand, and the hand was not older and not younger. It was the hand of a man who had not seen the rest of the book.

The entry said: one bell. Nine stone by the founder's weight, which was a figure a clearance clerk took off a bell in the yard. It said *unclassified salvage*. It said no city and no river and no district. It had a number against it and an hour against the number, and it was a perfectly good entry, and it was the only entry in four hundred leaves of that roll with nothing in it to say where the thing came from.

"Now tell me the year," said Mara.

"Three hundred and two."

"And the other half of that year is three hundred and one. What is in three hundred and one?"

Sarn did not answer, and the not-answering was the answer, and both of them let it stand, because there was a rule about a missing half in this office as firm as the one on the wall and it was not a rule that had been written down by anybody.

Mara Vey wrote one line in the day-book and then stopped with the pen on the paper.

"Publish it," said Sarn.

"Publish it where."

Sarn had an answer ready and it was the wrong one and she gave it anyway, which was a thing she had learned to do in this room. "The magistrate. She made a finding on the fourth. A finding says an office produced nothing. We produce a Crown roll with a page in it that has been put in by a hand, in the year after, about a bell, with no place on it. That is not an argument. That is a document a Crown office has to answer."

"That is an argument made out of a document," said Mara. "And the moment I put it in front of her, it stops being a page in a roll and becomes a claim that a Crown roll has been written to, and I will have to prove the hand is not the registrar's, and I cannot prove it, and the registrar is a man who has already given me one honest sentence at no charge and I am not going to spend him to make my own sentence heavier."

Sarn stood at the frame for a moment with her hands behind her back.

"Then it goes in the press and nobody knows it's there," she said, "and in four years it is a thing I knew about."

"Yes."

"And the finding on the fourth does not touch it."

"The finding on the fourth does not touch it. And in four years, if it is in a Crown file, a clerk can trace it back to the hand that copied it out, and the hand that copied it out is a Crown clerk, and a Crown clerk who can be shown to have copied a page out of a Crown roll is a Crown clerk who cannot be a witness to anything else in the building."

"Then I want my hand on it," she said. "Not yours. Mine. And the date, and the hour, and the words *bound out of order, in a hand not of this roll*, and nothing else, and no opinion, and I will write it as a copyist and not as the keeper, because a copyist's note is a fact about a book and a keeper's note is an argument about a Crown."

Nobody said anything for a moment. Sarn Oyelaran put her hand flat on the board, and did not take it off again, and looked at her own hand on it for a while instead of at the person who had asked her for it.

Mara Vey looked at her for four seconds.

"Do it in that hand," she said, "and put it on the wrapper and not in the book, and give the wrapper to me and not to the press, because I have spent four years learning that a paper in a press is a paper that has been kept and a paper in a hand is a paper that has been sworn to, and the difference is the whole of this office and I have never once been able to put it on a wall."

---

The magistrate's clerk came at the ninth hour, and he came on foot, and he was the same man who had entered her rulings on the thirtieth and who had a bad chest and a worse manner and no opinion of anybody at all, which was the correct manner for a clerk, and who had by the ninth hour of the eighth of Greenmonth walked from the provincial landing up a rise he had no business climbing to serve four sheets of paper.

He put them on the table face down, which she noticed, and squared them, which she also noticed, and then he said the thing he had come to say without any of the four minutes of preamble she had been expecting.

"Ma'am. I have to serve the finding. It goes on the provincial court and on the office concerned, and the office concerned in this matter is an office that the court has found it cannot name, so it is going on me, and the court has directed me to notify the parties."

"And you have come to ask me who the parties are."

"I have come to ask you what the second clause means by *its dependent places*, and I have come because there is a page in this county that holds a list of names and no Crown office will pay for a copy of it, and I am a man who is sent to a place and not told why." He put his pen down, which was not a thing a clerk did. "If the seven villages in that valley are *dependent places* under the finding, they fall with the crossing. I write them into the service as dependent places and they are in it, and the finding does the rest of it by itself and neither I nor the court has to do anything further this year. If they are not, they are not parties to a finding about a bridge and I have served a copy on nobody and gone home."

Mara Vey sat very still, because a woman who has spent four years in filing rooms knows when an office has just handed her a door and cannot tell yet which side of it she is standing on.

"You have come to ask me," she said, "and you are going to write what I tell you, and I am going to tell you a thing and I am going to tell you why I am telling it, and then you are going to write it in your own hand, and I want both halves of that understood before I begin. Say yes or no."

"Say it."

"You may enter the villages as names."

"Then I may enter them as names," said the clerk, "and I shall, and it is four words shorter than the other way and I have never in five years been asked for the shorter version by anybody with a document."

He wrote it, then, at the table, in the ledger he had brought, and the entry read that the crossing of the Alder Vale and its dependent places, not being persons and not being parties, the service thereof was made upon the villages named below, being villages, and not upon dependent places, and the villages were named beneath it, and the clerk wrote each one out in full and gave each one the county's own spelling and not the Archive's, and Mara let him, because a clerk's spelling is the spelling a court will use and the Archive's spelling is the spelling a family will recognise, and the two are both true and only one of them has ever been served.

Then she said the other thing, and she said it out loud, in her own room, with a court officer writing in a ledger at her table, which is the only place in this kingdom where a keeper of records has ever been able to make a sentence stick.

"Now the number. How many are you going to write against them."

The clerk's pen stopped.

"There is a figure in the county's finding," he said. "Four thousand. It is in the finding. It is the figure the court will use."

"That is the Crown's figure and it is the Crown's. It is a number for a crossing and it is not a number for those seven, and I am not going to give it to you and I am not going to argue with you about it. The villages in this county are counted by name. On the first page of the book behind me there are two figures in my own hand with a line ruled under them, and the line is four years old, and neither figure has ever been added to the other on any page in this building, and I am not going to cross that line for a man with a bad chest and eleven cases at Dunmere."

She turned a sheet round and did not give it to him. She read it.

"Alder Cross, two hundred. Saltbrim, ninety. Wrenlow, sixty. Cobbett, a hundred and forty. Nine Elms, a hundred and ten. Stonebarrow, forty. And Hushwater, eighty."

"Seven hundred and twenty," said the clerk, and then stopped with his pen half an inch off the paper, because a man who has just added a column aloud in a room is a man who has noticed what he has done.

"You may not add them," said Mara Vey, "and I am not going to let you add them, and I did not. That is the whole of the reason and I am giving it to you without being asked, because I have a clerk's memory and you have a good chest and neither of us is going to be young, and because I would rather this rule were in your hand than in mine."

The clerk did not move.

"Ma'am."

"I know what it looks like. It looks like a woman who cannot add. Listen to the reason and then decide." She put one finger on the figure in the total line, which she did not do in front of claimants. "A total is one number. One number is a thing that can be checked once and can be disproved once, and nobody ever has to look at the reason it was given. The Crown does not have to beat me and does not have to answer a word I have said in four years. The Crown has to find one error in the sum. One. A hundred and six at Alder Cross instead of two hundred, and the seven are dependent places on a bridge that was never laid, and there is nothing left of them to appeal with, because the finding is in and the sum is out and the sum is mine.

"A name is not that. Nobody disproves a name. They look for it. And looking for Hushwater is a thing a man does for a year and comes back with a crossing book in a woman's hand and a schoolmistress's list and a ferryman off the north end who sat on a bench in my outer room for three days and would not leave, and not one of those three things can be knocked over with a column of arithmetic. That is the difference between a name and a total, and it is the only thing this office has ever had, and I have been keeping it four years with a press and a day-book and no money at all, and I am not going to have it undone because somebody wanted one clean figure for a service."

The room was very quiet, and the river went under the floor, and the clerk of the court of the Sable's mouth sat at the table of the keeper of an archive two floors above a man who weighed salt for a living and did not write anything for a long time.

"I have a bad chest," he said, "and I am not going to pretend to you that I have not understood that. I will tell you what I am going to do and then I am going to do it." He squared the ledger to the table the way he squared everything. "I am going to serve the finding on seven villages by name, in the county's spelling, with the figures of this office against each of them separately, and I am going to write *sum not stated* in the total line, and I am going to leave a space. And the space is going to be the shape of your argument for as long as this file is open, which is longer than either of us, and every man who opens it after us is going to see a clerk who was given seven figures and did not write down the eighth."

He wrote it. And he wrote the hour, because she had taught him to write the hour in six days by never once failing to ask for it, and when he had done he read it back and put the pen in his pocket and got up and had a small bad-breath moment by the door.

"Ma'am. Off the record, and I will deny it in a room."

"Go on."

"Clause three renews the county's power over the valley annually, to officers who are not named. I have served that in four counties in five years and I have never once been asked what it is for, and this week I have read a finding that says a watch captain in a fen town was given a paper and could not refuse it, and my lord's clerk in Dunmere has a file on the subject of a woman magistrate who has just written down that she cannot name the officers." He straightened. "I am not a friend of yours. I am a man who carries a ledger up a hill. But a thing I carry up a hill gets read by somebody, and this week the only thing anybody has sent me up a hill for is a bell that was lawfully taken by a paper, and I would like you to know that I have noticed."

"You have," said Mara, "and I am going to write the word *noticed* in my day-book, and I am not going to pretend that means anything. It means a man with a bad chest will remember a room. That is all a room is for."

---

Jos Rusk came at the eleventh hour with the crossing book in the waxed case and a week's arithmetic behind his face.

He had been in the market on the fourth. He had not been in the room in the stair; he had been at the back of the crowd on the wharf road, which is where a man of fifty-one stands on a market morning in a fen town nobody has ever counted, and he had heard a magistrate say *lawful procedure*, and he had heard in the hour afterwards, from two men at a stall and then from a third, that a man with a barrow had said in a stair that the Crown had taken the bell because it was the fever, and that if the bell was the fever and the Crown had taken it then the Crown was right, and if he was wrong then somebody had been paid.

"He has been saying it for a fortnight and it has got a week further into this town than the finding did," said Rusk. "I have been eleven years telling a room there was a Crown behind it and I have never once had a thing I could put my hand on. On Sunday I had a man in a stair with a sergeant's statement naming him, and I have been repeating it ever since, and I would like you to notice that a thing in a magistrate's record has got me further in one week than nine years of being right."

"Then let us be exact about what you have," said Mara Vey. "A magistrate held the seizure lawful. She found that the office behind the paper produces nothing. She recorded that there is no office of keeper of the Bellweather hinge in this county's charter, in the county's list, or in the Crown's own schedule, and that two instruments before her describe a person as holding it. And a watch sergeant, unasked, wrote down what his men did, and named a man who has been telling this market since the seventeenth that the bell carries a fever, and that man was in the stair, and a court cannot un-sell a story in a morning." She put her pen down. "That is four things and none of them is a name."

"I did not say it was a name."

"You said a man in a stair with a sergeant's statement naming him, and he was named by trade, in a record, in a court, and not by me, and I am not going to write *the eel-man of Merefen* at the top of a Hushwater claim and make your village into a thing that happened in a market. That would be the easiest page in this office to write and it would be worth nothing in a year."

He sat down without being asked, which was a thing he had learned in this room, and put the case on the table and undid the strap.

"The wrapper," he said.

Mara had it ready. It was a sheet of good paper with a number in the corner and the exhibit entered in her own hand and a line ruled under it for a second witness, and it was the only document in the building with a space in it, and the space had been empty for six days and she had looked at it more often than she had looked at the finding.

"Three things," she said. "The first is your wife's hand and the place her hand is in. That is done and it is done in the order she wrote it and no clerk will ever take it away again, because a keeper of a book is a name in the order of a book and that is a better fate for a name than being a heading."

"The second."

"The second is the eleventh line. It is in a different hand and it is in different ink, and you have told me twice that it is not hers, and I believe you, and believing you is worth nothing." She turned the wrapper so the ruled line was between them. "That line has to be attested by a man who is paid for his day and who is not in my office and not in yours and not in the Crown's, and it has to be attested by a tradesman, because a tradesman's account of a thing he has handled is a thing a court cannot improve, and because the only men in this county whose hands anybody checks are the men who write other men's books with a date in them. I am not going to do it and I am not going to let my clerk do it, because an office that witnesses its own exhibit is a room with nobody in it."

"And the third."

"The third is that it costs him," said Mara, "and you should know that before I say it, because you have been in this room twice and both times you have been careful with me and I would rather be paid in the same kind." She squared the wrapper. "There is a road warden in this town who is the only man in the county whose hand a court would take as a tradesman's account, and he is named in two provincial instruments this month as the keeper of an office that this court has searched three lists for and cannot find. His name is going into a Crown file again if he witnesses this, in a claim about Hushwater, alongside the two papers that call him a keeper, and the two sets of paper will be read together by somebody eventually, because somebody always reads them together, because that is how a man who is not careful with himself gets made into a thing."

Jos Rusk sat with that.

"You are telling me it makes him worse," he said.

"I am telling you it makes him *legible*," said Mara. "There is a difference and it is the only difference in this building worth money. A man nobody can read can be destroyed at leisure. A man whose name is on four documents, none of which agree, cannot be destroyed by any one office, because any office that touches one of them has to explain the other three. He will be safer in a file than he is out of one, and I know that sounds like a thing a keeper of records says to get a signature, and it is, and it is also true, and I have watched it be true twice and I have never once been able to prove it in advance."

"You want him to be worse off."

"I want him to be *findable*," said Mara Vey. "Which is a colder thing to wish on a man and a much more useful one, and I have known that for four years and I have never had a person to spend it on."

Rusk put the case down and squared it, and she saw him decide.

"Fetch him," he said. "He will come if you send for him, I think. He is the only man in this town who answered a note in a hand that was not his."

---

She went down to Weir Street at the second hour, and the yard was busy.

She had a reason for the walk and the reason was a mark. There is a bench mark cut into the third course of the Sedge Reach abutment, no bigger than a thumbnail, and every level taken on that bridge for two generations has been taken off it, and it had fresh lime in it on the first of Greenmonth because somebody in the guild had re-cut it by hand after the flood and had put it a quarter of an inch low, which is nothing to the eye and a very great deal to a level, and which means that every height anybody in Merefen has taken off that mark since the storm is a quarter of an inch wrong in the direction that makes a deck look better than it is.

A road warden had found it on the first, taken a level off it twice, written the figure in a notebook with a date and his own mark on the lid of the guild's chest, and told the guild's own man that a road warden does not put papers in against the bench's own mark.

She wanted it entered in her book.

It was, she thought on the stair down, the same disease. A Crown roll that is bound out of order, and a guild bench that re-cuts its own datum by hand in a wet month, and a finding that says *not built* about a bridge a mason would recognise in a morning. Every one of them was a record that had been changed by a man who needed it changed, and not one of them had done it for money, and the reason nothing could be done about any of them was that no person in this county had ever entered a figure that somebody else had to live with.

The yard was loud in the way a yard is loud on the first day of a month's work. There were four men on the face stone and the dust of it went up white in a shaft of afternoon sun, and the sound of it was the sound of iron on stone every four seconds, and there was a contract weighted on the bench under a lump of chalk.

Tovan Kest had a chisel in one hand and a pencil behind his ear and no time, and he gave her four minutes and then two more, and he did not ask her what it was about, which was the single most useful thing about him.

"The road warden took a level off it on the first," she said. "Off the Sedge mark. I want the figure entered in my book, and I want the guild's man to know it came out of my book and not his, and I want it in a hand that is not mine."

"How much of it do you want?"

"The figure, the date, and the fact that two readings agreed. Not the argument. I have no argument about a mark."

Tovan put the chisel down and got the pencil from behind his ear and wrote three lines on the back of the contract, and then stopped and looked at the lump of chalk on it and at his own three lines, and made a decision that she watched him make.

"I'm not putting a clerk's figure on a factor's paper," he said. "That's the third time this month I've had a thing offered that would have been my own signature on somebody else's account. I'll copy it out fair and I'll witness the copy, and the witness is the paper, and the paper goes in your book, and if the guild comes and asks me where the figure came from I can hand them the paper instead of an opinion." He looked up. "That'll want my hand on it. Not a signature. A hand that says *copied from this contract at the second hour of the eighth, T. Kest, carpenter*."

"That is exactly right and I would have asked for it in those words and I am glad you got there first."

"I've had a month of people getting there second," said Tovan Kest, and went back to the stone.

She stood in the yard for a while after that and did not say anything, because she had come down a stair with a figure in her head and had walked out of it with a paper in her hand, and the difference between the two was worth the walk.

And then she read the contract.

She read it because it was lying face up under a lump of chalk, and because she reads anything left in the open, and because four men were cutting stone on the strength of it and it had a factor's name at the top of it and nine tons of setts on the towpath below the mill and two tons still in a field on the western bank with nobody standing over either.

It was a good contract. It was, as far as she could tell from standing up, a better contract than anything the town trust had entered into in four years, and it had a date on the sixth of Greenmonth, four marks into the yard on the day, the cutting to begin inside the month, and the balance on delivery at the mill. It had a yard's name on it and a rate in it and a duration in it — a month, for four men, at eight pence a day and cartage at the yard price — and at the foot, in Tovan's hand, a line that said an advance was to be cleared against the cutting at the yard rate and at the first Friday after delivery, and not before.

Eight pence a day. Four marks is a hundred and ninety-two pence. It is twenty-four days at that rate, and every Friday in between there is a man in a yard a little further from owing it, and a rate, and a term, and a third party's name on the paper, and a clerk who could open the book and see all four of those things at once.

And there is not one other way to have four marks in Merefen by tomorrow morning. She knew the other one without having to look for it, because she had watched a man sign it in a guild hall in Mudmonth and had a clerk's remark about it in her memory ever since: six marks against a tool-chest the roll had priced at three and a half, and *a man who signs six and has three and a half is a man who has signed on faith, and faith is not a currency we take.*

A bond was a man's faith with his own tools as the only thing standing behind it. It bought a thing and it cost a thing and the clerk was right, and a clerk is never wrong about a thing he has had to record eleven times.

A debt against a contract was a document. It had a third party's name on it, and a rate, and a term, and a clerk could enter it, and a clerk who entered it was a person who would remember it in a room in six months when the man who signed it asked whether it had ever been real.

---

At the sixth hour the office had four things in it that had come in on one day, which was more than the office had held in any week of its four years, and three of them were documents and one of them was nothing of the kind: a page of a Crown roll bound out of order and attested by a copyist with no opinion in it, a sheet of good paper with a space in it, seven villages in a court officer's ledger with the sum left off, and a cooper's boy up the half-landing stair at the fourth hour with a paper folded in four and gone down again before she could ask him a word, because he had been told not to wait. The wrapper had four digits on it in a fair hand she knew.

And the one thing she had bought four shillings for was not in the building.

That was the part she had understood on the second and had not liked since. The four shillings had bought a date. The registry had a search entered against the Bellweather Claims Archive on the second of Greenmonth, a fee paid, and a receipt with an hour on it, and a receipt for a search is the receipt for having asked. The closed press at the provincial registry would not open to a keeper of a public register for anything on earth short of a magistrate's order, and the order was written on the second and stamped on the fourth, and it came back on the fifteenth, and until it came back the whole of what the office knew about the *Anchors, schedules and dispositions, Alder Vale* was a registrar's spoken sentence, taken down word for word and sworn to by a copyist in her own hand: that the book was entered on the roll as standing in the fourth press; that it was not on the shelf; that the gap was about two inches and a half; and that nobody in that office had entered the gap anywhere.

Eleven days. She could do arithmetic in her sleep and it had never once been any use to her.

She entered it before she did anything else, and she did it in the ordinary way, and the ordinary way is that a reported observation is not an exhibit and cannot be sworn, and that it goes in as what it is.

*Reported. A sound described as a bell, heard on the seventh day of Greenmonth between the eleventh hour and midday, on or off the Old Toll crossing, in a dead calm, by two men on the Sedge Reach deck and one boy, thirteen, of Merefen, afterwards at the tower. One note, three seconds, of a bad and cracked sound. No bell is on this reach. The bell is in the custody of the watch of Merefen, under seal, in a storehouse by the fish weir, four hundred yards from the crossing described. Witnessed by the tower's sight-line to the weir and the storehouse door. The report is filed as reported. It is not offered as evidence of anything.*

She read it twice, and it was a page that could not be answered, and she thought about the man with a barrow who had said a true and useful thing in a stair with no hour in it, and about the two men on a deck who had, and about the fact that the only difference between them was a piece of paper, and that the paper had been written by a boy of thirteen who had then been unable to resist adding that he had gone up the tower and looked and seen nothing.

*This office has no power to make a bell sound or to stop one. It has entered an hour, a direction, a sight-line, and the names of three witnesses, and it has entered them because they are the only parts that cannot be improved on by anybody.*

And then the door of the outer room, and two men in it, and one of them a claimant she had sent for and the other one not a claimant at all.

Rusk was there because she had sent for him. Aren Kest was there because Rusk had sent for him, which she had told him to do, and which he had done in a way that told her he had understood the instruction and its weight.

He came into the inner room without being asked, which was the first thing he had ever done in this office that was not a courtesy, and he put his hat down out of the light and stood with his back to the press, and she saw that he had not come for the wrapper first.

"You have something," she said. "It came on the half-landing and it is in the book and you may read it whenever you want, and you will want to be careful with it, because it is the only paper in this building that cannot be improved on by anybody and that includes you."

"Ma'am."

"That is not what I was going to say." She put the pen down. "Sit down. I have got four marks of arithmetic to do with you and it is the only arithmetic in this office that I am going to put in front of a witness, and Mr Rusk is the witness and does not know it yet, so."

Rusk sat down. And then Aren Kest sat down as well, which he had not been asked to do, because he had worked out in the time it had taken her to say the sentence that the two most expensive facts in this building were the height of a bench mark and the price of two discs of glass, and that she could not possibly have meant to spend an hour of a man's evening on the first one.

She told them about the glass.

She told it exactly. Two discs, Steppe glass, cut and annealed to a bridge pin. Four marks the pair. Ready in a fortnight. Money before cutting. And then she did the sum in a room with two men in it, out loud, because a keeper of records who does a sum in her head has no standing whatsoever if anybody ever disputes it, and because she wanted to see the number arrive.

"The ninth is tomorrow," she said. "There is a road warden in this town who is summoned to the provincial town on the ninth, and who is going to stand on a bridge somewhere and come back through it, and I have known since the second of Greenmonth that I was watching a man run at a thing with no weeks in it, and I have said nothing to you about it for six days because you did not come in and ask me and because I am not, on the evidence, a person who is told things."

"Then there is nothing to be done," said Rusk.

"There is exactly one thing to be done, and it is not a thing I can do, and it is the reason I asked you both to sit down." She looked at Aren Kest. "Four marks. You have a mark and a quarter and eleven days of wages that the trust will not pay until there is money in the trust. There are two ways to have four marks in Merefen by tomorrow morning. One of them is a second bond on the roll, on a tool-chest the guild's clerk has already priced at three and a half, and I want you to hear the clerk's own sentence about the first one, because you were not in the room when he said it and I have carried it since the twenty-eighth of Mudmonth: *a man who signs six and has three and a half is a man who has signed on faith, and faith is not a currency we take.*"

"I remember it," said Aren Kest.

"Yes. You remember it, and you have thought about it every day since, and you have known since the first of Greenmonth exactly which way that road goes and which way the other one goes." She squared the sheet. "The other one is a debt. Not a favour and not a gift and not a thing anybody says in a yard. A debt, against a contract, with a third party's name on it, at a rate, to a term, entered in a book that a clerk can open and that a clerk is obliged to keep. And there is a contract lying face up on a bench in Weir Street this afternoon with a factor's name at the top of it, and it will carry four marks to a man at eight pence a day for twenty-four days, and every Friday in between there will be a man in a yard who is a little further from owing it, and that is what makes it honest. Not the money. The third party's name."

Rusk had been looking at the two of them and not at the sheet.

"Miss Vey," he said, "you have just described a thing a clerk does, and a clerk is what you told me to be afraid of."

"I have described what a clerk does when a clerk is in a room with a contract and a rate and a man's own name in his own hand," said Mara Vey. "You want to know the difference between the two and it is one sentence and it is the whole of my trade. A bond is a man's faith. A debt is a man's work with a term on it. You may fail at both. Only one of them can be entered, and a thing that can be entered can be produced in six months by somebody who is not you and does not like you and is holding a ledger."

She wrote the day out in full, and she wrote it at the bottom of the page where she had written the other things, and the ink was nearly gone and she had to warm the pen twice.

*Bell report of the seventh filed as reported, with three names on it, and I have written out in full what it does not say, because a document that states its own limits cannot be improved on by an office that wants to be clever.*

*Four marks for two discs. The slip says four marks the pair, ready in a fortnight, money before cutting. If the money is in the room tonight, the glass is ready in a fortnight. If the money is in the room tomorrow morning, the glass is ready on the twenty-third of Greenmonth. The ninth is the ninth. I am not going to pretend to a man in my own office that the two figures can be made to agree, and I am not going to be the person who says a thing to him in a room at the sixth hour of the eighth of Greenmonth that the clock is not the clock.*

*And the honest way is a debt against a contract, and there is a contract, and I have read it standing up in a yard and I did not ask permission, and I would have walked four miles to see it and I would have asked, and I did not ask because I was in a hurry and because I am the keeper of an archive and the only thing I am entitled to take from a yard is a figure, and I took a contract. I want that written down in the same ink as everything else.*

*There are two people in this room and one of them has just been told by an office where his money can come from, and it is not my money and it is not his, and I would like somebody to know that I did it in front of a witness and that I did not enjoy it and that I would do it again before I would let a man buy glass with a lien on his own tools.*

Then she closed the book and capped the ink and looked at the two men in her filing room, one of whom had a claim and one of whom had a bridge, and neither of whom was going to be able to do a single thing about any of it until a bench carpenter in Weir Street decided what a wage advance was.

"Mr Kest," she said. "The attestation is a sheet of good paper and a line and a hand, and you will want to read it before you write on it, and there is nothing in it about Hushwater except a village and a book, and everything in it about you except the bell, which is not in it. If you would rather witness it in the yard and not in this room, say so and it will be witnessed in the yard and I will not think anything of it."

"I will do it here," said Aren Kest, "and I will do it now, and I will not read it twice, and you will tell me if I have got a figure wrong."

So Mara Vey turned the wrapper round, and put the pen in his hand, and stood over a road warden of thirty-two while he wrote, in a tradesman's hand, on a sheet of good paper, in a filing room two floors above a man who weighed salt for a living, that the eleventh line of the crossing book of Hushwater was not the hand of Anwen Rusk and was not written by her and could not have been, and that he had been in the room and had looked at both and was a man who writes other men's books with dates in them and had no interest in the answer either way.

Then she dated it and entered the hour and wrote her own name under his as the person who took it, and put the wrapper in the press with the bar on it, and it was the fifth claim-numbered thing in that press in four years and the heaviest.

Down on Weir Street a yard went on cutting face stone, and on the Old Toll road a slip nailed inside a hut in a frame of four split sticks said *two discs, Steppe glass, cut and annealed to a bridge pin. Four marks the pair, ready in a fortnight. Money before cutting,* and the fire in the shed at the back was not lit, because it was the eighth of Greenmonth and there was no order in the county for anybody to want a thing cut.

Mara Vey wrote the figure on the back of an envelope, the four marks and the rate and the term and the words *a debt against a contract and not a bond*, and put the date and the hour on it, and gave it to the cooper's boy and told him to take it down Weir Street and give it to a man in a yard who did not need to be told what it was for.

"You'll want him to sign it," said the boy.

"He will want to. That is not the same as being made to, and I want it on the paper that he asked for it or did not."

Then she went down the stair and out into a street that smelled of salt and cold mud and stone dust, and she did not go up the Old Toll road, and she went home up two flights to a room she rented over a cooper, and she wrote one more line in the day-book on her knee because she had left it in the press, and the line was that she had sent a slip down to a yard on Weir Street with a figure on it, and that she had never in four years sent a note to a man that was not in a file.

The file it belonged in would not be open for some time.
