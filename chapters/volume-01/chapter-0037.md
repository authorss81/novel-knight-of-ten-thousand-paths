# Chapter 0037

The bell came into Merefen on the ninth of Longlight in a cart at the fourth hour in the morning, and eleven people saw it happen and four of them told somebody, and it stood in the storehouse by the fish weir for one night, and a watch captain moved it out of there into his own watch house before daylight on the tenth.

That was all it was. There was no order in it, no seal on the cart, no officer walking beside it, and no boy sent ahead. A handcart came up the lane by the fish weir with a rope at the tail of it and two men walking, and the rope went through the headstock loop and a man on each side took the weight, because that is how a nine-stone bell is moved and nobody in Merefen had to be told, and a woman putting a line out of her door saw it go past and said the thing out loud and a boy on the wharf heard her and repeated it, and by the fourth hour of the afternoon there were three versions of it in the town and by the sixth there was one.

Then, on the twelfth, a boy on Weir Street told the guild's clerk that a Crown's paper had come to the toll-house off the county's post and had not been received by anybody, and the guild's clerk walked up the street and read the outside of it standing at a counter, and then walked back down Weir Street to a yard at a speed that four men in the yard stopped work to look at.

It was Tovan Kest's brother who read the paper inside, because the toll-clerk of Merefen is sixty-eight and does not open a Crown's paper for a guild, and because the guild's clerk had learned a thing about Crown's paper in eleven weeks and the thing was that you do not stand in a shop doorway discussing it.

The paper was a **returnable order of the provincial court**, dated the ninth of Longlight at the second hour, addressed to the bench of the Merefen bridge guild in the matter of the crossing of Alder Vale, and it required the bench, by the fifteenth of Longlight, to state in writing what it held in respect of the said crossing, by what authority it held it, and **the name of the officer in whose custody the object held in the said matter is kept.**

And the bench did not answer it, because the bench had no officer in its custody and no object and no matter, and it could have said so, and saying so would have been an answer.

Instead the county refused to carry it. The county clerk at the county town read it on the tenth, and the county clerk wrote back on the eleventh in four lines and sent it back down the road unexecuted, and the four lines said that no officer of this county has authority to execute an order of the superior court, and that the addressee is a bench of a town's guild and not an officer of anything, and that the matter is therefore returned, and that the county has entered the request with its day and its hour.

And on the back of it, because a county clerk has to say what a thing is in order to return it, and because there was a printed space on the form and a printed space that is left blank gets ruled through and dated and read as an answer forty years later, the county clerk wrote in his own hand the matter, exactly as it was made, and the matter was:

*In the matter of the Bellweather hinge, and of the office of keeper thereof.*

---

The bench sat on the fifteenth of Longlight at the ninth hour in the long room over the wharf, on a Saturday, which no man in the room remarked on, which was the remark.

Reeve Aldis Fenn had called it. She had called it on the Wednesday at the second hour, standing at a counter in the toll-house, having read a paper, and she had said two sentences to the guild's clerk and one of them was that the bench would sit and the other was that it would sit on Saturday and not on Friday, and the clerk had written both down.

There were eight men on the bench and the reeve, who had a minute to write, and the clerk with the roll, and the guild's man with the levels, who had a bench order and nothing else and had come because he was on the bench. Tovan Kest sat on a stool at the end, near the door, where a man who is a party and not a member gets put, and the putting had been done without anybody saying anything, which was how this town did things.

Aren Kest was not in the room.

Nobody had served him. Nobody could serve him: there is no process in this county that puts a man out of a room, and the trust's own instrument gives the bench no power over a person at all, it gives it power over a crossing. He had been told on the Thursday morning by his brother that the bench was sitting on Saturday about a thing that was about him, in a yard, standing up, with a bar in his hand.

The clerk entered the absence, and he entered it in a particular form, and Tovan Kest read it over his shoulder from the stool and did not say a word about it then or afterwards.

*Entered absent. The said Kest was not within this county's power to summon, was not served, and is absent at his own request, made on the thirteenth of Longlight, and the bench has been informed that the said Kest stands named in a provincial ledger in respect of the ninth of Greenmonth, and the bench has not been asked to consider it, and has declined to consider it, and has entered no reason.*

*No reason given.*

It is three words, and two of them are the clerk's own hand and the third is the guild's standing joke, and it was the fifth of them in the roll in four months, and the clerk had said in a yard in Greenmonth that he hoped in five years somebody would find the five and read them together, and he had not said it again, and he had begun, quietly, to write the dates in the margin beside them.

---

"I will put the question," said Reeve Aldis Fenn, "and I will put it in the plainest words I have and I will not improve it and I will not put anything under it.

"Does this bench stand behind its own road warden, its own roll, and its own member, in respect of the object in the matter of the Bellweather hinge.

"That is the question. It has been put to this bench by a document two hundred miles off, on the one side, and by a market board with five lines on it, on the other, and I am not going to pretend those are two different questions, because they are one question and this room has to answer it or it has to say it cannot, and both of those are available and I have thought about nothing else for two days.

"And I am going to say my own want once, in one sentence, and I have said it in this room in Greenmonth and I am saying it again because this is a different room.

"**The road open, the markets kept, and this town able to govern itself without a subsidy out of a magistrate's chair.**"

The room did not move, because eight men had heard that sentence before and had heard it twice in eleven weeks and it was not a threat, it was a want, and a want in a room is a different thing from an argument.

The reeve put her hand on the paper.

"Now. Every single argument for standing behind him is an argument this bench has already made against a Crown's paper. That is the difficulty and it is not a difficulty of a day. A bench that votes about a Crown is a bench that has stopped being a bench and become a letter. And this bench has taken one vote since the nineteenth of Mudmonth and it was a vote about a bond, six marks against a chest at three and a half, and the man the bond was about was four days off under an arch, and the vote was carried, and the minute carries his statement and my statement and the clerk's statement, and there is not one Crown paper in the whole of that minute. **I would like that read out in this room. Somebody read that minute out.**"

The clerk read it out. It took four minutes. Nobody interrupted.

"Thank you. Now, the true thing, and I am going to say it because I have costed it and I have a minute to write and the minute is the only thing any of us will be left with.

"In eleven weeks this bench has entered, in its own hand and in its own book, a notice of removal with a valuation on it, a bond for six marks with a surety's chest on it, a valuation of a bell as unclassified municipal salvage, a vote carrying that bond, a minute saying the trust of the Old Toll crossing is in abeyance, a minute of a Saturday in which a member of this bench is entered absent and no reason is given, and a resolution to keep the crossing out of our own materials and our own men's time. **That is seven documents. All of them are ours. Every one of them is true. Not one of them was written by a Crown.** And the two things that are the Crown's are not in that list, because we never entered either of them, and I will come to them."

She let that sit for about three seconds, which was a long time in a guild hall.

"Everything that office has in this county, it has because a bench of bridgewrights in a fen town did its job properly and wrote it down. The bond is the reason they know his name and his chest. The notice is the reason they know the bell was on our stone. The valuation is the reason they know a thing of six marks was on it and who said so. The account this bench swore to in Mudmonth is in a provincial file, and a word about a man on this roll is in a provincial ledger in another one, and both of those are in there because this bench sat for two hours and a half and did its work. And the two that are theirs are a quarter of an inch in a remarks column on a county schedule, which one of our own men put there in his own hand with a witness, and a piece of paper nailed to a watch house with four nails, which this bench did not write and did not enter and cannot answer for." **Nobody has forged anything. We handed it all over in our own hands and then we complained that it was being used, and that is the whole of the last eleven weeks and it is the most shameful thing I have ever had to say in this room.**"

The guild's man with the levels put his hand flat on the table, and did not speak, and nobody spoke for about ten seconds.

"So I am not going to ask nine men to vote on a Crown's paper," said the reeve, "and I am not going to ask them to vote about a man, and I am going to put the only thing to them that this bench has the power to put, and it is a bridge."

---

"I will not vote," said Tovan Kest, and he said it standing up, from the stool, before anybody had put a question to him, and four men in the room turned round.

"You will sit down," said the man in the chair.

"I will stand, and I will say why in one sentence, and then the clerk will enter it, and then I will not vote, and then he will read it back and I will put my hand under his and not over it, and it is four minutes of your morning and you will get the bridge afterwards.

"I am on the roll. I am his brother. I hold the advance of two marks and three quarters at eight pence a day, and it is entered in that roll as a debt of his and not of the guild's, and I said in this room in Greenmonth that a man on both sides of a thing cannot weigh it, and I am not voting now for the reason I gave then and not for a better one, and if I give you a better one today you should stop trusting anything I put in a minute."

"Master Kest, you said that in this room in Greenmonth," said the clerk, "and I have it in the roll, and I would like it entered that this is the second time in five months that a man has stood up in this room and said the true thing and then declined to vote on it, and I would like the second occasion entered as a second occasion."

"Enter it," said Tovan Kest.

"Entered. And the true thing first, because the true thing is the only part of this morning that is not about a Crown and I would like it in the book before we go on to the bridge."

He put his hand on the back of the stool.

"I am a carpenter and I keep a yard, and my brother cut the seat in the fourth course of the Old Toll sill himself on the eighth of Greenmonth, and there is a page in this guild's chest that says so with a date and an hour, and there is a page in a press over a salt merchant that says so, and there is a page in my own yard's book that says so in my hand and nobody asked me for it, and that is what he asked me for in the second week of Greenmonth, in a yard, at the fourth hour, in the rain, and he asked me for it in about nine words and I said yes and I have never been thanked for it and I am not going to be.

"Here is the true thing and it is not an opinion.

"**My brother is not the reason a Crown's paper can touch this bench.** The Crown's paper can touch this bench because the bell was on a bridge in this town and it was cracked and it was on our roll, and the roll is our roll, and every document in it is a true document written by us. The man did not make himself answerable to that office. **He made himself answerable to us.** He signed a bond in front of a clerk in this guild house on the eleventh of Mudmonth and he named the surety before the clerk had finished asking, and every single thing that office has ever been able to say about him in this county it can say because of a piece of paper in this room with my name at the bottom of it as a witness and my own mark and a quarter of my own money in the same line as paid and not as credited.

"So if the bench stands behind him it is not loyalty and it is not a vote about a Crown. It is a vote about whether this guild is going to keep the only thing that has made anybody in this town answerable for anything in eleven weeks, and that thing is that when a man in this town puts his name to a paper, this bench means it.

"And I am not voting on it and I have said why, and I would ask the bench to be careful about the fact that the man who says the true thing in this room in five months is going to have to live in this town, and I do not have a vote and I am telling you that out loud in a public room, which is the second time this year I have said the phrase *public room* in this building, and I would like the clerk to enter that too."

"Entered," said the clerk.

---

The bench voted on a bridge.

It sat for eleven minutes and it did it the way it did everything, which is slowly, and with the man in the chair saying the same thing twice to make sure the second saying was the one that got entered.

The resolution went in the roll in a fair hand and the clerk read it back twice, and it said:

*That the Old Toll crossing, four hundred yards of approach, the wharf kerb upon it, the footway, the parapet and the coping, being held in a trust in abeyance since the fourth of Greenmonth, YR 312, and having neither owner nor crew, be kept in repair by the bench of this guild out of the materials and the time of its own men and at the cost of this guild, and not out of the trust, and not at the charge of any person holding tolls upon it, and not on account of any person named in any paper of the Crown;*

*And that the bench records that it does this because a crossing with no owner has no crew, and that this bench is not the owner of that crossing and does not claim to be, and does not know who is;*

*And that the bench records that it has been informed that a returnable order of the provincial court dated the ninth of Longlight and addressed to this bench was returned unexecuted by the county on the eleventh, and that the bench has read it and has taken no notice of it, and has answered nothing, and will answer nothing, and that no reason is given;*

*And that the bench records that it sat on a Saturday, and that it is entered because it sat on a Saturday.*

The vote was carried. Eight hands and the reeve's, and one abstention that was not an abstention because Tovan Kest was not a member and had said so in a public room.

The reeve entered her minute afterwards, and it is four lines long, and it is in her own hand, and it is the shortest minute in the roll.

*I have been the reeve of this town nine years and I have costed the loss of the wharfage and the market at four or five marks a year and a market day, and I have said so in this room twice and I am not going to say it again. I record only that a bench has voted to keep a bridge it does not own, at its own cost, without being asked by any court, and that nine men in a guild house decided that on their own money on a Saturday afternoon in Longlight, and that I did not vote because I am the reeve and a reeve does not vote on a matter of the guild's own, and that I have wanted to for nine years.*

---

The toll-clerk's chalk on the outside of the paper was already there when the bench had finished, and it had been there for three days, and it read:

*Crown paper, the ninth of Longlight, YR 312, second hour, from the provincial court. Addressed to the bench of this guild in the matter of the crossing of Alder Vale. Returned by the county on the eleventh without execution. Held. Not answered. The bench is a town's guild and is not an officer of anything and this county knows it.*

And the reeve made them read the back as well, on account of the printed space, and the clerk read it out in a guild hall to eight men:

*In the matter of the Bellweather hinge, and of the office of keeper thereof.*

Nobody said anything at all for the length of a slow count of five.

"That is on the back of it," said the reeve, "because a clerk in a county office has to write down what a thing is in order to return it, and because a county clerk in this kingdom is not to leave a printed space blank, and because in about forty years somebody is going to be taught that a blank gets ruled through and dated. He wrote the matter down so that he could refuse it properly, and the refusing put the words *office of keeper* into this room in a Crown's clerk's hand with a date on it, and there is no Crown's paper in this county that does that. In eleven weeks an office that has answered every question we have put to it with a date and a seal has handed this town the one page it has wanted, and it has done it by refusing to do something, and it will not know for four years that it has done it, and by then it will be in a file in a building two hundred miles off along with everything else."

"That is a Crown's name in our hall," said the guild's man with the levels, "in a clerk's hand, on a returned paper, and in eleven months there will be nine men in this room who have seen it, and the ninth will say *I was here when it came*, and that is how a thing becomes a thing this town has always known, and I do not like it, and I am not going to suggest we burn a document in a guild chest, and I will say the thing I actually think, which is that the Crown's refusal to use its own court against a town's bench is worth more to this town than a bell-shaped hole in a parapet, and that I have been in this guild thirty-one years and this is the first time I have wanted a paper of theirs on my wall."

"Read it into the roll," said Reeve Aldis Fenn. "In full, the front and the back, with the county's four lines and the county's hand, and the date, and no opinion. And I will minute that the bench has read it and taken no notice of it and answered nothing, and that is already in the resolution, and now it is on the paper as well, and no reason is given."

The clerk read it into the roll.

And then the room did the thing that happens in a guild hall at the tenth hour when something has been read twice and voted on, which is that it emptied in about four minutes, and the man in the chair went out to the wharf steps and the man with the levels went to look at a gate post, and the clerk began ruling lines on a clean leaf for the next entry and had to stop and go and stand at the window.

Tovan Kest stayed on the stool for a minute after everybody else had gone, which he had not done in five months.

There was nothing wrong with the resolution. He had read it four times and it was a good resolution and it was the correct one and it was the only thing a bench could have voted on, and the bench had carried it with eight hands and a reeve's, and the road would be kept by four men out of a yard for about four marks and six a year, which the yard had, because a factor's four marks had come in on the seventh of Longlight and a second load was on the water, and the wages had gone out on a Friday at the yard rate as a wage and not a favour.

And a man who was not in the room had not been able to be in the room, and the clerk had entered the fact of his absence in a form that said the bench was protecting him, and that was true, and it was also the first document in the whole of this business that had been written in order to keep something out of it, and Tovan Kest had watched it happen and had put his name under the clerk's and not over the clerk's and had said the true thing in a public room and had not voted, and he was going to have to live here, and the road was going to be shut for a year, and his brother was going to read this minute in a year in a room like this one and would understand every word of it and would not be able to do one single thing about it.

He got up off the stool and went down to the Old Toll abutment at the eleventh hour with the bar over his shoulder, and the crossing was there, and the coping was sound, and the iron cradle was gone and the moss had come back over the whole of the bed of the stone, and the wax on the post had not run, and four hundred yards of the oldest bridge in the county was going to be kept, for a year, by nine men who had voted to keep it in a room on a Saturday, out of materials and time and not one penny of anybody's money, and not a single person in Merefen knew a thing about the four marks and six, and the two men who had been sent up the hill to clear the bed of the stone that morning knew the first half of it and had been told nothing at all of the second.
