# Chapter 0012

The office had one rule on the wall in a fair copy, and on the afternoon of the first of Greenmonth there were two men in the outer room who had both been told about it by different people and neither of whom had been told it by her.

The inner room was two floors above a man who weighed salt for a living, and the salt came up through the boards in the warm months and lived in everything in the cold ones, and the river went under the floor in a low continuous way that four years had not taught her to stop hearing. She kept her back to the wall and both feet flat, and there were three chairs at the table and a press against the far wall with a bar off its seat, and a day-book open to a ruled page with a line for the first of Greenmonth and nothing on it yet.

She had forty minutes before the light went off the upper window, and she had a notice to file, and the notice had to be in before the second, and after the second the province would begin the year in which everything this office held was a claim made under a finding that nothing survived.

The notice was four lines. It said that a document had been entered in the record of the county court on the thirtieth of Mudmonth; that it was a stub, or counterfoil, of a writ; that it was dated the fourteenth of Mudmonth in the eleventh year before the present; that it bore a seal the court had matched in its own hand to a second seal in its own file. It said nothing about what the writ had required. It said nothing about a bridge or a city or a fever or a man. It was a notice of the existence of a document, entered under a number a clerk had to touch, on a day that was still on the right side of a line that would move in nineteen hours.

Mara Vey wrote it out, dated it the first, signed as the keeper of the Bellweather Claims Archive, which is a title that meant she was employed and not that anybody had appointed her to anything, and read it three times to be certain that it was a notice and not an argument, because the day an office begins to make arguments it can be beaten, and an argument can be beaten, and a record can only be checked.

She sealed it. She entered it in the day-book, with the number, and with the hour, because a document entered without an hour is a document entered whenever the keeper got round to it.

And then she heard the outer room, which had a man in it again.

She had looked at him on the twenty-ninth and on the thirtieth, both times for the length of a cup of water, both times through a door she had not opened, because the rule is that a claimant is not refused and the rule is also that a claimant is not admitted to a filing room on the strength of being loud, and the second rule is the one nobody in a fen town understands.

On the twenty-ninth he had sat on the bench in the outer room with a waxed case on his knees and had told the salt merchant's boy that he would sit there until the keeper came down, and on the thirtieth he had done the same, and this afternoon, on the first, he had been in since the ninth hour.

She came out into the outer room with her pen in her hand and stopped, which was a thing she did on purpose.

He was fifty-one or thereabouts and had the shoulders of a man who has poled a flat-bottomed hull for thirty years, and a cord round his neck that had been a ferryman's cord and was now only a cord, and his hands were the hands of a man who works rope and who has not worked rope since the year of the fever. The waxed case was on his knees. It had been on his knees for three days and he had not once put it down, and there was a place worn in the case where his thumb went.

"Mister Rusk," Mara said. "Come in. I have been told you have been here three days and I have not come down, and I am sorry for that, and the reason is not that I thought you were a nuisance."

"You had your reasons."

"I had a rule and I applied it wrongly, which is different, and I would rather say so than let a man think I have a rule about him." She stood aside. "Sit at the table. Not that chair, that chair is where people sit when they are about to be refused."

"Which chair is the other one?"

"Anywhere else. Sit anywhere else and I will not move you."

He sat where he liked, which was the far side, with the case on the floor between his boots, and she sat down with her back to the wall and put the day-book where he could see that she had a day-book.

"Jos Rusk," he said. "Ferryman. Hushwater, until the water took the village, and since then I have done what there is in Merefen, which is odd jobs for the fishery and a hand at the sluice when the sluice wants a hand, and I have lived above a chandler's on the north end for eleven years and I am not here about money."

"What are you here about?"

"I'm here to file."

"You are here to file." She turned a clean sheet round. "Then we will do it properly, and I will ask you the questions I would have to ask, and I will not be kind about it, and I will be exact, and if I ask you something twice it is not because I doubt you."

"I know what an inquiry is. I have been on the wrong end of one."

"Then you know that the worst thing that can happen to a document is somebody who wants it to be a speech. So I am going to do the ungenerous thing first." Mara Vey put her pen down. "I am not going to use your daughter's name as a heading."

The room did what a room does. He had been composed for three days on a bench with a case on his knees and he was not composed now.

"She is eleven," he said.

"I know she is eleven. I have her age in front of me on a list that came up from a parish in a hand that a woman used to keep a school in, and I have read it, and if you want to know what I think of a list of children's names, I will tell you that I think it is the most dangerous document in this province." She turned the sheet so that he could see that she had not written anything on it. "If I head a claim *Bryn Rusk, 11, of Hushwater, lost on the last ferry out*, then a man in a provincial office reads four words and stops. He has a file with a number in it and a child at the top of it and a case that can be disposed of by being kind about a girl. It is the easiest possible shape for a piece of paper to take and it is the shape that loses. I have watched it done to other people. Your wife is a document and your daughter is a document and a document has to be *filed*, and a filing has a heading that a clerk can sort."

"My wife kept a book for nine years," Jos Rusk said. "She wrote down every crossing. Every one. In a hand that any man could come and check, and men did come and check, because that's what a crossing book is for, it's not for remembering, it's for checking. Nine years of it. And you want me to go up to that house —" he put his hand out towards the river and the fen and the whole drowned country "— and get the book, and give it to you, and you will file it under a number and not under her name."

"I want exactly that," said Mara. "Her name goes in the book where her hand is, on every line she wrote, in the order she wrote it, which is what a keeper of a book is. That is a better fate for a name than being a heading."

He looked at her for a while.

"You are not what the boy said you were," he said.

"What did the boy say?"

"He said there was a woman with a clerk's way of asking who wanted to see the book and not the grief. I told him that was what I wanted as well."

Mara wrote in the day-book. She wrote it in a line and no more, and the line was: *Claimant J. Rusk, ferryman, Hushwater, survivor. Brought a crossing book. Would not state a claim until asked what the office is for. Corrected my own delay in admitting him. He has been on the bench three days and I did not come down, and he is right about that.*

"Tell me about the book," she said.

He got it up onto the table, which took both hands, and undid the waxed strap, and inside it was a small book in boards with a linen spine, and it was a book of the kind that is made for a hand and not for a shelf: half a foolscap, boards gone soft at the corners, a linen tie, and a page of names.

"It is the ferry's book," he said. "Hushwater's crossing was a cooperative. Nine hands on the boats and a woman keeping the book and that was the whole of the constitution. Whatever went across went in the book, what it was, how much, and what came back, and the day of it. Not because anybody was sentimental. Because a cooperative that cannot be checked is a cooperative that gets blamed."

"And who checked it?"

"Everybody. The toll at the abbey. The miller at Cobbett. A man from the assize once, in the year before, who wanted to know whether a certain load of oak had gone over and had gone through the whole book with his thumb and found it in a fortnight, because that is what a hand you can check buys you." He opened it on the table and squared it. "It is a proof of nothing except that a boat went across. That is the whole of what a crossing book is. A hand that is regular proves a boat went across, on a day, in a direction, with a cargo. It does not prove why a village was not there in the morning. It cannot. It is a book about boats."

"That is exactly right, and I want you to know that I agree with you, because you have just saved me from making an argument I would have lost."

"I don't need saving. I need the eight of Hushwater in a record that a man in a provincial town cannot put his hand on."

"Then say the rest," said Mara. "Say what you think caused it, and say it in your own words, and I am going to write it down exactly, and I will not correct you while you say it."

He said it in his own words, and it took him four minutes, and it was a good story, and it was better than the Crown's.

The water had carried it. That was the heart of it. There had been a fever in the low quarter and the low quarter was downstream of nothing, it was the lowest ground in the vale, and a river fever comes up out of the ground and into the low ground first, and the low ground in the vale was a drowned channel with a village on it. Hushwater went to the water because Hushwater was on the water. Forty of them in four days, in the wrong season, with the wrong colour behind their ears, and the fever that came up through a marsh in Mudmonth does not care what a Crown's man writes about a bridge. And the Crown — this was the part he had come to say, and he said it with a great deal of control — the Crown had taken the finding and made it a bridge. They had a fever and they needed an explanation with a man in it, and there was a boy of twenty-one on a bridge in the dark, and there was a bell, and so the finding says fever, and the finding says the boy, and everybody in the province is satisfied to nod at that and go home, and the rest of it — the eight, and the women, and the children, and the man who carried the body of his brother out of a channel in the dark for nine days to the abbey and got the name of the fever put in the parish book and nothing else — the rest of it they buried. They buried it, on purpose, with a clerk's date on it.

"Say that last part again," said Mara.

"They buried it."

"That is the phrase I want," she said. "And I am going to give you the phrase back in a worse condition than you gave it to me, and I am going to tell you why, and you are not going to like it."

He had been leaning forward. He leaned back.

"Go on."

"Your cause is in dispute from this hour." She turned a fresh sheet round and ruled a line down the middle of it. "I am going to enter your claim, and I am going to give it a number in this office's book, and I am going to put the claim in your wife's name as the keeper of a book and your daughter's name as a name in the second column of a page and not as a heading, and I am going to enter the book as an exhibit with a chain of custody from your hands to this table, and it will be a document in a county court in nine days when the magistrate sits again, and it will be a document nobody has to be persuaded about, because a hand that other men checked for nine years is not an argument, it is a fact that weighs nine ounces and cannot be leaned on."

"And the cause?"

"The cause is that the record does not show the cause."

"That is not an answer."

"It is the only answer that is true, and I am going to write it in that form, and here is what it will cost you, because a keeper who takes a claim and does not tell a man what it is worth is worse than a clerk who refuses him." She put the pen down. "A claim with a wrong cause is stronger than a claim with no cause, and a claim with a wrong cause is stronger still in the twelve months after tomorrow. Yours says the water carried it. That is a claim, and it can be argued, and it can be tested, and every office that reads it must produce a document in answer or admit that it has none. If I file *the water carried it*, I am handing you a year of a Crown that has to disprove you. And the Crown will disprove you, because your water is a river and a river is a thing that can be tested and a Crown has men whose whole work is testing rivers, and in a year they will have a page that says the marsh channel was not below the water table in Mudmonth of YR 301, and that page will be a fact, and your claim will go down on a fact, and your wife's book will be on file and will not be able to carry the weight of a page about water."

"And if you write *the record does not show the cause*?"

"Then in a year, or in nine years, somebody produces a document that shows the cause, and your claim is the claim that was waiting for it, and it does not fall over, because nothing in it was ever wrong." She said it flatly. "I am going to make your claim weaker on purpose, Mr Rusk, in the one room in this province where nobody is watching, and I am doing it because a claim with a disputed cause can be *mended* and a claim with a right cause in it cannot be proved at all. I have watched men die of that arithmetic. Do you understand the difference between a claim that is weak and true and a claim that is strong and untestable? Most men in your position do not, and I have to know tonight, because I am going to be asked to hold this file for a year and there will be a day when I offer somebody in it something better than the truth and I want to know now whether you will take it."

He was quiet for a while. The river went under the floor.

"You think the fever was the coat," he said.

"I think a fever was a real thing," said Mara, "and I think a real thing was made to carry a false one, and I have on the shelf behind you thirty-one days of a weighmaster's brine weights with the arithmetic intact, and a weighmaster cannot be lied to about quantity, and that document says how much salt went out of Saltbrim in the first week of Highsun in the year before, and the finding says nothing survived, and both of those are in this building and only one of them is in a court."

"What are you saying to me?"

"I am saying that when the cause is proved, your village will be proved, and it will be proved by a ledger and a crossing book and a schoolmistress's list, and not by a theory about water." She drew the line down the middle of the sheet. "I am putting the cause in dispute. In writing. In your hand as well as mine, and I will explain the word *dispute* to you before you sign it, because a man who signs a word he does not own has made the same mistake twice."

He read it. He read it twice, which she had not expected of a ferryman from a drowned village, and then he said, without any particular heat:

"There was a name for the men who did it."

"Which name?"

"Only the one. There has never been a second. They came in on a Tuesday and they were in the vale four days and they were gone, and the men who went with them had a name, and it was not *army* and it was not *office* and it was not a word the finding uses. There was a name for them and people in the vale said it out loud for years and your county paper would not print it." He looked at her levelly. "There is a Culling Order, Miss Vey. There has been one since before I was your age, and it has a word for what it does and it has never once been in a finding, and that is not because there is no word for it. It is because a word in a finding is a thing somebody can be cross-examined about."

Mara Vey wrote one line in the day-book, and made a mark in the left margin of it, which was the office's mark for a phrase to be held back from a file until somebody had looked at the consequence of it, and she had made that mark eleven times in four years.

"Then I will hold the word," she said, "and not the order, and I will tell you why, and it is the same reason I would not put your daughter's name at the top of a page. The word is the only thing I have ever been able to prove in this office. I have four years of claims, salvages, hearsay, and letters, and the word *Culling Order* is the only item in the building that nobody in the Crown has ever put in writing. If it goes into a file it goes into a Crown file in nine days, and a word in a Crown file is a word somebody can be cross-examined about, and cross-examined about by men who are not going to be sorry. It is a good word and it is the best thing I hold, and I am going to keep it in a room where a magistrate can see it and a clerk cannot, until a man in a room with a seal on the table asks me for it in front of somebody. Will that do?"

He looked at her for long enough that she understood she had just been weighed, and that she had come out where she wanted to be.

"Aye," he said. "That'll do."

They did the rest of it the way a claim is done. Name, condition, trade, eleven years in Merefen. Where the book had been kept and in what and by whose hand, and how a man from the assize had checked it in a year nobody could now name, and which of the hands in the book were hers and which were other people's, and the last page. She asked about the last page because a keeper of records is frightened of one page and only one page, and the last page is the one that has been read a thousand times by a family and never once by a clerk.

The last page had eleven lines on it and the eleventh was the ferry out, on the day, with a cargo of forty sacks and eleven persons, and it was in his wife's hand, and the hand was the same hand as the nine years above it, and the ink was the same ink.

The entry under the eleventh line was in a different hand and in different ink and said: *Persons, 11. Not landed, drowned channel.* And then the page was finished.

Nobody said anything for a while.

"That is the only line in that book that is not hers," said Jos Rusk.

"Yes."

"And you are going to file it as it is, with a different hand on it."

"I am going to file it as it is. I am not going to have somebody say that she wrote it, and I am not going to have somebody say that she was mistaken about the day." She squared the book with both hands, the way you square a thing you are about to be responsible for. "A hand that changes is the most valuable kind of hand in a document. Mine does it and it is a fault. Hers does it and it is the truest thing in eleven years of a county's records, and there is exactly one man in this province who can say that the eleventh line is not hers, and I am going to have it written in his hand on the wrapper, and I am going to pay him for a day of his time, because it is his day and not mine."

He nodded, once, and did not say anything, and that was the moment in which Mara Vey understood that she had just made a man who would come back, and that he would keep coming, and that he would be the first of a kind of person she had in this room: not a person to be got rid of, and not a person to be managed, but a person with a case, who was owed a hearing, and who was going to be difficult for as long as the case was open.

She gave him the number.

It was four digits and it went into a book in a press in a room over a salt merchant, and it was the second number of that kind she had issued in four years, and she wrote it on the back of his hand in ink because a man who has a number on his hand can tell two people he has one.

"You will lose on this," he said, when she had done it.

"I expect so. I have made an enemy of your cause in a room in which I was the only person with a pen, and I did it because a cause that is wrong can be proved wrong and a cause that is right cannot be proved at all, and I would rather lose you than lose the argument for you." She capped the ink. "You may come back on the eighth. I will have the wrapper done and the copy of the exhibit and I will want your hand to it, and I will want to know the name of the man from the assize, if he is dead or alive, because a book that has been checked by a Crown officer is a book with a second witness in it and I have not got that anywhere else in this building."

He got the case up under his arm, and then stopped, with his hand on the strap, and stood there in a filing room above a salt merchant with his back to the wall and his hat in his hand and did not go.

He was not a man who was going to make a speech. That was the thing she had not been ready for, and she had been ready for a speech, and she had her hands flat on the table for it.

"Miss Vey," he said, very quietly. "Has anybody told the ferrymen?"

"The ferrymen here?"

"The ferrymen at Hushwater. There are none, and that is what I have been trying to say to four offices in eleven years and I have not said it in those words." He did not raise his voice and he did not need to; the room was eight feet across. "There is a cooperative of nine hands on that crossing and there are five of them living, and they are not in this county's book, and they do not know that anybody has come looking, and if the office that comes looking is the office that takes the water out of the channel, then a notice would be the worst thing that has ever happened to those five people. So I am asking you a plain question. Has anybody told the ferrymen?"

Mara Vey sat with her back to the wall and her feet flat and the day-book open in front of her and the number four digits in ink on the back of a ferryman's hand, and she thought about the eleven days since the thirtieth, and about a document nobody had been summoned to see, and about a room above a salt merchant where a bell was going to be described on Thursday by a man who had held it on a bar, and about a claim she had just made weaker on purpose in front of the man it was for.

"No," she said. "Nobody has told them, and nobody has told me how to reach them either, which is the first honest answer I have and it is a bad one."

"Then I will find out how," said Jos Rusk, "and I will not use your letter, and I will not use the office, and if I find out I will think a long time about whether telling them is the same as using them." He put his hat on. "Nine shillings the pair, ready in a fortnight. Steppe glass. I have seen the slip on the post. I have stood and read it like a man reading his own sentence."

Mara Vey looked up.

"That is not this office's business."

"No," he said. "It is nobody's business, and there is a man in Merefen who has a mark and a quarter and eleven days of wages, and I have been in your outer room three days and I have watched the ferrymen's boy bring your water up those stairs, and there is not one thing in that room that says he is short." He shifted the case under his arm. "I will come back on the eighth for the wrapper. And I will think about the ferrymen, and I will think about it properly, and I will not go up there and tell five men that a clerk upstairs is looking for them until I know what a clerk upstairs would do with the answer."

He went down the half-landing stair and out into a street that smelled of salt and river and cold mud, and Mara Vey sat in the outer room for a while with the day-book open in front of her, and then she went in and wrote the day out in full, because that is what a day-book is for.

She wrote the claim number. She wrote the dispute in the form it had been signed, in both hands, with the hour.

And at the bottom of the page, in the same ink, she wrote the other thing, because she had been trained to write down the shape of a silence while it was still fresh:

*I have made this man's claim weaker in a room with no witnesses but him and me, and I did it in front of him, and he asked me for a boat and I gave him a number. I want it on this page that I did not do it to be kind to him and I did not do it because the water is wrong. The water may be right. Nobody can show that it is right, and the difference between those two sentences is the whole of my trade, and I have just spent a morning explaining my own job to a man who is owed it. He will not be thanked for it. He will be here in nine days and he will not be grateful, and that is the correct arrangement and it is the first time in four years I have had one.*

*And: nobody has told the ferrymen. He is going to find out how to tell them. I do not know what he will do and I have no right to a vote and no instrument of any kind in this office that would let me have one.*
