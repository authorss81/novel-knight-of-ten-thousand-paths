# Chapter 0008

He came back on the sill.

There was no light, no folding, no weather. There was a cut stone abutment under a grey sky in a fen town with the sun two hands high behind a bank of cloud, and the smell of a river he had not smelled for six days, and his boots on the repacking he had laid himself in the autumn and again in the last week before he left.

Then the fever came up out of his chest and put him on both hands on the wet stone, and it was not the flood that did that. The flood was over. The sky was hard and clear and cold behind the cloud, the reeds along the Reed Line were standing up out of the mud in their straight cut, and nobody in Merefen had drowned since the seventeenth, which meant that for eight days the bell on the Old Toll abutment had hung there with nothing to say for itself and no hand anywhere near it.

He stayed on all fours for a while, counting, which is what you do. Nine breaths, of which four were bad.

The cradle was empty.

That was the second thing, and it was the thing he looked at longest. The iron cradle was still there, welded to the abutment by a fitter who was now eleven years dead, and under it the stone was swept clean, and in the moss along the third course of the abutment — the moss that has no business being there because this is a bridge and the water is under it — there was a clean rectangle, nine inches by five, paler than the stone round it, where nine days of green bronze had stood and where nothing had stood since.

The cut looked like a decision. That is what made him stay on the stone. Moss does not leave a rectangle. Men leave rectangles, and the men who take a thing off a bridge come with a cart and a rope and a written paper, and they put it down where it stood and they do not sweep under it, and this one had been swept. Eight days it had been gone. He had been asleep in a room two shillings a month above a cooper for a week and part of a day of that.

The watch had been on the bridge for some time. Four of them, by the fire-box off the second bay, in good coats, with the tell-tale stillness of men who have been posted somewhere for a length of time and are watching a man come up the steps.

The market was on the wharf road behind them, and it was there because the fourth-day market on the twenty-fourth had been lost with the road and put off to the first working day the town could hold it, which was a Tuesday, and which was thin, and half the sellers of the vale had not come back a second time after a wet Tuesday. Two stalls down there had already broken off and gone home with their boards. A barge lay off downstream with a full hold and no berth, because the wharf road is the first four hundred yards of the bridge road, and the bridge road had been shut since the morning after the storm.

Behind them, at the abutment, his own bar lay on the wet setts, where he had put it down in the fourth second of being back, before he had found out which day it was.

Aren Kest got himself up the last of the way on the strength of the stone and stood with his hand on the cradle iron, and a voice from the fire-box said:

"Sergeant. That's him."

A man came across the deck. He was fifty, in a watch coat with the town's badge on it and a sealed paper in an oilskin under his arm, and he stopped eight feet off, which was a professional distance, and looked at Aren from the boots up.

"You're Kest."

"Yes."

"Captain Rell. I hold a paper here that names you." He said it without relish. "You're wet through and you're grey, and I want you to sit down before you go over, and if you go over on my bridge I'll have to write two things instead of one, and I have had a very long nine days."

Nine days. Aren put his hand out and found the rail and held it.

The road crews' saying goes three days out and three days back, and the only other warning he had got was a voice in a place behind his eyes that had said one sentence and had not mentioned days at all, and he had gone anyway, and here it was, and it was nine.

"Mr Kest," said Captain Rell. "Do you know what this is?"

He took the oilskin out and did not open it, and held it so that the seal showed: a disc of grey wax the size of a crown piece, with a device on it that Aren knew, because he had carried that device's papers for six years of his life.

"That is the marshal's office," he said.

"It is the marshal's office's emergency office, and the marshal's office seals its emergency papers with a device you would know, and I have that paper because a courier brought it to my door on the evening of the eighteenth and gave it into my hand and told me to keep it and not to open it, which is not an instruction I was ever issued, and I have thought about that for nine days." Rell turned the paper over. "It is dated the sixteenth."

"The sixteenth," Aren said. "Delivered on the eighteenth. Before the storm. Before the nineteenth."

"Before the storm," Rell agreed. "Before the anniversary, before any weather, and before anything at all had happened in this town except a bond and a chalk mark. It names this bell as an object taken out of the river after the fever at Bellweather and thereafter hung where men walk, and it directs this watch to take it into custody for examination by the office, together with—" He stopped, and read from the paper, and did not read it as a man reading a document he had already read. "*Together with any person claiming the hinge of it, who is described herein as the road-keeper of Merefen and the holder of the Bellweather hinge, and who is summoned to attend at the provincial town to give account of the keeping.*"

He lowered it.

"Mr Kest, I have held that paper for nine days and I have not sent it on and I have not burned it, and I want you to know that both of those were decisions and not omissions, and I will answer for them to anybody who asks, including the marshal's office, including my own watch, including a magistrate." He put the paper back under his arm. "Now. Why are you wet, and where have you been, and why is there a clean place on that abutment where the bell stood?"

"A keeper," Aren said. "What is a keeper?"

Rell looked at him.

"That," he said, "is the first sensible question anybody has asked about that word in this town in nine days, and I have asked it four times myself and I have three answers and no two of them agree. A crown seal, a hand, and a bell. The road-keeper of Merefen, it says, and the holder of the Bellweather hinge, and *summoned to attend at the provincial town to give account of the keeping.*" He shifted the oilskin. "I read that paper the night it came to me, and I could not make a word of it, and I have not been able to make a word of it since, and I have spent nine days assuming it was a way of writing my own post in a document that has to be filed in a provincial office. Can you make a word of it?"

"No."

"Neither can the province, or they would not have sent it to a watch captain in a fen town with a string of tokens and a bad road." He turned the paper over and looked at the seal, and did not open it. "Here is the whole of it, and then I want your answer in front of these people, because I have read it out to the trust's clerk and to my own sergeant and to one man at the market, and every one of us has understood a different thing by it, and that will not do for a paper with a seal on it. Why are you wet, and where have you been, and why is there a clean place on that abutment where the bell stood?"

Aren Kest stood on the abutment of a bridge in his own town with a fever starting in his chest and forty people on the deck of a market bridge looking at the watch and him.

He could have said: I was taken ill, in the low quarter, near the weir, and a cart brought me back. He had a cough ready, which a man does not have to invent, because he was about to need it.

What he said was this.

"I have been at Bellweather. The bridge is standing. The arch is intact and it carries carts. I stood on the crown of it on the seventh hour of the morning and the water was under the third arch and the laundry goes over the middle span every day, and the bell founder's yard has your bell on two trundles under a tarred cloth with a cracked lip, and there is a bench of bridgewrights that refused a writ on the deck at the second hour of the night on the nineteenth of Mudmonth, YR 301, and the bell cracked on the third stroke of it."

The market had gone quiet in the middle of a sentence. That is what he noticed. Not that people were leaving. They stopped, the way a crowd stops, all at once, because a market crowd is a set of people who are listening to each other and it takes one person out of forty to stop and the other thirty-nine go inside a second.

"I will say it again," Aren said, "for the man with the book, because I am told there is one, and because I would rather say it twice in the right place than once in the wrong one."

The watch-sergeant on the fire-box had a book out already. He had it out before Aren started, because a watch-sergeant's whole training is knowing which words are paper.

Aren said it again, word for word, in the same order, standing up, with the bar on the setts behind him, and this time he said who he was.

"Aren Kest. I was twenty-one years old and I was a captain in the Lantern Guard by brevet, and on the nineteenth of Mudmonth, YR 301, I was sent to hold the Alder Vale crossing with a partial order and forty men, and I did not reach the inner city, and that city and the seven villages round it were taken out of the world that night, and the crown's figure for it is four thousand, and that figure is the city alone and has always been the city alone, and I have not once seen the villages counted." He got his breath. "I said the word *fever* about it in a room in Embermonth in that year, and in three years of debriefs after it, and I let it stand because it was the account I was given and I had nothing to put in its place. The crown's finding says I lost a bridge. I did lose a bridge. It does not say that a bridge is why the city is gone, and I have known that for eleven years, and I have not been in a position to say it until this morning."

He put his hand in the breast of his coat.

They said later that the fever turned him at the end of that, and it did, and it turned him in the middle of the second telling, and he had to put one hand on the rail and wait for the deck to stop moving and then finish the sentence, because he had made a point about saying a thing twice in the right place and he was not going to break it in the middle.

He took out the paper.

It was folded in four and it had been folded in four for six days, in a different city, in a different coat, in a room over a toll house with an oath-stone in the floor. Grey, thin, official, with a seal on the stub that had gone the colour of a duck's egg.

"Written and sealed and dated the fourteenth of Mudmonth, YR 301, and sent from the Crown to the north gate of this city to require the severance of this crossing and the removal of the persons found within the sealed limit." He held it up. "This is the stub. The half of it that goes back to the office. It is called a counterfoil and it carries the seal and it does not carry a name, and it has been in a room in Bellweather since the year it was sent. The other half — the one with the name on it — is in a register, and the register is in the hands of the toll houses, and I have not seen it and I cannot get it, and the toll houses are not the guild and are not my friends and are the only people in that city who have offered to give me anything at all."

A voice from the market crowd, a big voice belonging to a carter: "Kest. Kest, what in the drowned's name—"

"Sergeant," said Captain Rell.

"Captain," said the watch-sergeant, "he has said a great deal and I have written it down, and I will write it all down, and it goes in the book tonight, and the book goes up on Thursday whether anybody likes it or not."

"I know what goes in the book," said Rell. He was looking at the paper, not at Aren. He said, in a different voice: "Mr Kest, I want to tell you exactly where I stand, because you have just said something in front of forty witnesses on a market morning and forty witnesses on a market morning is a thing that cannot be got back, and I would rather you knew I have heard it and that I do not believe a word of it and that I have written it down anyway."

"That is a strange position for a watch captain."

"It is the only honest position there is. I do not believe a word of it. A fever in the low quarter, the crown's finding, a city that a man walked into off a bridge nine days gone and stood on the crown of in front of forty people." He had the paper back under his arm. "I have read the finding eleven times. I was a boy when it happened. And here is the part I will write down if you let me, and you can refuse, and I would think less of a man who let me write it.

"I think the bell brought it. That is what I have believed since I was fourteen, and it is not foolishness, it is a thing I can show you: a river fever came down out of that city and it went through the low quarters and up the coast road, and the crown's people who lifted the bell out of the water a year afterwards got a fever in the lifting, one of the six, and it is the only fever in this province in forty years with a date on it that anybody can point at.

"That is why this paper exists. There is an office in the provincial town that has been waiting nine years for this bell to be handled by a person, and the office is not interested in a bell. It is interested in the hand that can make a bell do something, and it thinks that hand is attached to a crack in a piece of municipal salvage, and it is going to look for it. If that is foolishness, then I am a fool, and I will put my name to the foolishness, and I will also put my name to the fact that you went off this bridge nine days ago and came back nine days later with a roadmark in your hand and a fever in your chest, and that a man who can do that is exactly the man that paper is about, and if I do not take the bell I shall be asked why I did not take the bell."

He stopped, and looked at the empty cradle, and at the clean rectangle in the moss.

"I have taken the bell," Rell said. "It went off the abutment at first light on the nineteenth, the morning after the storm, by a crew of four with a cart, and it is in the storehouse by the fish weir, under seal, and the seal is the crown's, because the office sent a seal with the paper. That is the whole of what I have done wrong and I will not pretend otherwise to a magistrate, and I have done it on my own authority with a paper that was sealed before anything happened, and I would do it again tomorrow, and I would be sorry to the four thousand afterwards."

The fever made him sit down on the sill, in the end, and put his back against the cradle iron, and the four of them watched the watch and the watch captain did not tell him to move.

Aren Kest got out his hand and looked at it. The roadmark was there, black, healed into the crease, running under the skin and past the edge of it, and where it crossed the heel of his palm the black had a grain to it in one direction only, exactly as it had had it in a bar's length of time on the far side of a seam in the rain, and it had not washed off in nine days and a river and a week, and when he turned his palm up the flesh was unbroken across it and would be unbroken in a year.

He thought about the song.

It was not a song he could sing. It was a thing a woman had sung near water, in a room with a window open, and he had been very small, and out of a long life he had kept four lines of it and one tune and a woman's voice, and the tune was in him the way a name you do not use is in you. He hummed it under his breath, on a bridge, in the cold, in front of a watch captain.

The first line went where it went. The second line went where it went. The third line — the one that had always come down onto a note, always the same note, the low one that a boatman would sing and that he had sung since he was seven years old — went somewhere in the middle and would not land.

Not gone. He could find the words and he could find the shape of the melody and he could get most of the way down the phrase, and there was a place where the tune used to arrive, and the tune arrived short of it. He had noticed it once before, on the other side of a thing that had happened to him, in a place with no name yet, standing up, with his hand on a bridge that was not his, and he had been in a hurry there and had put it aside as something somebody had taken.

He had paid for the door, and the door had taken the note.

Somewhere under his hand, in the cut stone of the sill, the packing was wrong. He could feel it through the crust of fever the way he could feel a bad joint in a deck: the three lengths he had repacked in the autumn and again in the last week had been lifted, all three, and set back down out of line, and the lime bed had been blown out of the joints in a line across the stone from the parapet to the parapet. There was a mark in the middle of it. It was not a mark of tools. It was two lines crossed, and the stone inside the crossing was pale and dry and warm in a way that the air was not, and it was the size of two hands, and it was the only warm place on the whole bridge.

He put his palm on it, because it was the sill, because it was his, and because the hand he put on it had a road in it.

And a voice, in the place behind his eyes that he was going to spend a good part of a year learning not to argue with, said one thing, and it was true, and it told him nothing about the only question he had:

*What you carry back will not remain only yours.*

He took his hand off the stone.

"Well?" said Captain Rell, after a while, not unkindly. "Mr Kest. What would you have me write at the top of this?"

Aren Kest thought about the paper in his coat, and about the forty people on a market morning, and about a bench of bridgewrights in a city four hundred feet over a river that had a mould in it the shape of a cross, and about a woman at a salt loft who had asked him for a promise with no date on it and had got it, and about the woman who had said the answer to a question he had not asked her, which was that he had already chosen a night.

"Write what I said," he said. "Write it as I said it, twice, and write that I said it twice."

"And the roadmark?"

"Write that a roadmark is not a road. Write that I do not know what it is, and that if you want it looked at, I would rather it was looked at by a man who is not a keeper of anything and has not been told by a piece of paper that he is."

Captain Rell looked at the paper, and then at the cradle, and then at the man sitting on a sill in front of him, and said, "There is no such office in this town. I have looked. There is a road warden appointed by the trust, a toll-holder, a watch, and a bell that a man off the north road put on a bridge on his own authority in Mudmonth. I could not find a keeper in eleven years of registers." He put the oilskin back under his arm. "That is the second thing I am going to write down, and I am going to write it exactly as you said it, which is that the office exists and that neither of us can find the authority for it, and that a man who has never been named in any instrument I have seen has been named in one."

He turned to go up the steps, because the market was breaking up on the wharf road and there were forty cart-loads of a bad morning's work to get into the town, and he stopped with one foot on the bottom step and looked back down at a man on a sill.

"You'll not get up these steps," Rell said. "I have watched four men with the low fever try and two of them went down on the stones. Stay where you are. I'll send the sergeant for a cart and a blanket, and I'll send for the town's physician, which will cost you a shilling you have not got, and I'll put it on the watch account, and you can argue with me about that later when you can stand up."

"I can argue about it now."

"You can argue about it after," said Captain Rell, and went up the steps.
