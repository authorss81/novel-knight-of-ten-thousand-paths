# Chapter 0042

The marking had been done at the fourth hour and he came up out of the steps at the sixth, and the first thing he did was not to go down to it.

He went to the hall.

That is not what a bridgewright does, and he noticed himself not doing it about two hours later, standing in a room with a stone in the floor, and he wrote it down that night as the first line of a day that turned out to be the best day he had in that city and the one he spent the least time on.

The guild hall is over the arch. The arch runs under the floor of it. The bench sits around a stone in the floor of the room that is the crown of the third arch, and the room has been the hall of this bench for four generations, and there are two people in it at six in the morning who are not the bench and who are not about to be.

One of them was a sett-cutter from the fourth camp with a thumb broken in the fourth year and a fifth drying floor, and she was eating something out of a paper twist and did not get up. The other was the bench-clerk, who had a book open on the table and who had been there since the fourth hour, and who put his pen down flat on the page when Aren Kest came through the door, which is a thing a clerk does when a thing has landed on him that he was not expecting.

"Eight," the bench-clerk said.

"I heard."

"You did not hear it. You have not been in this city since the spring and I have been sitting here with a book for two hours, and the whole of my life for the last six weeks has been a page with a number on it and nobody to read it to." He turned the book round and put his finger on the line. "Read it."

It was a ruled page, and the page was the marking page, and the marking page had a heading and four columns, and the columns were *day*, *hour*, *where*, and *number*, and the rule under the heading was the same rule the bench-clerk had put under everything for nineteen years.

*Twenty-sixth of Longlight, YR 312. Fourth hour, at the lowest water. Second pier, outer ring, river side. Number: eight. Two men and a clerk. Read back at the fifth hour by the man with the mallet and the number was agreed.*

"Eight," said Aren Kest.

"Eight, and it is the twenty-fourth of these, and I am going to tell you what the whole of the book says, because I have it here and you have not, and because a man has come four hundred miles to look at a number and the number is thirteen lines long."

He turned back eleven leaves and then twenty-two and then eleven more, and the leaves came apart in his hands at three different corners, which they do after nineteen years, and he read the figures out loud in a room with a stone in the floor, in the voice a man uses for a price list and not for a discovery.

"Twenty-second of Rainmonth, YR 306, eight and one tenth. Twenty-sixth of Longlight, YR 306, eight and three tenths. Twenty-fourth of Rainmonth, YR 307, eight and one tenth. Twenty-sixth of Longlight, YR 307, eight and three tenths. Twenty-second of Rainmonth, YR 308, eight and two tenths. Twenty-fourth of Longlight, YR 308, eight and two tenths. Twenty-fourth of Rainmonth, YR 309, eight and two tenths. Twenty-sixth of Longlight, YR 309, eight and two tenths. Twenty-second of Rainmonth, YR 310, eight and one tenth. Twenty-fourth of Longlight, YR 310, eight and one tenth. Twenty-fourth of Rainmonth, YR 311, eight and one tenth. Twenty-sixth of Longlight, YR 311, eight and one tenth. And the one before this morning: the twenty-second of Rainmonth, YR 312, eight."

The room was very quiet. Somewhere underneath the floor a cart went over the crown of the arch and the sound came up through the stone.

"You read that wrong on purpose," said the bench-clerk.

"I read it in the order it is in, which is the order it was entered, and every second entry in that book is a marking in the warmth and every other one is a marking in the cold, and the cold reading is always the bigger one, and it has always been the bigger one, and nobody has ever once put two of them side by side, because there is no column for it."

"There is no column for it," said the bench-clerk. "That is what I have been saying to this bench for six years and then I stopped saying it. I said it in the winter of the year the abbey wall came down. I said it the year my father died. I have said it in front of four different men in that chair and every one of them has looked at me as though I had asked them to weigh the water."

He put the book down and put both hands flat on it, one on each side, the way a man does with a thing he has kept.

"I have never compared them," he said. "I want that said in a room. Not because it is a confession. Because it is the reason that page is worth reading and nobody has read it, and a thing that is worth reading and has not been read is not a fault, it is a piece of luck, and it happened to come to me on the morning a man came up the north steps with a bar on his shoulder."

He squared the book up, turned the leaves back the other way, and put his finger on the head of the first column.

"The mark is taken twice a year and I will tell you why the two are different, because I have looked at it and you have not, and this is the trade's part and the trade's part is the only part I am entitled to in a room I do not own. **A ring stone opens in the cold and shuts in the warmth.** That is not a defect. That is what stone does when it has stood in a river for a hundred and forty years, and it is why your bench marks it at the two low waters and not at the two high ones. Your Longlight marking is the cold one, because Longlight is the end of the year in this valley and the ring stands open in the cold. Your Rainmonth marking is the warm one, because Rainmonth is the other low water and it is mild here, which I did not expect and learned in about a minute.

"So there have always been two numbers for that ring. Every Longlight in that book is bigger than every Rainmonth in it, and the gap between the two is two tenths in most years and three in one of them, and **the gap is the winter.**"

---

He said what it was, and he said it in the fewest words he could find.

"Your cold readings are the six Longlight entries and your warm readings are the seven Rainmonth entries, and the arithmetic of both sets is short. The cold set has gone from eight and three tenths to eight, which is three tenths of an inch in eighteen years. The warm set has gone from eight and one tenth to eight, which is an eighth of an inch. **Three tenths in eighteen years is a fifth of an inch in three years, and a fifth of an inch in three years is not a crack going anywhere. It is stone being stone, and on the whole of the twenty-four numbers on that page it is the only thing anybody can say.**

"And the whole of that page is between eight and eight and three tenths. The largest number ever written in that book in nineteen years is eight and three tenths, and it is in the hand of a man who died in YR 308.

"So the ring is standing still. It has been standing still for longer than this bench has been marking it, and it is very probably standing still now, and the reason nobody in this city has ever been able to say so out loud is that a bench's book is looked in for a date and not for a series, and I have sat at this table three times in nineteen days and this is the first time that page has been read as a page."

The man with the mallet had come in while he was saying it and stood at the end of the table with his hands behind his back and did not sit down.

"Eight," he said. "Say that again and say it slower."

"Eight, at the fourth hour, at the lowest water, in the cold. And eight and one tenth in the cold the year before. And eight and one tenth in the warmth the year before that, and eight and two tenths in the warmth the year before that. **The whole of the twenty-four is between eight and eight and three tenths, and the difference between the two sets in any year is two tenths, and the two tenths is the winter.**"

"Six years," said the bench-clerk. "We have a figure for it after six years, and the figure is the winter, and I have a bench order on that stone and I have obeyed it since the year my boy was born."

"Then your order is right and your book is wrong," said Aren Kest, "and the second one is nobody's fault and the first one is nobody's doing, and a bench that has kept a mark for six years without once reading the two sets against each other is not a bench that has been careless. **It is a bench that never had a reason to.** There has been no second pier in this city in eleven years. The marking is the oldest thing in the hall and it is the only thing in the hall that nobody has ever had to make a decision with."

And then, because he was the best man in the room and the room knew it, and because a man who is the best man in a room has about four minutes in which anything he says is worth twice what it will be worth later, he said the other thing, and he said it before anybody had thought to ask.

"There is a number in my country," he said. "In the north. Eleven. And somebody there has been told that the same mark was eight at the last marking and is eleven now, and a man has written it down in a fair hand in a filing room in a town called Merefen, with the hour on it, and five limits."

The bench-clerk's pen came off the page.

"Eight to eleven," he said.

"Three inches. In a year."

"That is not a mark on a ring. **That is a foundation.** I have seen one go three inches in a year and I have seen the year after that. A ring on a second pier does not do three inches in a year, because the stone is sound and it is four hundred years old and it is not carrying anything it was not carrying in the year it was built. **Three inches is not a ring. Three inches is a man on a tower with a sight-line and a bad rule, or a man in a tap-room with a bad memory, or a different bridge.**"

"You have just said it in a room and you have said it about a mark in my country that you have never seen."

"I have said it about a number in a book that any man could have written, and the number in the book is eight, and it was eight at the last marking, and it is the largest number this bench has ever written that is not eight and three tenths." The clerk wrote a line and read it back. "Entered, on the word of a stranger, that the stranger has been told in his country that a mark of this kind went from eight to eleven in a year, and that this bench has not seen that mark and has entered the saying and has not entered an opinion, and that this bench's own number this day is eight and was eight at the last marking."

"That is the first time in nineteen years that anyone in this hall has entered an opinion about a thing that was not in this hall," said the sett-cutter, without getting up. "It is a strange afternoon."

---

He did the scour at the fourth hour of the next morning, in the water, up to the thigh, with the lamp strapped to his wrist and the bar over the lip.

He did it in an hour. That is the part nobody in this city would believe afterwards, including the man he told it to, and it is the whole of what his trade is: the second pier stands on the river side of the great arch on a shelf of rock, and the shelf has been in a book in this hall since before the flood of the second year in a hand nobody living has, in one line about a flood, eleven paces long at the top and about four at the water.

And at the lowest water of the year, with the river out of the way, there is a hole on the river side of that shelf.

Not a crack. A **scour**: a bowl about four feet across and about two feet deep at the deepest, with its lip soft and undercut on the north side and the gravel out of it gone down to bed, and eleven feet of it under the pier's footing that nobody had seen in eleven years, because in eleven years nobody had been in the water at the lowest water of the year.

The honest repair is a new cutwater. A new nose, coursed, carried down to bed. And the bed has to be cleared, and the old foot cut off, and a new foot set under a standing pier, and a standing pier is a thing you do not do to an arch in a season.

**Nine months of this bench's whole capacity.** Stone, iron, wages, and a season in which the fourth camp cannot lay the gate bridge approach, against a debt on the arch pledged to the ninth of a grandson, and the man who pledged it knew what he was doing.

He wrote one line that night and it is the only line in the notebook for the twenty-sixth of Longlight and it is in his own flat hand:

*Scour, not crack. Four feet across, two deep, eleven feet under the foot, new since the flood of the second year and found at the lowest water of the twenty-sixth. The fix is a cutwater and the cutwater is nine months and the nine months are against a pledge to the ninth of a grandson. I have read this in one hour standing in the river. I have not said it in a room yet and I am not going to until somebody asks me what it is, in trade's words, in a room with a clerk in it.*

---

The bench sat at the ninth hour with about two hundred people at the back, which is what four thousand does on a market day in a city with one arch.

The man with the mallet asked him what it was.

Aren Kest stood up in a hall where he had said, on the eighteenth day of his first time here, the truest thing he had said in eleven years, and he said it in the trade's terms, in the fewest words he could find, in the order a man says a thing standing at a table with a man taking it down.

It took two minutes. It said what a scour hole is, and where it is, and how big, and that it is not new and it is not a crack, and that the honest repair is a new cutwater, and that a cutwater is nine months of the bench's whole capacity, and that the arch's tolls are pledged to the ninth of a grandson.

The bench-clerk wrote it down. He wrote it in the same hand he wrote everything, and he read it back twice, and the second time he read it with the pen in the waterpot and did not put it down, and then the man with the mallet said one sentence, and the sentence was: "Enter the hour."

"Entered," said the bench-clerk, "at the ninth hour of the twenty-sixth of Longlight, on the word of a stranger, unverified, at the bench's asking. And I would like to enter that the stranger was in the river at the fourth hour and that the bench was told so by its own foreman and not by the stranger, and the foreman is in this room and can be asked."

Nobody asked the foreman. About nine people did, later, in the yard, which is the correct number and the correct place and is a different nine people every time.

Then the man with the mallet said the second thing, and it was the reason there had been two hundred people at the back, and it was a thing that had been coming for a month and had been waiting for a stranger to put a number in the room so that it could not be a matter of opinion.

"Alis Merrow is going to ask us for something in this hall before we break up," he said. "He has been asking me for a fortnight and I have been putting him off on the ground that a bench does not its business in public. That was a coward's ground and I am saying so in front of two hundred people.

"**We have held this arch for four generations. We have never once been asked whether we will go on holding it.** Tonight we are going to be asked, out loud, in a hall, by a creditor, and we are going to have to answer, and the answer is going to be in this bench's book in a clerk's hand with the hour on it, and every man in this room is going to remember it in a market in a year. So I am going to ask it first, before he does, and I am going to put it in plain words, and I am not going to improve it and I am not going to ask it kindly.

"**Does this bench hold the arch, or does it not?**

"Not a stone. Not a repair. Not a bench's intentions. Does this bench hold the arch — this arch, this pier, this crossing, and the tolls and the water and the four generations of men who put their names in this book — through nine months of a cutwater, and after it? And if the answer is no, then we say no in this hall tonight, and we say what we are going to do instead, and nobody in this city will ever be able to say afterwards that this bench was asked and did not answer."

Two hundred people in a hall over an arch.

At the back of the room, on a bench against the wall, a woman in a good coat with a lamp on the floor beside her had her hands open in her lap, because she had been told twice to keep them open on the twenty-sixth of Longlight and had kept them open all morning, and there had been nothing to come, and there was nothing to come, and there was a bench order for a marking in a book in this building that had turned out, in the space of one morning, to be the winter.

Aren Kest did not answer it.

He stood up in a hall where he had been the best man in the room twice, and where a man had said in public eleven weeks ago that a city rescued by the main world would be a city the main world owns, and he understood, standing there, that the single most useful thing he could do for that city was to say nothing at all, and to let nine men who had been keeping a crossing for four generations find out what they thought about a nine-month cutwater in a room where a creditor was in it.

He said one sentence, in the trade's terms.

"Scour holes get bigger while they are being looked at," he said. "That is what a scour is. It is the only failure in a bridge that improves on being reported. **Do not put a number on it in this hall tonight. Put a date on it and put a man on it, and go back to the water in the spring and read it again.**"

And then the creditor stood up at the near side of the table, with a bundle of string on the end of it, and did not thank him for that either.
