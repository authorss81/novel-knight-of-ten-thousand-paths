# Chapter 0024

The sixteenth of Greenmonth was a Friday, and the fourth hour came in under the shutters of the Bellweather Claims Archive the way it came in every day of the year, which is to say that it came in sideways off a river and made a floor of the filing room that was not quite water and not quite not, and Mara Vey had been getting up in that floor for four years and had stopped noticing it in her second.

She had been in the building for six days with nothing to do but hold a file, and holding a file is a thing that looks like nothing from outside, and from inside it is a person counting a thing that is not in the room.

On the ninth of Greenmonth a bench of the Merefen bridge guild had voted, and the town knew it before she did, because the town does not read a roll and the town reads a board. There is a board in this town that is not a square — a board a clerk wipes every morning and chalks fresh, in a small hand, with a rule over each notice, where a line without a rule is a thing somebody put there on purpose — and on the morning of the ninth a clerk in that clerk's own hand had written a line with a rule over it, and had left a second line under it without one.

*Bond of the eleventh, road warden Kest, six marks against a chest at three and a half. Charge entered on the man's wages.*

*Ask him what the three and a half is for.*

Nobody had asked her about it. Nobody in Merefen had come up two flights of stair about six marks. The market had gone on being a market, and the wharf road had gone on being shut, and the town had gone on saying the thing it had been told to say in a stair, which was that the law was on its side, and which was now joined up with a second sentence, which was that the man who was owed the money was the man who had gone.

She wrote both lines into the day-book, in the second column, with the date and the hour, and she wrote what a board is.

*A board is a document a town keeps without knowing it is keeping one.*

Then she did the other thing, which was to write down the seven days since the eighth and the fact that not one person had come into the outer room with news of a man.

---

Here is the whole of what she had, on the fourth hour of the sixteenth, laid out on the table in the order a court would want it and not the order it had arrived in.

The bell report of the seventh of Greenmonth, filed as reported, with an hour, a direction, a tower sight-line, and three witnesses, one of them thirteen years old, and the full text of what it does not say.

A claim number, hers, the thirty-first of Mudmonth, holding a counterfoil of a severance writ that had been read aloud at a north gate in YR 301 and refused on a deck, with a stamp on it and a date and a chain of custody with three hands on it and no gap in it anywhere, which had cost the most she had ever spent on a piece of work and was worth, on the evidence of eleven weeks, nothing at all before the second of Greenmonth next year.

A sworn account in a man's own hand, beneath the counterfoil in the file, that put him in the wrong on purpose.

A copyist's attestation, in Sarn's own hand as a copyist, that a page in a Crown bound order was out of place and in a hand not of the roll, with the date and the hour and no opinion, in a wrapper in her own press and not in the building's book, because a paper in a press is a paper that has been kept and a paper in a hand is a paper that has been sworn to.

A magistrate's finding of the fourth of Greenmonth, in a county file two hundred miles off, in a magistrate's own name, five clauses and a private minute at the foot saying that the finding is not a victory.

A *memorial*, which had arrived on the twelfth and was the reason she had not slept on the thirteenth.

And a shape where the *Anchors, schedules and dispositions, Alder Vale* should have been, with a gap in it of about two inches and a half, standing since YR 309, entered on the roll, gone.

---

"A memorial," said Sarn Oyelaran, "is a paper a person writes who cannot sue."

"They are cheaper to write and much harder to answer," said Mara Vey, "and that is exactly why the Crown's men use them, and it is why I have answered none of them and I want that on the record this morning in my own hand before I say any more about them. Put that down as a standing rule of this office. This office answers instruments, and it answers memorials by being dull at them, and it does not argue with a memorial in writing, ever, and here is the reason and it is the reason for the other wall rule as well.

"A memorial asks for nothing in particular. That is its whole craft. It says that a keeper of records in Merefen is harbouring a man without a warrant and has twice named an office the Crown has not produced, and it asks that the keeper be instructed. Now. Instructed in what? There is no office that can instruct this office, because this office is funded by the town and answers to the town's assembly and to nobody else in the world, and if I answer that in a page, I have answered a question I was not asked, in a form that can be quoted, in a document that can be produced. If I answer it with nothing, a man in a room in the provincial town reads nothing, and the reading of nothing is not available to him, and a memorial dies of its own weight in about five years and then somebody reuses it in a different case and finds that it does not work, because a thing with no answer on it has never been answered.

"So we do not argue with it. We make a gap in the chain into a document with a date on it, in the plainest form I have, and then we stop, and we let the memorial sit on a desk opposite a piece of paper with a date on it and no rhetoric in it. A man with a rhetoric in his hand and a date against it loses, eventually, and I do not have to be there when he does."

"It is not fair to him," said Sarn.

"No," said Mara. "It is not fair to him and it is fair to the file, and I have four years' experience of which of those two I am actually employed to be." She put the pen down. "Write it in your own hand as well. Not because I need your attestation of a rule. Because in three years neither of us is going to remember who said it first, and a rule with two hands on it is a thing that survives the person who made it."

---

"Ma'am. The order came back."

It came back on the fifteenth and Sarn had sent for her at the eighth hour and had it in her hand at the fourth of the sixteenth, and she was holding it the way you hold a thing that has been unfolded and refolded by somebody else's clerk.

"Late," said Mara. "It was returnable on the fifteenth."

"It was returnable on the fifteenth and it came on the fifteenth at some hour that a man in a provincial registry decided was the fifteenth, and I am not going to write down what I think that hour was." Sarn laid it flat. "The stamp is good. The order is good. The fourth press was open to this office from the ninth hour yesterday to the fourth hour yesterday afternoon, and a clerk named for the day stood in the doorway of it the whole time, and I have written that down and he has initialed it, and it is the only part of yesterday I am certain of."

"And?"

"And the book is not there, and the gap is bigger than it was, and Mr Halven was in the room for ninety minutes of it." Sarn's mouth did a thing. "He was not asked to come."

"No. He never is."

"He brought the shelf register out himself. The press's own register, the roll that says what the press holds, in his own office's hand, which is a Crown document and which we had asked for a search fee for on the second and been told the four shillings bought a date. He read us the line. He had it copied out by his own clerk, in the office's hand, and he had the clerk initial the copy, and he gave the copy, and he said that if it was ever disputed he would say in a room that he had given it of his own motion and not on a fee."

"That is either a great deal of honesty or the beginning of something," said Mara Vey.

"I wrote it down exactly as he said it, and I would like to know what you think it is, because you have known him a week and I have known him longer and I keep coming out wrong about him."

"He is a keeper of a public register," said Mara, "and a keeper of a public register has no jurisdiction. He cannot decide what a register means. He cannot refuse a fee without a magistrate, so he cannot be got at except through a magistrate, and he cannot be threatened with anything a magistrate would not also throw at him. That is his position and it is a good one and it is the only reason he is not the villain of this file. And a man in that position is not helped by a Crown office making a mess of its own press, because the mess makes people look at the register, and people looking at the register is the worst possible thing for him." She turned the copy round. "Read me the line."

Sarn read it, in a flat copyist's voice, and Mara Vey listened to it with her hand flat on the table.

The register of the fourth press. The *Anchors, schedules and dispositions, Alder Vale*, entered in order among two hundred and nine other volumes and rolls, on its own shelf, with its own press-mark.

And against it, in the same hand, in the same ink, in the column where a press-mark book keeps what has happened to a volume: a date, and the word *removed*, and nothing else.

*Nineteenth of Mudmonth, YR 301. Removed.*

No name. No office. No authority entered, no destination entered, no fee entered, no receipt entered, no initial of the clerk who took it out and no initial of the man who told him to.

The same date. The date of the Sundering. The date on the north-bank writ that came down the river in YR 301 and was refused on a deck and was read aloud in a guild hall by a man who had been told to read it and to say nothing else.

On the day the cull was ordered, somebody walked into a Crown press two hundred miles from here and took the book of Alder Vale's anchors off its shelf, and wrote the date, and did not write who.

"Well," said Mara Vey, after a while. "That is the most useful sentence anybody has put in this building in four years, and I would like to be a long way from it, and both of those are true at once and you may write both of them down."

"How is it useful?"

"Because it is a *date*." She had it in front of her and she was not going to touch it, which was how she treated anything she wanted. "A date can be looked for. A date can be put to a man in a room and it does not need me to have an opinion about it. In four years, or eleven, somebody in that registry is going to remember a morning, and the day I want that is the day I want to be a person who wrote down a shelf-mark in a fair copyist's hand on the fifteenth of Greenmonth, YR 312, and did not put a speech round it. And it can be looked for, and there is not one other thing anybody in this county can be given this month that can be looked for." She squared the copy. "You did exactly the right thing with this and I want you to know that you did it before I told you to, and I have written your name against the hour of it in the day-book, in my own hand and not as a copyist, because I have told you what your third post is for and this is the first thing you have done that it is for."

---

The claim in the outer room at the sixth hour was not a Hushwater claim.

It was a woman of about fifty-five, in a coat that had been walked in, with a bag in front of her on the floor, and inside the bag a piece of dressed stone about the size of two fists laid on a cloth.

She had come from Stonebarrow. She would not give her name and Mara Vey did not press her, because a claimant who will not give a name has usually been told what happens to a name.

"It is off the crossing," said the woman. "Not a piece of a bridge. A piece of *the* bridge. Look at it."

Mara Vey looked at it. It was a fragment of a dressed face, an inch and a quarter thick, with a chamfer on one edge and a line of drill holes along the top of it, and it was the same stone, and the same work, and the same tooling as nine other fragments of dressed face that had come into this outer room in four years from four villages and were sitting in a tray on the shelf behind her, and every one of them had been refused and none of them had been thrown away.

"What is your claim," said Mara.

"That is my husband's name on it and I would like it read into your book and I would like the date and I would like it to be a number, because a thing without a number is a thing you can say was never said."

"Whose name."

The woman did not say it. She put two fingers on the stone and left them there.

"Right," said Mara Vey. "Then I am going to tell you two things and the second one is the reason I am going to say the first.

"What this record says is that a dressed fragment from a Crown clearance of the fourth of Greenmonth was produced by a woman from Stonebarrow, unaccompanied by any name, and that its face, chamfer, and line of drill holes correspond to the facing of a structure recorded as dismantled in that year. That is four words doing the work of a page and every one of them can be checked by a man who does not trust me and does not need to. What happened is that a man cut this out of a wall, or took it out of the ground where a wall came down, and walked two hundred miles with it in a bag, and it may be the only thing left of a person, and I have no way on this earth of knowing that, and I am not going to tell you that I do."

The woman sat down on the bench in the outer room, which nobody had asked her to do and which told Mara Vey that she had been on her feet since before the fourth hour.

"And the number," said the woman.

"You get the number," said Mara, "and I am going to tell you exactly what it is and exactly what it is worth, because you have walked in with a stone and you have not been lied to in this room yet and I would like to keep that true for as long as I can.

"It is not a claim to a person. It is a claim to the production of a document. I am entering it against nobody. It asks a question of whoever holds a book, and the answer has to come back in writing, and it will be dated, and it will be filed where I can find it in a year, and it will be of no use to you this month or the next. And I will tell you the thing that is worse than that, and then I will stop talking: this office is about to be asked in writing by the Crown's own county whether it exists lawfully, and I am going to answer that myself and it is going to cost me the only thing I have got, and I would rather have your stone entered the day I do it than the day after."

"Why have I been walking since the fourth hour," said the woman.

"Because you have not had it entered," said Mara Vey, "and I am sorry. I could not enter it before the second of Greenmonth and after the second of Greenmonth I have been three days behind a file, and there is no version of that sentence which improves on it. Do you want the number now or at the fourth hour tomorrow?"

"Now," said the woman.

---

She entered the woman's at the ninth hour, and then she entered the second at the eleventh, and the second one is the one that matters and she entered it on her own account.

The order of the second of Greenmonth, stamped by a magistrate on the fourth, had come back on the fifteenth and had been spent, and there was no second order and no money to get one, and the Crown registry would be three weeks finding out what had happened to the press, and the shelf register with the word *removed* and the date on it was in a wrapper in her own book, attested by a copyist, and would be worth nothing whatever to anybody.

And there was one other thing in the office's possession that she had never entered, and never intended to, and could not now not enter, because the twelfth of Greenmonth had put it in a file with a memorial in it.

The out-of-place page in the Crown's bound order, of the three hundred and second year, between two entries of three hundred and four, in a hand the same age as its neighbours: one bell, nine stone by the founder's weight, unclassified salvage, a number, an hour, no city and no river.

And a registrar's word for word, attested, that the book that would say what that bell was has stood on the shelf marked for it since YR 309 and is not there, and that nobody in that office has entered the gap anywhere.

Mara Vey took a sheet of the office's good paper and wrote at the top of it, in the heading she used for a heading:

*The Bellweather Claims Archive, Merefen. Application for the production of records, in the matter of Alder Vale. Entered on the office's own account, against no person, and with no opinion.*

She wrote the date, and the hour, and the two facts it rested on, exactly as two facts and with no speech round them, and the two facts were: *that a bound order of the Crown registry contains a page out of its order, in a hand not of the roll, concerning an object entered in the three hundred and second year; and that the volume entered on the press register as containing the schedule of anchors at Alder Vale is not on its shelf and its removal is entered with a date and no authority.*

Then she entered a claim number.

The seventh claim number this office has entered in four years, and the sixth of them in one morning, and she wrote it in the book in the fair hand and not in her own late hand, because a number is a thing other people have to be able to find.

"You are entering it against nobody," said Sarn.

"Against nobody is the only thing that is safe," said Mara Vey. "A file held by one keeper for one man is a favour. A file entered under a number is a thing a court has to answer, and a court has to answer it in a year and a day whether anybody wants to or not, and a man who is owed an answer and is not given one can be shown to have been owed one. That is the whole of the difference between me and a woman keeping a book in an apron, and I met that woman through a report a stranger filed about a bell, and she told me the reason in her own trade's words and the reason was that a copy is a second thing that somebody can lose."

"Write that down."

"Write it down," said Mara Vey, "in your own hand, and date it, and do not improve it, and do not put my name in it."

And then she did the thing she was worst at, which was to say a price out loud in her own office.

"It will cost this office, and I am telling you what it is before you sign anything, because you signed the last one before I told you and I have not forgotten that and neither, I suspect, have you. One. There will be a second memorial inside a month and this one will be better written than the last and it will not be answered. Two. My name goes on a roll in a provincial book, in a column with a number against it, and my name is the name of the keeper, and any man who wanted to know where the Archive keeps its records will be able to find out from it where the Archive keeps its records. Three. If anybody ever examines this office, the thing they will find is a keeper who has entered an application about a Crown registry's own books in the year I did it, and the examination will be about the order of those two things and the second one will not help me at all. Four." She stopped. "Four is that if I am wrong about all of it, I have put a number on a record that says a Crown's shelf is short a book on the day of a cull, and if the man who took it off the shelf is not the man who ordered the cull, then I have spent the only asset this office has, which is being dull, and I will never be able to be dull about anything again."

Sarn Oyelaran wrote for a while, and then stopped, and said: "And what is it worth to you if you are right?"

"Nothing," said Mara Vey. "I want to be extremely clear about that, because a person who hears an office talk about an asset thinks the office wants money. I am not after the shelf register. I am after the *date*, in a fair copyist's hand, in a book of this office, dated the fifteenth of Greenmonth, YR 312, on a page that does not have a speech round it. A date is the only thing anybody has ever brought me that the Crown cannot disprove by finding one error in it. I have been in this building four years and I have never had one. I have had a great many things that were nearly dates, which are the same as nothing, and I have had one whole man, who is four days off under an arch and is the only person in the world who could be asked to produce the other half of anything, and I could not tell him I had entered a number for him because he was gone before the pencil was off the paper."

She capped the ink.

"Sign it at the fourth hour," she said. "And Sarn. Four times I have been right in this building since the sixth of Mudmonth. Four. And the arithmetic of a woman who has been right four times and has never been wrong once is a woman who has begun to price it, and I have started doing that this week and I did not notice until about an hour ago, and I would like to be at a table with another person when I notice that."

---

She was at the table with another person until the seventh hour and then she was not, and at the eighth she wrote one line in the day-book in her own hand with the lamp going, and it was the only line in four years that she had entered and had not entered in a file.

*Twenty-seventh of Mudmonth to the thirtieth: fever, three days, over on the thirtieth, entered from a watch statement and not from his own mouth. The same man went out of this world at the ninth hour on the ninth of Greenmonth and has been gone six days. I have no word, no paper, no clerk, and no authority to require anything of anybody in a city that does not admit to the Crown that it exists, and I have not sent a thing and cannot send a thing, and the reason I have written this line is that the man is going to come back through a seat he cut himself in a bridge and the rule is three days, and I have had a week to think about the fact that I am the only person in this county who knows the number and the man is not the kind of man who counts his own costs.*

Then she stopped, and read the line back, and did not enter it as an exhibit, and did not rule it under, and did not tear it out.

What a keeper of records does with a rule she has not told the subject of is the oldest question there is, and there is only one answer, and it is the answer she had given a woman in a stair in the first week of Mudmonth, and she had given it to a man who was angry.

*You tell him. In writing. Before he is told by his own body.*

She took a sheet of the office's good paper and wrote at the top of it, in the fair hand, because a fair hand is a hand other people can find:

*The cost of a crossing, as known to this office, entered on the office's own account and with no opinion. That on the twenty-seventh of Mudmonth, YR 312, a man returned from a place this office does not acknowledge, and that from that day to the thirtieth he was in a fever, three days, and the fever broke on the thirtieth, and that the same man went out of this world at the ninth hour on the ninth day of Greenmonth. That this office has no means of enquiring after him and did not send a thing and cannot send a thing. That he is not a man who counts his own costs. That therefore the number on this page is the office's own account of what a crossing costs, entered so that when he comes back he is told it by a paper and not by his own body, and entered without one word of opinion, and entered on the sixteenth of Greenmonth, YR 312, at the ninth hour, by Mara Vey, and dated.*

She read it back. It said what the record said and it said nothing about what happened, and the difference between those two things was the whole of her trade and she had never once got tired of it.

She put it in the day-book and not in the press, because a paper in a press is a paper that has been kept and a paper in a hand is a paper that has been sworn to, and she was going to have to hand it to him herself, standing up, in a room, and watch him read it.

Down on Weir Street a yard was shutting for the night, and she did not know that, and nobody was going to tell her, and in a toll-house by the fish weir there was a drawer with a Crown's letter in it addressed to a keeper of an office that did not exist, and not one of those two facts would ever come up her stair. That was the ordinary condition of a keeper of records and she had been standing in it for four years.

What was on her table was a hand and three numbers and no opinion, which is what a record is.

A man had gone out of this world on the ninth at the ninth hour, and a clerk two hundred miles off, in a building she had never been inside, had put a word against that ninth at the same hour, and in a year the word would be a fact about a man and not an argument. She had said so to his face in a room in this town in Greenmonth and had watched him hear it and not argue.

And the third number was nineteen, which she had taken from him and had not believed, and which she had this morning written out on her own paper with no opinion on it at the top of it, because a keeper of records who has an opinion about a number is not a keeper of records, she is a person with a case.

Not one degree of the days he was spending on somebody else's road was going to reach her. There was no door she could put a letter through and no office in this county that could ask after him, and the only thing in the world she had that could say anything to him about any of it was a page, in a hand he could not improve on, with a date on it, waiting for him to come back and be angry at it.
