# Chapter 0043

The writ of the ninth of Longlight was stamped at the second hour and it was returnable on the ninth of Rainmonth, and in the six days between the twenty-seventh of Longlight and the second of Fallowmonth the Bellweather Claims Archive could not move a record, copy one out, or part with one, and everything in the building stayed exactly where it was, and that was the whole of the damage.

It was not the whole of the cost. It took eleven hours a day for six days and it cost two of them four shillings eightpence each in a boy's time and a boy's wages at a shilling a day, and it is not a small thing for an office that had been spending four shillings on a registrar's fee in the spring and had counted it.

That is what a writ of enquiry costs a small office. It does not take anything. It makes a thing that cannot be copied into a thing that cannot be produced, and it does that to you, in your own building, in your own time, at your own charge, and it does it while asking you nothing and accusing you of nothing and never once putting a question that could be answered.

Mara Vey wrote the return in the fair hand over four days, on the office's own paper, at a table by the window with the light coming in sideways off the river, and she filled every printed space, and she made no concealment and no argument, and she did it the way she had done the form of the ninth of Longlight, which is to say she was dull at it, and being dull is a technique and not a personality, and she had four years of it.

The form ran to nine printed spaces and then a schedule, and the schedule was the part that took the four days, because the schedule asks a keeper to list, item by item, what she holds, and there were forty-one lines on it before the schedule and the office had about nine hundred holdings, and the rule is that an item is entered with a reference, a date and an hour, and a reference is a press and a shelf and a leaf, and a keeper of records who cannot give you three of those has an office and not a record.

She did the nine hundred in four days. Sarn Oyelaran did the copying because Sarn was faster and because Mara Vey had discovered in the first year that the best use of a second keeper is not a second opinion and it is not a second pair of hands, it is a person who can be wrong in a way you can check.

---

The ninth space on the ninth of Longlight's form had been *the authority on which this office holds each item of its holdings*, and the answer to it is four lines and had been written in the first hour.

The eighth space had been *items received after the date of this writ*, and the answer to it was the hardest one on the page and it took two of the four days, and it is the reason this chapter is about what it is about.

In thirty days a keeper of records receives things. That is the whole of what a record office does; it is what the door is for. And in thirty days she could not move, copy out, or part with anything she held, and so every single thing that came up those stairs between the second hour of the ninth of Longlight and the second hour of the ninth of Rainmonth had to be **received and held and entered and not disposed of**, and a thing received and held and not disposed of is a thing a keeper has a key to and no authority over, and she wrote that down and it is the sentence she has since repeated more often than any other in her life:

*That during the pendency of the enquiry this office received items and held them and entered them, and did not move, copy out, or part with any of them, and that the receiving of an item is not a holding of it in the sense the writ uses, and that this office is aware of no authority in this county by which a keeper may refuse a thing that is brought up a stair, and has not refused one, and would not know how.*

It came to nine items.

Four of them were claims. One of them was a dressed fragment of a bridge face out of Stonebarrow that had been walked a hundred miles by a woman who would not give her name, and it was refused as a claim and kept as a stone, and it came up the stair for the second time in Fallowmonth because the woman came back with a second fragment and a story about where the first one had lain.

One of them was a fishing-net weight with a name cut into the eye of it, which is the only kind of object this office has ever received that nobody could argue with.

One was a sheet of paper in a child's hand, folded four times, brought by a schoolmistress's daughter, and the paper said a name and a date and nothing else, and there was no way to know whether the name belonged to this matter at all, and it went in on the same terms as everything else and it was entered and it is still entered and nobody has been able to do one single thing with it.

And two of them were **lendings**, which is a thing this office does and which the office has to do because a record held by one keeper is a favour, and the first was on the twenty-second of Longlight at the fourth hour of the afternoon, when a tradesman of Weir Street walked up that stair and left a bundle of eleven sheets with a man of sixty-one and put seven lines into a creditor's roll in his own hand, and the second was on the twenty-sixth of Longlight, at the fourth hour, and it was a man with a bar on his shoulder and a line in his voice and there was nothing in the room about a bell.

And she listed both. That was the cost.

She listed them under the eighth space, in the fair hand, with the dates and the hours, and she listed the *lending* of the twenty-second of Longlight as an item received and held by this office as the lender, with the words *the property of a person and not of this office*, and she did not name the person, because a schedule of holdings is a list of things and not a list of people, and because a name in a schedule is a name in a file in a building two hundred miles off.

So the answer she gave on the twenty-ninth of Longlight, in the fair hand, in a return of about nine hundred words, said in the schedule: *a chain of eleven sheets, received the twenty-second of Longlight, YR 312, at the fourth hour of the afternoon, held by lending, property of a person, the holder being the toll-holder of the Old Toll crossing of this town, who is entered in this office's records as a creditor in this matter and not as a party.*

And it told a provincial office, in her hand, in a form, correctly and without concealment, on a date, that there was a document about the office of keeper of the Bellweather hinge, made on the twenty-second of Longlight in this county, held outside the office that was under enquiry, by a man in this town, in a creditor's book.

She had known it would, from the fourth hour of the afternoon of the twenty-second, and she had put the day-book entry in at the seventh hour that night, in advance, with the hour on it, and the entry is four lines long and it is the fourth cost this office has ever put on its own account, and the four before it were all paid and this one is still being paid.

---

The other form came on the first of Fallowmonth.

It came in the same tube as a county clerk's copy of a minute of a county council, which is what a tube is for, and it was two leaves and it was not sealed, and it was not a writ, and it had no returnable date on it, and the thing that made Mara Vey put it at the top of the table and not the bottom was that it was **polite.**

*To the keeper of the Bellweather Claims Archive.*

*It will be understood that this office has before it a file concerning the crossing of Alder Vale, in which this county appears, and that the office has received a schedule of attendance in which a person is described more than once by county and office and not by name.*

*That office is asked to furnish this office with the names of the persons so described, together with their occupations and their places of residence, upon the enclosed form, at the convenience of this office, and at the cost of this office.*

*No date is named, because no date is required.*

The enclosure had a ruled column. The column was headed, in a fair hand, and the hand was not a young man's:

*Keeper — Name — Occupation — Place — How often.*

It was headed *Keeper*, and the column was ruled on every line, and it was ruled on the fifth column, and the fifth column was the one that said *how often*, and *how often* meant *how often did you see him in your building*, and the Crown had been told, in a building two hundred miles off, that there was a keeper in this county, and the Crown was now asking the keeper.

Sarn Oyelaran read it over her shoulder and said, "They have got the word from the schedule."

"They have got the word from the county's own schedule of inspections," said Mara Vey. "There is a form in that county with a ruled column headed *Keeper* on every line, and a man's name went into it forty-one times in Longlight, and somebody in a provincial office read the heading off the form and never once read the line under it. **They are not asking me who I am. They are asking who I keep.** That is a different question and it has been sitting in that file since the second of Longlight and it has just walked out of it and into my building with a form."

"Are we going to answer it."

"We are going to answer it and it is going to be the best piece of work this office has ever done and it is going to cost somebody a name and it is not going to be mine."

She took a clean sheet of the office's own paper and wrote in the fair hand, and she wrote it slowly, and she read it back to Sarn before she put the hour on it, and the reading back is not a thing she invents; it is the third question she asks a witness, applied to herself, and she apologises for it out loud every time and she did not apologise for it this time because the witness was her own hand and a person cannot be gentle with their own hand.

*The keeper of this office answers the enclosed form as follows, and states the reason in full, so that the answer may not be improved upon by a clerk in a filing room.*

*Names, being the names of natural persons, are not furnished. Four persons are described in the schedule of attendance in the county and office terms given, and the office enters that the number of persons so described is four, and the number of attendances is six, and that no further description of them is entered.*

*And the reason is entered on the office's own account and against no person, and it is this: a natural person's name, entered in a form in a provincial office, in the matter of an object which this county's own magistrate's court has found to be lawfully held and has asked the sealing office to produce and has not been answered, is a name that may not be produced, retracted, corrected, or explained, and the office which holds it may be closed, and the person may not. A name in a form is a thing a man is made out of. This office has refused four claims in four years and has entered all four, and has never in four years furnished a name to any office, and is not going to begin in a form with no date on it, and a form with no date on it is the most dangerous object this office has ever been sent, because it does not have to be answered and therefore does not have to be tried, and it will go on being in a drawer in a building two hundred miles off until a person decides it is more convenient to send it than to answer it.*

*And the number four is furnished instead, and the number is exact, and it can be checked against the schedule of attendance by anybody who has the schedule, and it is worth what a number is worth and not what a name is worth.*

*Entered at the second hour of the first of Fallowmonth, YR 312, on the office's own account, against no person, with no opinion, and sent on the county's post on the second at the first hour.*

"Read it back," said Sarn Oyelaran.

"I have read it back," said Mara Vey, "and I am not going to write the other one, and I want the not-writing entered."

---

The not-writing is the second of Fallowmonth and it is the only thing in six days that this office has ever put on its own account twice.

On the second of Fallowmonth, at about the ninth hour, there is a wall in that filing room with a rule on it in a fair hand, and the second rule is: **no combined figure is entered in this office.** And under the counter, in a locked drawer, in a wrapper, there is the thing the rule exists for.

Ferris Oat's **brine weights**: thirty-one days of salt weights at Saltbrim, the weighmaster's own arithmetic intact, four figures to a day and the day's tonnage under them, and the arithmetic adds up, and it cannot be argued with, because a weight is not an opinion.

It is in that building. It has been in that building for four years. It is **cited** in this office in eleven documents and in a magistrate's finding and in a claim and it is **not filed**, and it is not an exhibit, and it has not been an exhibit since the sixth of Mudmonth, and the reason it is not an exhibit is written on the wrapper in Sarn Oyelaran's hand and it is a line about an order, and it is a good line and it is the reason a roll of the dead has not been built.

And on the second of Fallowmonth, at the ninth hour, with a writ running against her and a form with a column on it for names in a drawer in a building two hundred miles off and a schedule of nine hundred holdings in four columns on a table by the window, Mara Vey sat down and worked out, for about four minutes, what the roll would be.

It is not a hard thing to do. That is what nobody understands about it. It is four days of reading and one afternoon of arithmetic, and the arithmetic is already done, because Ferris Oat did it in YR 301 with a steelyard and a slate and no idea of what he was for.

Eighty-one days of a market town's salt, weighed and carried and delivered, and every day of it with the number of loads and the number of households that a load went to, because a weighmaster in a salt town writes the load and not the soul and that is why his book cannot be argued with. **A city eats about this much salt in a month.** A village of two hundred people eats about this much in six weeks. And a low quarter where the people were already ill eats more than a low quarter where they were not, because salt is what you give a person with a fever, and there is a figure for that too and Ferris Oat has it in the third column of the fourth week, and it is the only line in the four hundred years of that office's books that anybody has ever wanted, and it wants the house, not the hearth.

Put the thirty-one days against the eight places and a man can produce a number for each, and the numbers will be worse than the Crown's and better than anybody's, and every one of them will be a figure about weight.

And then the names go in beside the numbers. And a roll of the dead built out of a weighmaster's ledger, entered in the order of the ledger, with the weights standing beside the names, is a document that can only be checked, and cannot be argued with, and cannot be improved, and cannot be got to change in a room.

**It is the best document this office has ever been in a position to make. It is four years of work. It is the reason the office exists.**

And on the second of Fallowmonth, at the ninth hour, with a return of nine hundred words half written and a form with a column for names in a drawer and a man under an arch four days off, she got out the office's own paper, and wrote the heading, and wrote for about eleven minutes, and it is a page in the day-book with no claim number on it and no press reference, because it is not a record and it has not been entered and never will be.

*Entered on the office's own account, against no person, on the second of Fallowmonth, YR 312, at the ninth hour, in this room, with no witness, which is entered.*

*That the said keeper did on this day, being alone in this room, sit down with the brine weights of Ferris Oat of Saltbrim, being held and cited and not filed, and did work out, to within a margin she is content to stand behind, what the roll of the dead of the crossing of Alder Vale and its dependent places would be if this office were to build it, and did the arithmetic, and did not build it.*

*And that the said keeper knows, being a keeper of records and having four years of the work, that this office is at this moment the only office in this kingdom that can build it, and that the roll would be unanswerable, and that the reason for not building it is not that it cannot be built.*

*And the reason is entered in full. It is that the roll is a ledger and the ledger is the whole of the case, and the ledger is the only instrument that the people of that city are owed, and it belongs to the city, and the city keeps its own dead, and has been keeping its own dead for eleven years in its own order in its own hands, and has twice refused this office a copy of a book of its own and been right both times, and a woman with a crossing book at a hamlet on a channel said in a room in the middle of this year that a copy is a second thing that somebody can lose, and she was not talking about paper.*

*And that the keeper has held that rule on that wall for four years and has never once had a reason to hold it, and has it now, and the reason is that this office is a party, under a writ, in a matter about a bell, and a party that builds a roll of the dead has claimed the dead, and a keeper who has claimed the dead cannot afterwards stand in a hall in that city and be a person with no opinion.*

*And that on the second of Fallowmonth, YR 312, the keeper of this office did not file the roll of the dead, and did not begin it, and has not begun it, and does not know that she ever sat down to it, and this line is entered so that in eleven years somebody will know that she sat down to it and did not, and why, and on what day, and at what hour.*

She read it back once and did not read it back twice, and put the hour on it, and did not initiall it, because a rule is not signed by the person it binds, and put it in the day-book and not in the press, and then went back to the return, because the return was due on the county's post at the first hour on the third and it was the second of Fallowmonth at the ninth hour and she had four hours.

---

The other thing that left the building in those six days was a piece of paper, and it did not go to a creditor, and that was deliberate and it cost her a night of thinking.

On the tenth of Longlight she had sent a certified copy of this office's own instrument of foundation out of the building to a man of sixty-one in a toll-house, on the argument that a document left with a person is a thing a person can be arrested for, and an office can only be closed, and a closed office still has a door.

The man in the toll-house was a creditor, and a creditor has a chest, and a chest is a thing that is shut, and a shut thing in a town where the Crown's man has a commendation nailed to his own wall is a thing that will be opened in a year by a man with a form.

The second thing went to **Tovan Kest**, at the fourth hour of the first of Fallowmonth, and it was the writ itself — a true copy of the instrument of the ninth of Longlight, all of it, the recital, the paragraphs, the stamped hour, and the bar — and she sent it in a plain wrapper with no seal on it at all, and she walked it down Weir Street herself with Sarn Oyelaran behind her, because the first time she had sent a boy up a stair with a thing in it the boy had come back with it in his hand.

He read it standing at a trestle with four men and the iron going, and he read it twice, and he gave it back, and said the thing about it that she had walked down the street to hear.

"You are handing me the reason they cannot move anything out of your building," he said. "For a month. That is the whole of it and you have walked it down here yourself."

"Yes."

"And you want nothing."

"I want one thing and it is not a favour and I have not come to ask it." She said it in the gateway where he had invented a rule in front of four men in Greenmonth, because she had learned in four years that a thing said in a gateway is a thing said in public. "If anybody in this town comes to this yard with a Crown's paper and asks you whether your yard holds records belonging to a record office, the answer is that this yard holds tools and a week's wages and the fact that things were set, and that there is no officer of this county with authority to ask you, and that the clerk who asked in Greenmonth said so in writing on the back of a paper and lost nothing by it."

"I have a rule that says the last of that," said Tovan Kest. "In a gateway, in front of four men, in the last week of Greenmonth. Nothing that belongs to a man goes in this yard's book and only a thing set."

"I know the rule. I have it in a page."

"You have it in a page and you have come down here to say it out loud in a gateway, and I am going to say it out loud too, because you are right and because you came." He put the copy of the writ down on the trestle and squared it. "And here is what it costs, and I want it written down somewhere with an hour on it even though you will not write it in your book.

"**A piece of Crown paper in this yard is a piece of Crown paper in this yard.** It is shut, and it is on a shelf, and a Crown's man can come into this gateway and put a hand on that shelf, and he can do it without a court, because there is no officer of this county with authority to ask me, and if he takes it off the shelf and goes up Weir Street with it, then in a year the Crown has a copy of its own writ in a carpenter's yard in a town where four men know where it is and two of them are mine. And that is the *best case*. The other one is that a man takes it off the shelf, and the Crown has in its file that a person in Merefen has concealed a document — and it does not matter that the document is the Crown's own, and it will not matter, and I will be a man who kept a Crown paper in a yard for a month because a woman he does not like came down the street and asked him to."

"Does that change it."

"No," said Tovan Kest. "It is the cost of the thing and you have come down here to hand it to me and I am going to say that it is the correct decision and I am going to take it, and I am not going to like it, and I am not going to thank you, and I am not going to be one of the men who is nice to you about it." He picked the copy up. "It goes in the room at the back, on the shelf above the bench, in a wrapper with no seal, and it is not in my book, and the book says the thing that is the cost, which is the day and the hour and the fact and that the yard does not know what is in it. And when the writ is answered on the ninth of Rainmonth you will tell me, and I will take it off the shelf and put it in the fire in this gateway in front of four men, and it will be the only document I have ever burned and I shall want somebody to see me do it."

"You will have me to see it," said Mara Vey. "I will be here and I will be dull about it."

---

The tube went back down the county road on the second of Fallowmonth at the first hour with three things in it: a return of nine hundred words and a schedule of nine hundred holdings, and the two forms in a fair hand, one of which said *names, being the names of natural persons, are not furnished*, and the other of which was a county's minute of a county council about a culvert, and which nobody in the building read and which was in the same tube as everything else, which is the price of a tube.

Sarn Oyelaran tied it and Mara Vey counted the leaves and put her thumb on the knot the way she always did, and then she stood at the window in the dark for a while after the lamp had gone down the road, doing the thing she does, which is what it is she does.

Because on the first of Fallowmonth, in the same tube, in a clerk's hand, there had also been a **second writ of enquiry into the custody of records**, and it had been stamped on the twenty-ninth of Longlight at the second hour, three days after hers, and it was addressed to **the office of the reeve of Merefen**, and it used the same form, and it named the same object, and it did not have a returnable date on it because it was returnable on the ninth of Rainmonth as well.

Two in the same tube, three days apart, on the same paper, from the same office, in the same matter.

There would be a third.

And the third one, when it came, would be on the town clerk, or on the clerk of the bench, or on the county's own filing room, and each of them would be answered in a form in the fair hand, and every one of the answers would be correct, and every one of them would cost a man an afternoon and a woman a night, and there is no point in any of them at which a county can be got to do this to itself, because the Crown's clerk in the provincial town had worked it out in nine minutes on a wet afternoon in Greenmonth, and she knew that he had, because it was written at the end of a memorandum she had never seen:

*A writ of enquiry into a record office is the cheapest thing this office has ever found. It takes nothing. It closes nothing. It is worth nothing on the returnable day. It is a form with a date on it, and a date is a thing a person can be got to turn round.*
