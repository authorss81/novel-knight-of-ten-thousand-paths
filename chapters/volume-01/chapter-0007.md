# Chapter 0007

The Bellweather Bridge Guild's hall is a room over the crossing, which is not a metaphor; the arch runs under the floor of it, and there is a stone in the floor of the room that is the crown of the third arch, and the bench sits around that stone and has sat around it for four generations.

The room smelled of lamp oil, wet wool, and hot ink. There was a long table that had been two, a stove that was not lit, and at the head of it a lead-lined trough about the length of a man's arm, covered with a board.

Aren Kest came in at the ninth hour, wet again, with a paper in his hand and a cut on his right hand that had been bound with a strip of sailcloth by a woman who did not offer to do it, and there were nine people in the room and two of them were not guild.

The toll-house steward sat on the near side of the table with a clerk behind him and a great deal of paper, and the guild had put him on that side themselves, which told Aren more about the guild in one evening than a year of looking would have.

"The bench will hear you," said the bench-clerk, "and the bench will put one question, and you will answer it once, and then the bench will make its terms. Do not thank the bench. Do not say you are honoured. The bench has heard both from strangers for two hundred years and neither has ever once been a reason for anything."

A man in his sixties on the guild side, with a mallet hanging from a nail behind him, put his hands flat on the table.

"We'll go straight to it," he said. "You said on the deck, at the seventh hour, in front of forty people, that you came from a country where a finding says this city is a fever pit with four thousand in it, and that you have a bell of ours in that country, split through the mouth. Which one of those is the reason you are in this room?"

"Both," said Aren. "But if you want one, the bell. The finding I can recite. It's taught."

"Then you have a use already," said the man, "and I'll be frank, because the bench does not like being got around. We have a stranger who has come out of a road, and a stranger who has seen a Crown finding, and the two of those are the same stranger, and the whole of the city has heard about it by the eighth hour including the toll houses, and the toll houses have sent us a man with paper." He nodded at the steward. "So. The steward wants to ask you about the north's roads. I want to ask you about a bell. Let us not pretend we are a single mouth, because the bench has spent four generations not being a single mouth and that is the only reason it still exists."

The toll-house steward did not wait to be asked. He had a merchant's way of putting a question so that the answer sounded like an opportunity.

"There are forty-one toll-houses in this city and the three northern ones have been shut eleven years," he said. "The northern road is a good road. It is a raised road with a paved causeway and it is the road your carts would come on, and it is four days from a kingdom. We have never lost the paving. We have lost only the traffic. And I am told, by a man on a bridge this morning, that the country to the north has never stopped existing." He put two fingers on the table. "Tell me about the roads."

"There are roads," Aren said. "There's a raised road at Merefen with a reed channel on each side and four bays of timber at the crossing, and there's a lot of fen between this and anything else. I can't tell you what is in the north. I've never been past the abbey at the river mouth."

"That is not what I asked."

"Then it is the only thing I have," Aren said, and the room enjoyed that, a little, and he did not, and the guild side did not, and the toll-house side wrote it down, which meant the toll-house side had been right about him in a way that would cost him later.

"North," said the bench-clerk, writing. "Anonymous. Confirmed by nothing. Continue."

The man with the mallet said: "The bell."

"Where is it?"

"In the founder's yard, on the north bank, under a lean-to, on two oak trundles, with a tarred cloth over it, and it has been there since the year it was cracked." He looked at Aren. "It is a third bell. Do you know what a third bell is?"

"I know what tolling is. I don't know your third."

"A third bell is the bell you ring to say the crossing is closed, and it is rung from the deck at night, and it is rung once and it is rung long, and it is rung by a man standing at the crown, and everything on the water stops and turns back." The man's hand went flat on the table and pressed. "It was cracked on the third stroke. It was rung on the night of the nineteenth of Mudmonth, YR 301, by a boy of seventeen who had a bar in his hand and was told he had a full minute, and the first two strokes carried over the whole city and were heard at Saltbrim, and the third went lip to shoulder and has not made a note since and never will again, and it is the reason we would not answer the writ. A city that rings a third bell is a city that has made a decision. We rang it, and a Crown's man wrote down the date."

Nobody in the room said anything for a while. The stove that was not lit ticked.

"So I rang your bell," Aren said.

"You rang our bell," said the man. "And you rang it in your own country, on the night of the nineteenth, which is the date, in a storm, with your hand on it, and it is cracked, and it is still our bell, and it went somewhere. That is a fact and I have had it in front of me since the seventh hour and I have not been able to make anything of it, and I am not going to pretend I can. What I can tell you is what it is and what it was for, and then you can tell me what you think you did, and I will decide whether I believe you, and I will tell you which of us needs the other one more."

"That is fair," said Aren.

"Do not thank me for being fair. I am fair because a fair bench survives and a partial bench does not."

He lifted the board off the trough.

There was water in it, and the water was cold, and under the water was a block of grey stone the size of a bread loaf, and cut into the face of it, in letters two inches deep and clean-cut, a pledge. It ran in one line and then a second and then a date.

Aren read it upside-down from where he stood and then came round the table, which nobody stopped him from doing, and read it the right way up.

*That this crossing shall be held by this guild and by no other hand; that no order, writ, or office shall close it while hands remain on it; that the guild will answer for the arch with its own strength and pay for the keeping of it out of its own chest; and that the arch is not to be sold.*

The date under it was the nineteenth of Mudmonth, YR 301.

"That is eleven years old," Aren said.

"It is eleven years old and it is renewed every Longlight and it is read under water in the middle of the year so that nobody reads it in a hurry." The man put the board back. "That stone and that writ are the same event, and I will tell you the whole of it because you have earned it with a boy's hand and you would otherwise have guessed it wrong within a week.

"A writ came to the north gate on the evening of the fourteenth of Mudmonth, YR 301, from the Crown's emergency office, sealed, and it was read in this room by a man who was not a guild man and who was told by the Crown's courier to read it aloud and to say nothing else." The bench-clerk's hand had stopped moving. "That is the part I have never been able to say in one piece. He read it. It required the severance of the crossing at Bellweather and of all places dependent upon it, and the removal of the persons found within the sealed limit, and it required the handing of the arch to officers of the office. It gave five days, and the five days were counted from the day before it was read, which is the oldest trick in the book and the reason a courier's mouth is a thing you have to watch.

"There was a vote in this room and I will not pretend the guild was brave, because it was frightened. Nine of us could refuse. Six of the nine wanted to read it twice and go north and ask somebody, and two of those six wanted to go north because their families were north of the river, and one of them said aloud that a crossing is a thing you can hand over once and pick up again in a year.

"That was a stupid thing to say and I did not argue with it, and I have thought about it every year since. We refused it on the deck of the arch on the nineteenth, at the second hour of the night, because the bench's whole purpose is written on a stone under a trough in this room and a bench that hands over its arch is not a bench. And we refused it with the third bell, at the crown, at the second hour, once and long, and the boy who rang it was seventeen and had a bar and was told he had a full minute.

"The tender came back to the gate at noon on the twentieth and found two hundred people on the deck with the bar stock. Nobody ordered those two hundred. They came. The captain of the tender was an old man in a Crown cloak with a written duty and, in my hearing, no instruction to use it, and he sat down on the toll stone and said he would hold there until somebody with more than himself told him to begin. He held a day and a half in the rain, on that stone, and ate what we gave him and did not give an inch, and I have not decided to this day whether that makes him the best Crown officer I have ever met or the reason we are still alive.

"Then on the twenty-second a second boat came up the river with a written recall in it and a name on the outside of it, and the crew went down the river, and nobody on the deck was hurt, and that is the whole of our war and the whole of our story." He looked at the toll-house steward. "You will note that I have not read the name on the outside of that boat out loud, in a room, to a stranger, and you will not get it out of me this month.

"What we kept: the counterfoil. That is the stub. It is the half of the writ that travels back to the office and stays in the office's own copy of what it sent, and it is dated, and it has the office's seal on it and no signature at all, because a stub carries the seal and not the name. The name is on the other half, in a register, and the register of every writ touching this arch since the arch was built is in the hands of the toll houses, and the toll houses are not the guild and are not your friends and are the only people in this city who have offered to give you anything at all this evening. I am aware of what I have just done."

"You have," said the steward, "and I have been sitting here for two hours deciding whether to be offended, and I have decided not to be, because the man who can tell us what the north's finding says is a man we want in a room, and a room where the truth comes out is a room where a trade can be discussed." He folded his hands. "My office would never give that page to a Crown office. My office might, in a year, give a copy to a man who had come back with something."

The man with the mallet raised his hand. "That is the toll house's business. Here is the bench's."

"Before the terms," said the man on the guild side who had not spoken all evening, and he reached along the table and turned a book round so that it faced the stranger, and pushed it a foot and stopped, "so that you know what you are being asked to carry and what you are not."

It was a crossing book. Quarto, board covers gone, the spine gone, the pages swollen and cockled and re-laid twice by somebody who understood that paper is a material with a grain.

"Don't open it past the second leaf," he said. "It is the Hushwater book. Hushwater Reach is a hamlet of the far channel, a mile and a half downriver, on ground that goes under at the spring tides, and it is not on the arch and it is not the guild's, and the guild has no authority over it and never has. The ferry there is a cooperative. Nine hands on a crossing and a woman who keeps the book, and the book says who crossed and what they crossed with and what came back, and it says it in a hand that other people can check, which is the only reason anyone has ever believed a ferryman.

"It is a living book. There is a woman there now with the same book, a different one, on the same page, going back forty years, and the names in it are not the names in this one." He turned one leaf with a fingernail. "Second page. Hushwater Reach, and the crossings, and the hearths on each side of the water. And you will notice what is not on it."

Aren looked. "No number of souls."

"No number of souls," said the man. "A cooperative counts ferry hands and hearths, because a cooperative is a set of boats and a set of chimneys and not a parish, and the Crown's clerks have wanted a soul-count out of that book since my father's time and have never had anything but what is in it. There are eleven crossings listed on that page and nine names against them, because the ninth name is the woman keeping it and she does not cross on a working day, and I have had it argued out in front of me by two assessors and a man from the abbey, and the best figure anybody could put to the hamlet was near sixty, and every one of them said it as though apologising for the imprecision, and I told them the imprecision is in the counting and not in the hamlet.

"So that is what you are carrying, and it is on the table because a claim about four thousand is easy and a claim about *dependent places* is where a person is actually made to disappear, and you will have both in your hand by morning or you will have the one that the crown prints." He shut the book. "Now the terms."

"Here is the bench's," said Aren, before the man with the mallet got there. "I would rather say it than be asked twice, because I am going to be here long enough to be asked twice."

"You are not," said the man with the mallet. "You will be here long enough, and that is the difficulty, and it is the whole of the difficulty. Hear the terms, and they are not money, and the bench cannot be bought in a matter of this kind, and a man who has been in this room for one evening has no idea what that sentence is worth."

"You will be here," said the bench-clerk, writing, "if you are the man I take you for, which is a man the north can send back. And I would add, since nobody else in this room will say it and somebody should: you will also be here because you have already decided to be. There is no version of tonight after which you go home and forget the date. So hear the terms, and take them or leave them, and be quick about it, because the lamp is going.

"First. The bench will answer your question — the whole of our night, and the date, and the fact of a writ, and the fact of a refusal. That is an answer and it is worth more than a market's takings, and it is given to you freely, and the bench will expect you to carry it exactly as it was given, in our words or in words that a man can swear to, and you will not be able to soften it and you will not be able to enlarge it.

"Second. The bench will say a refusal, and the refusal is ours and not yours, and you will carry it in your hand to whoever sends the next order. If a writ comes to this gate again, from any office, with any seal, this city will refuse it on the deck again, and the bench wants that refusal to arrive at somebody who can be shown it. You are the only man in the world presently going to be able to carry it, and that is not a favour to you, it is an accident of your arrival, and the bench does not care why accidents happen.

"Third. You will come back. Not to be thanked and not to be rewarded, but because the road back to your country is in your own country, and the only person in the world who can open it is a man who has been in this city and been given something, and that is you. So you will come back, and the bench will not ask how or when, and the bench will hold the arch."

The room was quiet. Two hundred and eleven people had been buried of a fever, and a bell had cracked on the third stroke, and a bench of bridgewrights had sat on a stone and refused a Crown, and the room was quiet because a stranger had been told something in front of witnesses and had not yet said whether he would carry it.

"Agreed. All three, and the third one first, because I would rather be held to the promise before I know what the answer is going to be worth." He put his cut hand flat on the table and then took it off again, having decided halfway through the gesture not to do it in front of them. "And I will take the register page back. I know I cannot get it. I know the man who holds it has told you he will sell it to the right foreigner in a year, and I would do the same in his boots. But I will take the job, and I will come to the toll houses with something in my hand, and if I come with nothing then I will not come, and you may put that in your own book in your own words.

"Ten years ago, and one year after the writ — the writ in this room is dated the fourteenth of Mudmonth, YR 301, and the entry is the year after that — an officer of a Crown office in my country set a bell on a clearance roll at a town called Merefen. He wrote *unclassified salvage*. He did not write a city, because the finding of my country says this city was never built, and he did not write a river, because a roll does not take a river. I have stood in front of that roll and read it, and the clerk let me read it, because I had put two pence on the block for the thing it was describing.

"That bell is this bell. I know it the way I know a load path, and I will say it in a plain sentence because a plain sentence can be checked and a strange one cannot. Nine stone. Split lip to shoulder. And a founder's mark on the shoulder of it that is your founder's mark, which I read in a scrap yard in Merefen before I ever came up your north steps, with my own hands, on a wet morning, in a line of men who were laughing.

"It is under a tarred cloth on two trundles in your yard. In my country it stands on an iron cradle on a bridge abutment, and I rang it there on the night of the nineteenth in a storm, with a bar through the headstock, and something answered, and I came out of the arch nine hundred feet below the place I went in. Both of those are true. I cannot make them agree and I have tried for six days, and neither can you, and I would rather say it out loud at this table than carry it home and be asked about it in a room with a seal on the table and a man who is paid to be disappointed.

"In the north there are registries, and there is at least one office that answers letters and files them, and I have already been told that office's price for one look at a file and got nothing for it. I cannot promise you the north's finding. I can promise you that I will not stop asking in a room where asking is allowed, and that the moment I learn what the road is I will bring it here and put it in front of this bench, and you may put it in front of the toll houses.

"And I will not put this city in a document in the north without the bench's word for it. If the bench says no, then no, and the bell was a bell, and I will have been wrong about all of it and you may tell me so at the gate."

"You may," said the man with the mallet, "and it will be a pleasure."

The bench-clerk wrote that down too, in the same hand, and read the last line of it back, and Aren made him read it back twice.

"And the third thing," said the man with the mallet, and he took a folded paper out of his coat and put it on the table, "which we do not ask and do not refuse.

"The arch has a crack." He said it flatly, the way a man says the price of bread. "The outer ring of the second pier. It is on the river side, low down, and it is eight inches long, and it opens in the cold and shuts in the warmth, and it has opened and shut eleven years, and it is not growing. I have watched it every Longlight for eleven years. The honest repair is a new cutwater on the river side of the second pier, and a new cutwater is nine months of this bench's whole capacity, and it is stone and iron and wages, and there is no money in this city for it, because the tolls on that arch are pledged to the ninth of my grandson and the man who pledged them knew what he was doing. So we do nothing, as we have done nothing, and we watch it twice a year." He sat back. "You are a reader of timber. You will want to look at it. You will not be able to do anything about it, and looking at it will cost you something, and I would rather tell you now than let you find it out for nothing."

Aren Kest sat with his cut hand in his lap and looked at a stone trough in a floor.

"Second pier," he said. "River side, outer ring. Eight inches, and not growing."

"Not growing this month."

"You'll let me look."

"You'll be on the works in the morning and you'll look at it and you'll tell us the truth about it because you cannot do otherwise, and I'll have Quen standing next to you when you do it, and she will write down what you say." He got up, which ended the sitting, because the bench was finished and everybody in the room knew it. "You'll want to know about the register page. It is not the bench's to give. It is the toll houses'. And the toll houses have a foreigner's promise on the table now, and promises in Bellweather are renewed, and I would not be a good steward if I told you that a renewed promise in this city has ever been broken. Nor would I be a good steward if I told you it is safe."

Afterwards, in the yard, in the dark, with the death bell going and Quen waiting with a lamp for the walk back up to the gate, Aren said: "You told me a bench could be bought in a matter of this kind."

"I said the bench cannot be bought in a matter of this kind. That is the same sentence with a different door in it." She walked. "You went to that room with a question and you came out with a promise and a debt and a crack in a bridge, and you said the thing about the north's road before anybody asked you for it, and you offered not to put this city in a document without the bench's word, and do you know what the first three things were?"

"Work, a job, and my word."

"No," said Quen. "They were the exact terms you came in with, in a different order, from a different mouth. I had them at the slip this morning. I did not tell you. It is what I do and it is what this yard does, and I will not apologise for it, and if you have learned one thing here it is that a man in a strange city who wants something from a strange city has a duty to know who else in the strange city is selling it to him." She stopped at the gate. "Go and be useful in the morning."

He was useful in the morning.

For six days he was the most useful man in Bellweather, which is a thing that happens to strangers and is never the same afterwards. The bench put him on the second pier because the bench was short of hands, and the bench is not a body of heroes, it is a body of tired men, and it had lost the north trade and had nobody to send north and had a stranger standing in the hall who could read a crack in a pier the way a man reads a roof. He worked. He went down on the chain in a cold river four fingers below the springing of the cutwater, and he put his hands on the outer ring stone by stone, and he found the eight inches, and he found that they were not growing, and he found the thing he had half expected and could not have said out loud, which is that the ring stone was sound and the mortar behind it had been raked out from the inside by water that came up under the arch and out through the spandrel, and there was no repair to that which did not begin with the arch coming out of the water for a season.

He told them that, in one hour, in the trade's words, on the third day, and Quen wrote it down, and the bench read it, and nobody said one word about it in his hearing, and the next morning there was a bench order that the pier be marked twice a year and that the marking be done by two men and a clerk and kept in the hall, and Aren asked who had put that order and was told it was the man with the mallet, and that it had not been asked for by anybody.

He carried timber. He learned the arch the way a man learns a face, from underneath, and he learned the toll schedule, and he learned which of the forty-one toll-houses was which, and he learned what a fifth bell is for. He went out on the third morning along the raised road north with a boy from the slip as far as the old tide mark, four miles of good paved causeway with a drainage ditch on each side, and at four miles the road came down off the embankment into reed, and the reed went on for two miles in every direction, and the boy said, cheerfully, that the road goes that way because the river turns, and that if you want the north road you must come back round by the fish weir, and that it was a fine day's walk, and that strangers always wanted to see where the road stopped.

At the weir he turned round, and looked back south at a city with a broken arch across four miles of fen, and understood with total clarity that there was no road here that went to Merefen, and that there was going to be a bell ringing in Merefen on some night when he was not standing under it, or that he was going to have to be the kind of man who can find a door nobody built.

He tried, in the end, on the sixth day, and what he found was the gate and the ladder and the water.

On the sixth day he had a paper in his coat that nobody had signed because there was nobody here to sign it, and a bad hand, and a fortnight's wages, and a scar on his palm that had a black line through it. He had looked for a while. What he had not found was a way out, and the reason he had not found it was not that it was hidden. It was that nobody in that city had ever needed one, and a hundred and forty people had asked him questions in six days and not one of them had been *how does a man leave*.

In the yard, at the end of the sixth day, they cut a new shear pin and set it in the new garboard fastening and clinched it, and it went in true, and Quen looked at it and made the boy look at it, and said, "That is the only iron in this city with your work in it, and it is a pin, and it will be under the water in eight months. Do not be sentimental about it."

Aren Kest stood in a ferry slip in a city that was not in the world and looked at a pin, and thought about a sill in a fen town, repacked in lime with his own mark chalked in the bed of it, and found that he had no idea, and no witness, and no way home.
