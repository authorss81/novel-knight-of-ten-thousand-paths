# Chapter 0029

The ninth of Greenmonth was a Friday and a market day, and the Merefen bridge guild sat at the ninth hour in the long room over the wharf with the market going on outside the shutter, and Tovan Kest was in it, and he was not a surety, and he had known that for three weeks and had not said so to anybody, and had therefore discovered what it was like to be in a room where a vote was being taken about a man and be there as a brother.

There were eight men on the bench and the reeve of Merefen, who had a minute to write, and a clerk with the roll. Tovan Kest sat on a stool at the end, near the door, where a man who is a party and not a member gets put, and the putting had been done without anybody saying anything, which was how this town did things.

The market outside was loud. Under the wharf the water was going over the Sedge Reach four bays and the millers were shouting, and there was a man selling eels at the top of the ramp with a pitch, and the whole of it was the best market this town has had since the sixth of Mudmonth, and every man in the room had worked out by the second hour that a good market is not a reason to be pleased on a morning like this and none of them said it out loud.

Nobody spoke about the bell.

That was the thing Tovan Kest noticed first and thought about for a week afterwards. The bell was in a storehouse by the fish weir under a Crown seal, the cradle on the Old Toll abutment was empty and going green, the finding of the fourth of Greenmonth said the seizure was lawful and the trust was in abeyance, and the bench of the Merefen bridge guild sat for two hours and a half and did not mention it once. They voted on a bond. It was on the roll. It had been on the roll since the eleventh of Mudmonth.

That was the whole of the business, and it took two hours and a half, and by the end of it Tovan Kest had worked out that the guild was not avoiding the bell. It was doing the only thing a bench can do, which is to take one vote about one thing a man can be held to, and a bench that takes a vote about a crown instead of a vote about six marks has stopped being a bench and become a letter.

---

The clerk read the bond, because a clerk reads, and Tovan Kest had heard it read in a yard on the evening of the sixth of Greenmonth by the same man in the same voice, and he discovered that he was watching the clerk's mouth instead of listening, which is what a man does with a thing he has already heard.

"And the bench has voted," said the man in the chair. "The third."

That was the whole of the announcement. There was no count, because a bench of a guild does not count, it sits, and every man in the room knew what the third option was before the clerk had finished the first sentence of it, and the vote was over before Tovan Kest had decided what he was going to say.

That was his fault and he worked it out inside a minute.

"The minute will say," the man in the chair was saying, "that the surety proposed the third himself, at the ninth hour, and withdrew from the room before the vote, and is not in it now and has not been in it since the half hour. And it will say the reeve was here and said so at the half hour. I want those two in one place, because in a year a man is going to open that roll and he is going to have to choose between them, and he should not have to."

"He will not have to choose," said Reeve Aldis Fenn, from the end of the table, without moving. "He will have both, in one hand, in my minute, and a finding from a magistrate in a county file two hundred miles off. They do not agree with each other and they were written three days apart, and that is the honest state of this town and I would like it kept that way. The town loses four or five marks a year and a market. I have costed it. It is not a disaster, and I am not going to be thanked for saying so in a room."

She said that in nine sentences where she had taken twenty in the morning, and Tovan Kest understood that she had said the long version to the bench and the short one to him, later, on a stair, and that she was a woman who did that on purpose.

---

He had got up. He had got up before the vote, which he had not planned, and which he worked out afterwards was the only thing in the last ten weeks that had come out of him by itself.

"Before you take it," he said. "I have no vote in this and I am not going to take one, and I would like one thing said into the minute before you do, and it is not an opinion. The bond is six marks. The road is shut. Both of those were true at the ninth hour this morning. And the man the bond is about is four days off under an arch and cannot be fetched. That is four facts and none of them is going to be changed by a vote, and I would like a man in a year to be able to read that this room knew all four of them when it voted."

Several men turned round.

"Master Kest, you are a party to this roll," said the clerk.

"I am, and I am not voting, and I have said why and I will say it again in a minute, and I want the four facts entered first, because in a year the man who reads this roll is going to find a vote and a brother standing in the room, and he is going to assume one of those two is the reason for six marks, and it is neither."

Nobody spoke for a count of three.

"Sit down," said the man in the chair, and Tovan Kest sat down, and worked out afterwards that the three seconds had been the whole difference between a party and a man who had made a point, and that he had got them by not adding anything to them.

And then he stood up again, because there was one more thing, and he had been in that room for two hours and a half and had not asked it, and he was going to be asked it by a man in a yard on Friday.

"I am not voting, and I am not going to vote, and I am telling the bench why in a public room so that it is on the record that I did not sit on it out of modesty. The advance of two marks and three quarters is on this roll and it is mine, and the surety is my brother, and a man on both sides of a thing cannot weigh it, and that is not a principle, it is the reason for the rule. The bench has the roll. Use the roll."

The clerk wrote it, and asked him to spell something, and Tovan Kest spelled it, and the clerk asked him to put his hand to the minute, and he did, at the bottom, under the clerk and not over the clerk, and his hand went on it in a trade's hand and not a brotherly one, and that was the correct place for it.

---

It was over at the eleventh hour and a half, and the market had gone, and the room emptied, and Tovan Kest stood in it with the man in the chair and the clerk and the reeve of Merefen, who had not left, and who had not spoken for two hours and a half, which Tovan Kest understood was the whole of what she had come for.

"Your brother was here at the ninth hour," said the reeve, "and he left before the vote."

"He was here."

"I have minuted that. And I would like you to know that the minute is worth more than anything anybody said in that room, including the vote, and I have never had a minute in my life that was worth that and I have had forty years of them." She had her gloves in her hand. "And I would like to say one thing to you that I have not said to anybody in this town, and I am going to say it to you because you are the only man here who has told me in the last three weeks what the yard is for, which is paying a man for a day's work without asking him what he thinks about a bell."

---

"Master Kest. It is the tenth of Greenmonth and I have your brother's thing."

The clerk of the guild came into the yard at the fourth hour with a sheet of paper folded in four, and it was the ninth page of the week-book from Aren's own hand, copied out into the clerk's hand, and the clerk had put his own name under it and the date and the hour, and the guild's chest had it, and Tovan's week-book had it, and the Archive's press over a salt merchant had it.

"You said to tell you to enter it tomorrow," said the clerk. "So I have entered it. I have entered the whole of it: a seat in the fourth course, two discs of Steppe glass set across a mark in the stone, a pin and an oak collar, the maker's hand at the foot with an account of how it fails, the ninth of Greenmonth, a half hour past the ninth hour, a purpose set down in the man's own words, and one line at the foot of his own which says that he has no person to witness what he is about to do and that he has put a page in three books instead. And I have entered *no reason given*, and I have entered nothing else, and I will not be asked for a reason, and I would like to say that I have entered three things on this roll this month with the words *no reason given* and I have begun to think of them as the guild's standing joke, and I have written in my own book that I hope in five years somebody finds the three and reads them together."

"Thank you," said Tovan Kest.

"Master Kest, I am going to say a thing and then I am going to go and cut a face stone." The clerk put the sheet down on the bench and squared it. "Your brother put six marks of his own wages on this roll on a Friday morning, on his way out of a room, and every man in that room including me thought that was a fine thing, and I have thought about it for a day. And I have had four marks of work in this town in a fortnight and I have had a bench order about a cup-board in the guild house and a handrail for nothing, and I have had one true thing given to me this month and it was a sheet of paper that a man asked me to copy because he was not going to be here to hold it.

"That is what this office is. I do not know what anybody else thinks it is. I have a rule on a roll about sworn tradesmen's hands and it is the only rule in this building I have ever believed, and I believed it in a yard in Mudmonth when a road warden put three lines on the back of a factor's paper and would not let me put my name on the clerk's figure, and he was right and I have thought about it every day since. So I have entered your brother's page and I have not asked him for a reason and I am not going to, and if it comes to a thing in a bench-room in a year's time, there will be a man in this town who can say exactly what was set on a bridge on the eighth of Greenmonth, at what hour, and whose hand was on it. And that is worth more than six marks and it is worth more than a bell, and I am the clerk of a guild of bridgewrights in a fen town and I have never in my life been able to say a thing like that in a room and be listened to, so I have said it in a yard, to one man, on a Saturday, and you may do as you like with it."

He went and cut a face stone.

Tovan Kest stood in his own yard for a while afterwards and worked out what had just been said to him, and what it meant, and that it was the second time in a month that a man in this town had come to him correctly.

---

The discovery came on the eleventh, at the seventh hour, and it came from a man who was not a clerk.

It came from a carrier who wanted eight setts cut at a price, and Tovan Kest said the yard was full until the twenty-eighth, and the carrier said, in the way a man says a thing he has been carrying about for a week:

"You were full before the ninth."

"We were."

"You were full in Mudmonth, weren't you. You had the reach and the old toll and the wharf kerb."

"We had," said Tovan Kest.

"I have been in this market eleven years," said the carrier, "and I have a cart, and I go where there is work, and I am telling you the thing nobody in this town is saying, which is that this yard has had more in it since the sixth of Mudmonth than it has had in the four years I have been coming to it, and I know exactly why and it is not because the bridge is any good."

"Two of my crew were on the Sedge Reach scour for eleven days and I never saw the money for it," said Tovan Kest.

"Aye. But somebody came and looked at the hole." The carrier shifted a set on his shoulder. "That is the trade, that is all. A man comes down a street and looks at a thing that is wrong and says a figure out loud, and everybody behind him hears it and somebody with a cart takes his work and somebody with a yard cuts it. And the guild's man with the levels and the bench and the writ about a cracked bell — he is the reason there is a queue at your gate, and everybody in this market knows it, and none of them would say it, and I have been in this town eleven years and I have never seen a town say a true thing to a man's face, and I have come to say it, and I am saying it badly, and I am saying it because you are the only one of you who has ever told me the rate."

He went off up Weir Street with the setts cut for nothing, which is a thing men do on Weir Street.

Tovan Kest stood in the yard.

He had built the yard as a place that was not a question. He had said so in a doorway, to a bench carpenter and a town clerk and, in front of four men, in a yard, on the seventh of Greenmonth: *you're the one people ask.* And it had been true, and it had been the only true thing about himself for eleven years, and he had built a gate out of a career and put it in front of a brother and then found out that the brother was the reason the gate had anything behind it.

He had spent a month being paid for a day's work without being asked what he thought about a bell.

It was not a coincidence and it was not injustice, and there was not one word to be said about it in either direction, and the yard's only defence — the one that was true, and that he would be able to say in a room in a year, and would say in a room in a year — was that it had been paid for a contract, at a rate, on a Friday, with a factor's name at the top of the paper and a third party's name in the second column and a clerk's hand on a copy.

Not a favour. A contract. A yard is a thing that is paid for or it is a favour, and if it is a favour then it can be stopped by one man, and one man had been under an arch for twenty days and had come back on the sixteenth and would find four men on a stone contract and a bond on a roll and a bench that had voted behind his back, and none of it would be a favour.

---

The letter came on the twelfth of Greenmonth, on the Monday, at the second hour, and it came to the town trust and not to the guild and not to the reeve, and it was brought by a man off a provincial post, and it had a Crown clerk's hand on it and a county seal, and it was addressed:

*To the road-keeper of Merefen, or such officer as now holds that office, in the matter of the Bellweather hinge.*

Tovan Kest read it standing in the wharf office with the reeve of Merefen in the room, because there was nowhere else in this town to read a Crown letter, and the reeve had come in at the second hour for the market wages and had stayed.

"'Such officer as now holds that office,'" said Reeve Aldis Fenn. "That is the second time this month a Crown paper has used a title this county cannot find."

"The finding says there is no office of keeper of the Bellweather hinge in the town's charter, in the county's list, or in the Crown's schedule," said Tovan Kest. "There is no office of road-keeper of Merefen either. There is a guild post called a road warden and it is elected in this room in Frostmonth and it is not an office anybody can be summoned to."

"So the letter," said the reeve, "is addressed to a man who does not exist, in a matter concerning a bell that this town has been told it lawfully lost, and it is sent to a trust that has been in abeyance since the fourth, and there is nobody in this town who can receive it."

"There is nobody in this town who can *answer* it," said Tovan Kest.

They looked at each other across a table for a moment, and both of them were thinking about a magistrate, and neither of them said anything about it, because it was a working room and there were wages in it.

Then Tovan Kest said: "I will take it to the toll-house."

"Give it to me," said the reeve.

"No," said Tovan Kest. "Ma'am, with respect, and I know what I am saying. If the town trust receives it, then the town trust has received a Crown paper about a thing the town trust no longer holds, and the receipt is a fact about this town's knowledge and it will be read in a room in the provincial town as this town having been told something and holding on to it. If the reeve's office receives it, then the reeve's office has received a Crown paper addressed to a man who does not exist, and a reeve who receives a paper addressed to nobody is a reeve who has admitted there is nobody, and you have spent eleven years on the other half of that. So it does not get received by anybody, and it goes in a drawer in the toll-house, because the toll-house is where the other Crown paper in this town is, under a grey seal, on a post, and it is the only place in Merefen where a Crown's paper has ever been kept, and a thing kept in the same drawer as the other thing is at least kept."

Reeve Aldis Fenn looked at him for a moment and then said, "That is correct and I had already worked it out and I am annoyed about it."

---

The toll-clerk of Merefen was an old man with a chest and a good hand, and he took the letter, and he read the address twice, and he said:

"There is no such man."

"There is no such office."

"There is also no such man," said the toll-clerk, "which is the half they will use. There is a man in this town and everybody knows his name and every one of us has been in a room with him, and if a Crown paper says *such officer as now holds that office* and there is no office, then in a year's time a clerk is going to write *no such office* in a book and the man is going to be nothing to do with it, and that is how a man is made to disappear out of a file while he is standing in the room." He put the letter down on the counter. "It goes in the drawer. And I am telling you what I am going to do, because you have brought it to me instead of to a door, and that is worth an answer.

"I am going to put it in the drawer under the book that is on the fish weir, because that is where the Crown's paper in this town lives. And I am going to write on the outside of the drawer, in chalk, in this office's hand, the day, the hour, and what it is: *Crown letter, twelfth of Greenmonth, YR 312, second hour. Addressed to the road-keeper of Merefen. No such office in this county. Not received. Not answered.* And I am going to put the word *not answered* on the outside of a drawer in chalk, where a man in this town can read it, and I am going to do that because I am sixty-eight and I have kept this town's book for forty years and the only thing I have that is worth anything is that if I write a thing down, it is still there when the men who said it are gone.

"And then it will cost us," said the toll-clerk, "and I would rather it cost us than not happen, because the other way is a drawer with a Crown's letter in it and nothing on the outside, and in ten years a man opens the drawer and says it was never there, and there would be no reason why not."

Tovan Kest stood at a counter in a toll-house and felt the exact weight of what he had just done, which was nothing at all, and which was the correct amount.

The reeve had said nothing. She was standing by the window with her back to the room, and when he turned round she had her face turned to the glass.

"You have chalked *not answered* on a Crown's letter," she said, to the window, "in a town where the market board has had a rule over every line but one since the first of Greenmonth. Do you know how many people will read that drawer."

"Yes," said Tovan Kest. "That is the point of it."

And then he went and did the other thing, which was the other thing he had come out of the toll-house for, and which he did in a yard, at the seventh hour, in front of four men and a fifth man doing cartage.

"You have all heard the bench," he said. "Six marks at a shilling a day. And a trust in abeyance for a year, which means the Old Toll crossing has no owner, which means there is no work coming off it, and there is no work coming off the wharf road, and the wharf kerb is the trust's and the trust has nothing until the frost. So I am telling you where the yard stands and I am telling you at the seventh hour on a Monday, and not on a Friday when it is a matter of wages.

"The stone is cut. Nine tons of setts for a factor and two tons on a field, and a contract with his name at the top and a rate and a term and a month in it, and the cutting is not finished, and the stone does not unsplit. So the cutting finishes, whatever comes, and your wages come out of the cutting and not out of anything else, and on the Friday after delivery the factor's money is due at the mill and the yard is square with him and the wages are paid, and that is the whole of my promise and it is the only one I am going to make this month.

"And if he does not come for the second load, then I owe four men a fortnight that I have not got, and I have told them that this morning and I have told them in the same words, and I would rather tell a man on a Monday that a thing might not happen than tell him on a Friday after he has moved his family.

"And there is work coming that I do not like, and I am taking it, and I will tell you why, because I have found out in the last ten days that the only thing this yard has ever sold is a queue, and the queue is a man who comes down a street and looks at something that is wrong and says a figure out loud. That man is my brother and he is on the other side of a door at this hour, and I have spent a month building this gate so that nothing of his came through it, and I have found out that nothing of his comes through the gate and the gate has four men behind it, and I would rather say that to four men in my own yard than have it said for me."

The fourth man said, "The toll-house's kerb?"

"The toll-house's kerb, and the north approach to the fish steps," said Tovan Kest, "and it is a creditor's money and it is going in a creditor's book, and the yard is on that book's page for the first time in nineteen years, and I have thought about that for a day and I am telling you that I have thought about it, so that nobody can say afterwards I took it without thinking, and I would like one of you to ask me about it now if anybody wants to, and I will answer it now, because I will not answer it on a Friday."

Nobody asked him about it.

Then the stone went on the trestles and the sound of iron on stone was every four seconds, and the five men were in the yard by twenty past the hour, and the iron did not stop for anything.

---

He was standing at the gate at the ninth hour on the fifteenth of Greenmonth with the master's tally on the back of his hand and four marks and a quarter against a contract and a stone and a lime and a boy's hour, and the wharf road shut, and the cradle on the Old Toll abutment empty, and the post sealed, and a board in the market with a rule over every line and one line under it without one.

The sixteenth was tomorrow.

He had known it since the eighth, and he had said it in a yard on the eighth, and the yard had been empty on the ninth because it was a market day, and the town had gone to market, and there would not be a road warden in the square, and there would be a clerk in a provincial town writing a word in a book.

He had a sheet in the week-book under the ninth page with three hands on it, and a copy in the guild's chest, and a copy in a press over a salt merchant.

Tomorrow the sixteenth, at the first light, a quarter of a mile up the Old Toll road, there would be a bridge with two pieces of Steppe glass in a seat cut square in the fourth course, and a man would come up out of it or he would not.

And if he came, he would come with a fever on him, because he had come that way once and it was three days and it broke on the thirtieth of Mudmonth and there is no arguing with three.

And Tovan Kest, who was twenty-five years old, and had a yard with four men in it, and six marks of his brother's wages on a guild roll at a shilling a day, and a factor's contract that a man in a counting house had promised to keep, and a Crown's letter in a drawer in a toll-house with *not answered* chalked on the outside of it, stood at his own gate in the dark and did the only arithmetic a carpenter does.

"If he is not there by the first hour," he said, to nobody, "then I send a boy up the road at the second to look at the seat, and if the seat is whole and the glass is whole and there is no man, then there is nothing in this town to be done about it and I will not do anything about it, and I will have lost a day and a boy's wage."

"And if he is there," he said, "then he is a man with a fever on him and no wage and a bench that voted behind his back, and I will put him in the room at the back of the yard, which is the one I keep for a man who is ill, and I will tell Wenna before she has it out of four other mouths by the fourth hour, and I will not say one word to him about a road for as long as he is in that room."

The river went over four bays in the dark and answered nothing, which was the correct condition of things, and Tovan Kest went in and shut the yard door and hung the tally on its nail with the mark's name on it and the hour beside it, in his own hand, in a yard that had four men in it and work that had a third party's name on it, and a bridge with a bell-shaped hole in the parapet where the cradle used to be, and a Crown's seal on a post, and a seat in the sill four hundred yards up the road that a man had cut square at the tenth hour at night ten days ago and had told him, on that bridge, in the dark, that he did not know what was at the other end of it.
