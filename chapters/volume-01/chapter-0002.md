# Chapter 0002

The bell had grown into its cradle.

That was the first thing Aren found out at half past five on the morning of the eleventh, standing on the near abutment of the Old Toll crossing in the blue dark with his hands on the cold bronze of it. Eleven years of rain and eight of nobody lifting it, and the tongue had settled into the lip of the yoke until the two had become a thing that had been made that way. He put his thumb in the gap and levered, and the bell gave him a quarter inch, and the split lip rode over the headstock and touched the iron yoke, and the note that came out of it was not a ring. It was a knock. A dull knock, like a man rapping the table at somebody who has already been told.

"It's not coming off like that," said Tovan.

He was standing four paces off with his own back against the parapet and a bar in his hand, and he had three journeymen with him, and it was the first time in four years the two of them had been on the same structure before breakfast. Tovan Kest was twenty-five and had been running the Merefen crews since he was nineteen, and he had his father's forearms and none of his father's patience, and he slept with his boots under the bed, which is a bridgewright's habit and not a soldier's.

"It has to come off like that," Aren said. "It's nine stone and there's a bar through the headstock loop and two men on the ends, and if the lip goes face-up it goes into the channel. That's the whole of the operation. The operation is deciding which way nine stone falls."

"I know which way nine stone falls. I've been doing it since I was fourteen." Tovan shifted the bar against his shoulder. "I'm saying it will take us till noon and the clerk will be here at eight."

"Then the clerk can watch."

Below them, out of the shadow of the arches, a flat-bottomed ferry came up the channel broadside with the current, and there was a woman in the stern with a pole longer than she was. She put the pole against the pier and stopped the hull with a sound like a sigh.

"Wenna," Aren said.

"Wenna," Tovan agreed. "And eleven of her people standing on the wrong bank of the old crossing with carts because your bridge is shut to everything but feet and the raised road to her slip is under a foot of water, and she would like the old crossing kept like it is, and she has asked me, twice, in front of witnesses, which she'd not do if it wasn't true."

"She'd not do it if it wasn't true," Aren said. "She's a fair woman. Tell her I'll walk her people across at the change of the tide myself and carry what they can't carry."

"That's tomorrow. Today the clerk comes at eight and takes the bell off the cradle, and after that the crossing is a crossing with nothing on it and a notice nailed to the post, and your repair marks are on the sill of a bridge that everybody's been told is a ruin."

"Then they'll have a notice on a bridge I'm working on, and I'll still be working on it." He put his shoulder under the headstock loop and felt the weight go onto his back. "Bar's there. Take the other end and lift on my count, and don't twist it, whatever you hear."

They lifted it. It came up a quarter inch, then a half, and the lip found the yoke and knocked again, and the sound went out under the arches and along the channel and was answered by nothing.

"Mind the crack," Tovan said.

"Keep it off the deck side. If it goes over the low side it takes the parapet."

They set it on the sill stones, nine stone of split green bronze standing on its own head on the cut stone that Aren had repacked in the autumn, and the crack showed pale where the light got into it, a long hairline from the lip up over the shoulder, and inside the split there was a seam of something dull and red that was not rust and did not come off on a cloth.

"You can see daylight through that," Tovan said.

"You can see a good deal through that. It's the reason it's six marks instead of twelve." Aren straightened up and looked at the crack for a moment longer than the metal deserved. "There's bell-metal in the founder's yard at Bellweather that never cracked at all, and this is the one they got in the river."

"There's no Bellweather."

"There's a crack through the mouth of it," Aren said, and did not say the rest, which was that the split had not gone inward from a blow. It had gone outward, the way a bell goes when a man rings it hard enough to be answered, and he had been turning that over in his mind for ten days without arriving anywhere.

The clerk of the Merefen bridge guild came at eight with a boy carrying a ledger. He was forty-odd and had a way of standing that made a notice look like furniture, and he read the notice out loud on the abutment for the benefit of anyone who wanted to learn something.

"Removal of unclassified salvage from the Old Toll crossing, at the guild's direction, at the guild's charge, to the guild's yard. Held under municipal trust. Valuation, six marks, less the split." He turned the paper round. "This is dated this morning."

"Yes," Aren said.

"I expect it was," said the clerk, in a voice with no opinion in it at all. "It's signed by the bench clerk and dated this morning, and it will be at the sitting on the twenty-fourth, and the guild does not attend to a matter twice." He looked at the bell standing on its head on the sill, and at Aren's chalk, and at the bar. "You've put a hold on it. Your own form. I see it in your book."

"Twenty-fourth."

"Twenty-fourth." The clerk wrote something in the ledger, and did not read it out. "The trust requires a surety for anything in its care at a valuation. Six marks."

"The chest," said Aren. "My tool-chest. I'll stand the bond on it."

It was out before the clerk had finished the sentence, and Aren heard it go, and understood about half a second later that he had walked onto a bridge at eight in the morning with a decision already made and had not known it was in his coat.

"That's three and a half," said the clerk, "and half of it is rust." He waited, pen up, with the enormous patience of a man who is paid by the hour for the whole day.

"It's good for six marks," Aren said.

"Mm," said the clerk, and wrote it down. "Then the removal stands against your bond and the bond stands against your chest, and the matter waits for the twenty-fourth, and if you have sold the metal in the meantime that's a matter for the trust and not for me."

Nobody had said that he was going to sell the metal. The boy with the ledger looked up. Tovan, behind the bell, said nothing at all, which for Tovan was a shout.

"It wants a witness," said the clerk, without looking up from the page. "A bond is no good without a man who saw the metal change hands into your keeping. You have had the metal. Who saw it?"

Aren did not turn round. There was one man on an old bridge at eight in the morning, and the clerk and the ledger boy could not be it, and the only man who could be it was the one standing at the other end of the bar, who was owed six marks by this matter and wanted it very badly.

"You'll do, brother," he said. "Put your name under mine, and mean it, and I'll not argue with you about it afterwards."

Tovan looked at the ledger for a moment, and then took the pen, and signed it, because he was a witness and a witness does not get to have an opinion, and a Kest writes his name the same whichever way up the paper is. The clerk wrote the hour, and ruled the line, and turned the ledger round so that both signatures sat under the six marks in a fair row, and Tovan gave the pen back without a word and went to take his end of the bar.

That was the whole of his part in it. He would remember, for a long time, that he had not once been asked whether he agreed with the bond, and that he had signed a piece of paper which said that on the eleventh of Mudmonth nine stone of unclassified municipal salvage had passed into the keeping of Aren Kest, and that neither of them had mentioned a yard.

They put it back on the cradle in the same three minutes it had taken to come off, because the tongue had settled into the lip and there was nothing to be done about that until a man with a chisel and a day could argue with it, and that was not a job anybody in Merefen was going to be paid for. The lip knocked the yoke once on the way down, and neither of them was holding the bell while it did it, because the yoke took the weight and both of them had let go to get their hands out from under the headstock.

They stood clear. Tovan went to the rail to look at the water and put his elbows on it. Aren went down onto the sill with his back to the abutment and his hands full of the bar, the wedge, and the rag he had been using to keep the rust off the yoke, and neither of them was within an arm's length of nine stone of green bronze.

The bell gave a note.

It was not a ring. It was the same dull knock as before, and it came out of the split and not out of the mouth, and it was not loud, and it lasted about the time it takes to let a breath out. It was the sound of a cracked bell being struck by nothing at all, on a wet morning, under three arches, with the river going past underneath.

Tovan came off the rail.

"Did you hear that."

"I heard it."

"There was a wind in it. There is always a wind in it. That yoke has been moving in that hole since Thawmonth and a man could put a finger in the crack and the wind would take it and—" Tovan stopped, because he had put his hand in his coat pocket and left it there.

"A starter would look at it," Aren said, to the sill, not to his brother.

"Would he."

"A starter would look at it and put his thumb in the crack and wait for the next one, and if there was a next one he would take the bell down and have it examined by a man, and there is no man." He got the rag out of his pocket and wiped his hands on it, slowly, the whole of both of them, which took longer than the job required. "And I would rather not take the bell down, and I have not thought about why, and I have thought about everything else in this town all week."

The clerk and the ledger boy went off down the road toward the foundry, and the two Kest brothers were left alone on an old bridge at eight in the morning with the water going past underneath at eleven fingers and rising.

"Right," Tovan said, and unhooked the bar. "Then the metal. Six marks, and the roll's right, because the roll's always right about metal, that's the one thing the guild's good for. The yard'll take it today. I'll have it in the furnace before noon and there's no argument to be had about it, because you don't owe a cracked bell anything." He waited, and the offer sat there in the wet air between them, honest, and generous, and the only way out. "You're a road warden in a fen town. What are you doing with a bell?"

Aren looked at the water going under the third arch.

"Six marks," he said, "buys the bronze and nothing else. The notice is on the bridge whether the metal's in my yard or in a founder's, and the guild's clerk has got a name to write the delay against, and the delay is what I want. If the metal goes in the yard, it's a bell somebody else owns by the middle of the week, and I'll have given it away for four and a half, and the notice comes back at the twenty-fourth anyway and there's nothing on the bridge to put against it but a chalk mark and a bad word." He took the bar from Tovan and set it down. "The bond puts it in my name in front of the guild. That's a different kind of paper. I'd rather be answerable for it than be free of it, and you can put that down to a bad season and a bad head, because that's what it is."

"You can put it down to whatever you like." Tovan took up the bar again and shouldered it, and his voice changed, which it did about twice a year. "The second the fever came down I asked you what a bell ringing in a storm was for, and you said don't write, there's nothing to put in a letter. You said it the way men say a thing they've been given, and I've had eleven years to decide whether you were given it or whether you had it ready." He shifted the bar on his shoulder. "You'll be the one they sent for. That's all. Whatever this is, you'll be the one they send for, because you're the one who's good, and being good is how they get you." He nodded at the bond in the clerk's ledger. "I can live with six marks against your chest. I can't live with you making yourself the only man in Merefen with a name on a paper about that thing."

"Tovan."

"You were the one they asked to hold it." He said it flat, to the river, and then he shouldered the bar and went down the steps two at a time toward his own yard, and left it there.

Aren stood on the near abutment alone with a bell and a bar.

The next three hours he worked the sill.

That was his own project and the only thing he had touched on this bridge in two years, and he had touched it because it was cut stone laid by a man who knew his trade, and the third and fourth lengths from the abutment had worked loose where the water came under the corner, and he had repacked them in the autumn with three courses of setts and a good lime bed. Now he went over the two lengths that were still proud, and cut the lime out of the joints where it had gone to sand, and re-packed them, and chalked the sill with his date and his mark the way a mason chalks a foundation, low down where the flood would keep the record.

It is the least dramatic work in the world. It is also the only work in his trade that anybody in Merefen has ever paid him to do, and while he was on his knees in it with the rain coming off the arch above him in a sheet, he could not think about the bell at all, and that was worth the morning.

Wenna Koll came up the slip at noon with a basket on her hip and the ferry's rope over her shoulder.

"You walked my people across," she said. "Don't tell me you didn't, I watched you do it at the change of the tide, and I've a boy with a crutch he can't get along a deck in a swell and he got along it fine."

"It was eleven crossings."

"It was eleven crossings and no toll, which you'll hear about." She looked at the bell on its cradle, and then at the sill, and then at the two men on the arch above them who had stopped working to look down at the noise. "That rang itself. Not an hour since. Ten minutes, and I heard it from the slip with eleven people standing on the steps, and the men up there heard it, and the child with the crutch heard it and put his hand over his ears, and it is not a story and it is not for you to make it one, and there has been a great deal of talk about it already."

"I know. I was under the arch. The tongue's settled in the lip; it catches when the yoke settles and the wind gets behind it."

"That's one explanation."

"That's the one I'd give a bench."

Wenna had the face of a woman who had grown up on a channel and had heard a great many explanations. "It's got a different crack than it had, is what the men on the arch are saying. And the cooper's woman says a bell that was rung at your Bellweather, in the year before the fever, for a man drowned in the channel — well. You tell me a bell is metal and I'll tell you a bell is metal, and I'll also tell you we're the ones who ring it. It's what we do. We ring for the drowned. It isn't a fair thing to say out loud in a town that's decided whether we owe them anything, but we do it, and we've used that bell because there isn't another one on this reach that anybody will leave alone."

She shifted the basket on her hip.

"And a woman came down to my slip yesterday afternoon," she said, "and asked me who'd signed the sill. Not the guild, not the trust. The sill. She had a clerk's way of asking, like the answer was a number."

Aren sat back on his heels with lime on both hands.

"Did she say what for?"

"No. She said she'd come back." Wenna looked up at the two men on the arch, and lowered her voice a little. "Aren. Your bell rang with nobody near it, in a town of four thousand souls, and by noon there'll not be a soul in it that doesn't know whose name is on the hold. Whatever you signed on Friday, that's what the town'll have decided by this evening. So you'd better have meant it when you signed it."

"I meant it," he said.

He went back down onto the sill and cut out another foot of lime bed, and the rain came off the arch in a sheet, and the bell hung above his head on a yoke it had grown into, and it did not ring again, and he found that he was listening for it anyway.
