# Chapter 0016

The mail boat from Merefen was a punt with a man who poled it and a woman who sold milk, and it came down the Sable in the slack between the floods, and Lysa Fenn got off it at the provincial landing at the fourth hour of the afternoon with her saddlebag over her shoulder and her hands going at the third hour of the day, in a town that had heard about the bell at the ninth hour of the morning and would hear the rest of it by the fourth hour of tomorrow.

Her rooms were two, over the county clerk's office, on a stair that any of the clerks in that building could climb in ninety seconds, which was the first fact about a magistrate and the only one that mattered to anybody who wanted to be let in. She had chosen them for that reason in her first year and had never regretted it and had understood within two years that it was also the reason the reeve's office regarded her as a subsidy for people who would not govern themselves, which was a sentence she had heard twice and had never once been able to answer with anything but the fact that she could be climbed to at ninety seconds and so could every person who had a grievance and a hat.

She put the bag down, took off her gloves, and sat at a table that was better than the chair.

Then she did the thing she had come down the river to do, which was to write down, in her own hand, in a record that would leave the building, the fact that nobody could produce the authority for a lawful act.

It took the whole of the rest of the afternoon and it took four sheets, and it was not difficult, and that was the part she disliked. The procedure was: a magistrate's finding, in a county that had no superior court within a day's ride, is served on the provincial court and on the office concerned and is a public document the moment it is signed, and the Crown's answer, if it comes, comes in a document, and a document is slower than a man and lasts longer. She had learned that in her fifth month and had thought it a piece of luck. It was not luck. It was the whole of what a county court is for, and the reason nobody had ever used it in this matter was that using it required somebody to be willing to be a party.

She wrote it in the plainest form she could, because plainness was the only weapon in the building.

*I, Lysa Fenn, magistrate of the county of the Sable's mouth, having heard the application of the Merefen bridge guild for the return of an object seized by the watch of Merefen on the nineteenth day of Mudmonth, YR 312, and having found the seizure to be lawful procedure, further find and record as follows.*

*First. That the instrument under which the object was seized is sealed by the emergency office of the marshal, and describes that office as having authority in respect of the Alder Vale in this county, and that the office has produced nothing to this court in support of that authority, and that this court has no power to compel it to do so.*

*Second. That this court has searched the charter of the town of Merefen, the county's list of appointments, and the Crown's schedule of provincial offices, and has found no office of keeper of the Bellweather hinge, no office of road-keeper of Merefen, and no commission or appointment conferring either upon any person; and that the two instruments before it on the fourth day of Greenmonth refer throughout to a person as holding such an office, and to the object as held in his charge.*

*Third. That the emergency powers granted to this county in respect of the Alder Vale were renewed on the second day of Greenmonth, YR 312, for the term of one year, upon the same terms, and to the same officers, as in the year last past; and that the officers are not named in the finding or in any instrument before this court; and that the seizure in question was executed by this county's watch.*

*Fourth. That the object remains in this county's custody, under seal, lawfully.*

And then the last paragraph, which she had rewritten once, and which is the whole of what the afternoon was for.

*Fifth. That the authority for the fourth being unproducible, and the officers holding the third being unnamed, this court is unable to state to any party what officer of the Crown caused the seizure, by whose instruction it was made, or under what instrument the town of Merefen was found to be subject to it. That finding is made of record and is public, and any person, office, or court thereafter purporting to act in respect of the object, the hinge, the road, or any person named as keeper of any of them, is put on notice that this court has asked and has not been answered, and that the asking is dated.*

She read it four times. Then she signed it, and dated it the fourth of Greenmonth, and wrote the time, which was the seventh hour, because a finding with a time in it is a finding made on a day and not on a mood.

And then she sat with her hands flat on the table and looked at her own name at the bottom of it, and understood what she had done, which was that she had put herself in the record.

That is the mechanism. There is no more to it than that and it takes eleven years to learn and it cannot be unlearnt. Before this afternoon she had been a magistrate in a county, which is an office that anybody with a grievance and a hat can reach. After it she was a party. A party's name is on the page, and a party's finding has to be answered, and an office that acts in this matter again — and every office that wants this matter to be settled will act in it again, sooner or later, because a thing that has been done once and unanswered is a thing that invites being done twice — has now, somewhere in its own files, a document with a magistrate's name on it asking a question it did not answer. The Crown can refuse her. The Crown can overrule her. The Crown can, in the fullness of time, decide that a county magistrate of the Sable's mouth is a woman who has read a seal too many times, and remove her, and then that document will still be in the provincial court with her name at the bottom of it and the date on it, and whoever takes her chair will have to read it before they do anything at all.

That is what a record is. She had believed it since she was twenty-four and had never before had to sign one that put her own future in it.

She wrote, at the foot of the fourth sheet, a line she did not intend to serve as a finding and which she left in because a magistrate's private minutes are also a document if anybody ever asks for them:

*I record, for the assistance of whoever sits here next, that the finding above is not a victory and should not be described as one. The bell is in a storehouse. The trust of the Old Toll crossing is in abeyance for a year. The town has been told in writing on a chalk board that the law was on its side. I have gained a question and a name. The question has been asked and it will be answered at some point by somebody with a great deal of paper, or it will not be answered, and in the second case it will have cost this town twelve months and me one reappointment, and the finding will be the only thing that says so.*

---

The door was not knocked on. In five years, nobody in that building had ever knocked on a magistrate's door, and the two clerks who came in came in because the door was open, and they came in one at a time, and the second one was the man who had entered her rulings in this matter in a small excellent hand and who was in his forties and had a bad chest and a worse manner and no opinion of anybody at all, which was the correct manner for a clerk.

"Two things, ma'am. The exhibit of the thirtieth is with the provincial court's copy of the file, and the messenger has asked whether this court wants the same wrapper back, and I said that was not mine to decide."

"It is mine. Send it back with a fresh custody entry in my hand, dated today, and a second witness's line for the wrapper itself and not for the contents, and if the messenger cannot wait then he can carry it back unsealed and the gap is his, not ours."

"Mm." He wrote it. "And the county's man has been in twice."

"With what?"

"With the finding of the second. The renewal. He has asked to see the third clause of your record of this morning, and he has asked whether this court considers itself to have a standing in the matter of the emergency powers, and I have told him both times that neither question is mine to answer."

"He is a competent man," said Lysa Fenn, "and he has been sent to ask two questions he was not sent to ask, and he will go back with the answers he has been given and he will not mention that he asked them, and in four months somebody will do the same thing in a different county and it will not occur to them to ask it, and that is how a practice becomes a policy in about two years. Tell him I have no standing in the matter of the emergency powers, which is true, and that the third clause of my record is a finding of fact in a proceeding in this court, which is also true, and that he may read the second one because it is a public document, and the first one he may take to whoever can stand in it."

"Mm." He wrote that too, and then he did not leave, and stood there with his pen in his hand and his bad chest and waited, which he had never done before.

"Say it."

"The third clause renews the county's power annually to unnamed officers. If an office in the provincial town does not name the officers, and this court cannot name them, then there is nobody anywhere who can name them, and a power nobody can name is a power nobody can be asked whether it was used properly." He said it in the flat way a clerk says a thing he has checked twice. "That is not a question about a bell, ma'am. That is a question about the county. And I do not know that this court can hold anything at all on it, and I have said so to nobody but you."

"Then it will not be held by this court," said Lysa Fenn, "and I am obliged to you for bringing me a thing I have been thinking about since the ninth hour. Go home. You have a chest and it is a wet week."

He went.

She read the third clause again with the door shut, alone, and she said the sentence out loud in the room, because she was a woman who had been talking to herself in rooms for five years and had stopped apologising for it, and because the room was cold and the words helped.

"The emergency power is the hearing. The bell is the peg."

That was the true shape of the thing and she had spent four hours getting to it. Nobody in Merefen was going to be charged for a bell. The bell was an instrument: a piece of municipal salvage under a bond, on a bridge out of cart use, at a moment when a man had gone nine days missing and the town needed a story. A Crown office had done the whole thing in three days off a paper written three days before the storm, and it had worked, because the only thing that had to hold up in a fen town was a seal. The emergency power was what let a watch captain in a fen town do it: not the paper, which was unlawful to refuse on its own terms, but the standing renewal that put unnamed officers over this valley every year without anybody in the valley being asked. Every time that renewal was signed, a paper like Rell's became a lawful instrument instead of an outrageous one, and the only man who ever noticed the difference was a sergeant who wrote things down, and that was on Thursday and he had lost his stripes for it.

The renewal is a provincial fact. It happens in a room two hundred miles from a wet town by a clerk with a book. She could not touch it, she could not enjoin it, she could not refuse to sit under it, and she had just spent an afternoon writing the only sentence in this county that says a power with no names in it is a power nobody can be asked about. That sentence was now going to leave the building, and it was going into a file in a building where the annual renewal was copied out, and it would be read by a clerk in about seven weeks, in a room like this one, in the ordinary course, on his own business, and it would be the first time in eleven years that anybody had written the sentence down in a place where a clerk would come across it while copying.

That is the whole of what a magistrate can do from a county chair, and it is not nothing, and it is very nearly nothing, and she had known both of those things before this morning and had known only one of them confidently.

---

He came at the seventh hour, without knocking, which she permitted, and stood in the middle of the room with his hat in his hands and the mud of the Old Toll road on the heel of one boot, and she did not offer him the chair and he did not expect it.

"Captain Rell wrote to me at the fifth hour," she said. "The post goes up the river at six and he put it in his own hand and paid for it out of the watch fund, which is a thing I have told him never to do again and which I am not going to undo tonight."

"I know. It was in the crowd's mouth by four o'clock."

"Then you have had a more efficient service than the Crown has managed in eleven years, and I hope you enjoy it and I hope you have the sense to know that a thing being said in a market and a thing being said in a court are not the same thing and the first one is worth about fourpence." She turned the fourth sheet round and pushed it to the far side of the table so that he could read it upside down. "That is mine. It goes out tonight. You will be in a provincial file by the sixth of Longlight with a magistrate's finding in it and my name at the bottom of it, and you will not be able to get rid of it, and in a year you will be able to say, in a room, that the authority for the seizure was asked for and not answered. That is all any of this gives you and I have given it to you, and I would like you to be exact with yourself about what it is, because a man who has been handed a principle tends to find himself paying a bill for it and then blaming the person who handed it over."

"What is it worth?"

"It is worth nothing until somebody in the provincial town finds it, and it will be found by accident, and the man who finds it will decide what it is, and that is a thing you have to live with." She took the sheet back. "I am going to say two things to you and neither of them is in the record, and I am going to say them because it is four o'clock and there is no one in this building who will ever hear them, and because you have spent a fortnight being a nuisance in the most useful way anybody has ever been a nuisance in this county and there ought to be one hour in the year in which somebody says so."

She stood up and went to the window, which gave on the back of a court building and a wall and eight feet of sky, and she looked at it the way she looked at it when she was going to say a thing she had not said before.

"I took a filing from you eleven years ago. I have not mentioned it in this room and I am not going to discuss its contents, and it is a different document from the bell, and the reason it is a different document is the first thing I was ever taught and it is the reason I have spent five years doing this instead of something better paid." She turned round. "The first thing I was taught is that a document is not what it is about. A document is a thing a person put their name to, on a day, in a place, and everything else about it is somebody's interpretation, and the moment a court starts asking what a document is *about*, it stops being a court and becomes a second office competing with the first one to say what happened. Two documents about the same man are not the same document. That is all. That is a clerk's rule and it is the best rule in this kingdom and there is no clerk alive who can be trusted with it, and I was trusted with it at twenty-five, and I have not broken it yet."

"Then what," said Aren Kest, "was the first thing I was ever taught?"

"You were taught what you were sent," said Lysa Fenn. "You were twenty-one and they sent you and you did not ask, and you have spent eleven years calling that a failure, and it is not a failure, it is an obedience, and the reason it is on this page of my life at all is that I filed a paper you gave me about a bridge and I never once asked you what it was about, and because I did not ask, in Embermonth of YR 301 a man in an office was able to write a sentence that was true about a bridge and false about a city, and there was nothing in my file to stop him."

The room was quiet. Down the stair a door went and a clerk's voice said something about a file and the voice came back up the well and died.

"Now the other thing," she said, "which is the one I have never said, and I have wanted to say for about a year, and the reason I have not is that it is not modest and I would rather be thought of as careful."

She sat down on the edge of her own table, which no magistrate does, and which she had watched two of them do.

"I want the law to be useful to people who cannot afford a title or a patron. That is the whole of it. It is not a large ambition. It is smaller than wanting to be thought fair, and it is harder, because wanting to be useful is a thing you can do at a desk and wanting to be fair is a thing that happens to you when somebody is watching. Every document I sign is either a thing a poor man can use or a thing a rich man's clerk can put away, and there is no third kind, and I have signed a great many of the second kind because I was tired at the end of a day and the second kind does not come back at you." She put her hand flat on the table. "And I cannot have it. I am a county magistrate on a salary that a clerk at the crown would call a discourtesy, in a town where the reeve's office thinks I am a public grant to people who will not govern themselves, and the reason I have the chair is that my family has held something in this county for a long time, and I have never once been appointed to anything, and I have never once had to ask, and I would not know how. That is the leash, and it is a very good leash, and it is made entirely of things I am glad to have."

She got off the table and sat down in the chair properly.

"So. You have a principle and no bell, a bench mark that is a quarter of an inch wrong, a fee for a shear pin, and a town that has been told in chalk that the law was on its side. And you are about to be told by every clerk you meet that a magistrate's finding is not a road, and they will be right, and it is not. And there is a thing coming on the ninth of Greenmonth that you have been summoned to, and I am going to say what I know about it, which is very little and is this: I cannot stop it, I cannot postpone it, and I cannot be in a room in the provincial town on the ninth of Greenmonth, because I have been ordered to sit in the assizes at Dunmere on the eighth and the ninth and there are eleven cases on the list and one of them is a man who has beaten his brother and the brother is nine."

"So the summons stands and there is nobody in it to argue it."

"The summons stands and there is nobody in it to argue it, and that is the ordinary condition of a summons in this kingdom, and you should know before you go that the great majority of them are answered by nobody at all, and that a man who does not appear is not a man who has lost, he is a man who has left a gap, and a gap is a thing a court notices and a court that notices a gap will sometimes do something about it." She put the finding in a folder and stamped it. "And you will not be able to use the eleventh of Longlight. That is the date on this and the date the provincial court takes it, and the order I am signing this afternoon for an inspection of a register without fee will not be returnable until the fifteenth, which is eleven days, and eleven days is a fortnight you have to lose on somebody else's schedule, and I have known a keeper of records in this county who lost a year of a file to eleven days, twice."

"Sarn Oyelaran," said Aren.

"Who?"

"The clerk. Twenty-six. Two years in that office and she has never once been asked whether she has read a page." He said it without weight, and then he stopped, because he had heard himself.

"Say the rest of it," said Lysa Fenn.

"Nothing. It came out with the other thing."

"Yes, it did, and you did not stop it, and I have watched you stop yourself in the middle of a sentence four times this afternoon and put it back the way a man picks up a tool he has decided is not his. Go on. You have eleven days, and you have one job in them, and it is not in a court."

"What is it, then."

"Glass," said the magistrate. "You will need two discs of Steppe glass cut and annealed to a bridge pin, and money before cutting, and a man with a furnace who will not sell it to you if he cannot ask you who will be standing on the stone when it goes. And I am a magistrate of the Sable's mouth and I have no power whatever over that, and I have said it out loud in my own room anyway, because you are the only person in this county who has ever come to a court with a stone instead of a speech and there is no reason on earth for me to waste an evening on a man who is going to be told by a glasswright that he cannot pay."

She stamped the second folder, the order for the inspection, and put the date on the outside of it: returnable on the fifteenth.

"Eleven days," she said. "The office is poorer than it was on the first of Greenmonth, the file is a year weaker than it was a week ago, and you are a man in a fen town with a bond for six against a chest worth three and a half, eleven days of wages, and about a mark and a quarter in hand, and no work that anybody will pay you for until the river drops in Longlight." She squared the two folders together. "That is the position of this court on the afternoon of the fourth of Greenmonth, and I have written it down in a public record, and the public record does not contain a single sentence that will get you any of it back."

She gave him the finding to carry, which she had not intended to do and did.

"Take it to the keeper of the archive and let her read it, and let her be angry about it, and tell her I said the difference between a document and what it is about is the first thing anybody is taught and the last thing anybody keeps, and that the argument she and I had on the thirtieth was correct and I am not sorry for it and I have not been sorry for it for four days, which is as close to a compliment as I manage."

He took it and went down the stair.

The room was cold. She had not lit the second lamp and she did not now. Outside the window, in a town where a person had to climb ninety seconds of stair to reach her, a man was carrying a magistrate's finding down to a salt-loft office over a man who weighed salt for a living, and the finding was dated the fourth of Greenmonth, and it said a thing that could not be taken back, and it said it without a name in it, and that was going to turn out to be the most expensive way possible of saying it.
