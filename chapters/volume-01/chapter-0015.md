# Chapter 0015

On the fourth of Greenmonth the market came back to Merefen, and it came back wrong.

The stalls went up on the wharf road at the fifth hour in the ordinary way, and the wharf road took carts that morning because the river was down six fingers and the Sedge Reach deck was open to anything, and by the eighth hour there were more people on that road than there had been on any market day since the storm. Half of them came for the market. Half of them came because a bell had gone out of the town and a road warden had said something impossible on a market morning four days ago, and a thing like that in a fen town is not news, it is weather, and it draws.

The stair of the salt-loft house had thirty-one people on it by the ninth hour and the magistrate's clerk had to have the door to the inner room propped with a wedge, and twice during the first half hour a man at the bottom of the stair asked the question out loud, which is what a crowd does when it is in a building and not in a square.

The inner room had been cleared for the sitting. The table had been wiped and there were four chairs on this side of it, and the magistrate's clerk sat at the end with his ledger and his ink and a folded sheet he had ruled himself, and the Bellweather Claims Archive's own press stood against the wall with its bar off its seat because there was no room in which a man could stand and look at a press and not think about it.

On the table, in front of the magistrate's chair, lay three documents, and they were in order of age, and the order had been the point.

The oldest was the Merefen bridge guild's notice of removal, dated the eleventh of Mudmonth, the morning it arrived, with a bench signature on it and the six-mark valuation and the words *unclassified municipal salvage*.

The second was the sealed order of the marshal's emergency office, dated the sixteenth, with a disc of grey wax on it the size of a crown piece, and a device on the wax of a hand laid flat on a book, a road and a lamp.

The third was a claim exhibit wrapper with a four-digit number on the outside of it in a fair hand, dated the thirtieth, with a stub of grey wax showing at the corner where the paper had been folded once to close it.

Lysa Fenn came in and looked at the three documents and did not sit, because she was in her own building and it was her room, and she stood at the head of the table and put two fingers on the middle one.

"This sitting is a hearing on the seizure of a bell," she said. "It is not a hearing on a road, and it is not a hearing on a finding, and anybody who has come up that stair hoping to be told about a city is in the wrong building and I would be obliged if somebody at the bottom of the stair told them so, because I will not have a proceeding turned into something it is not by an audience." She looked round. "The applicant in this room is the guild clerk, and the guild's notice is the oldest paper on this table, and the reason it is the oldest paper on this table is that it is the reason there is a seizure to hear. If that paper had been obeyed, we would not be here. Captain Rell, are you sworn?"

"I am," said Captain Rell.

"Sworn before me this morning at the eighth hour in the outer room, and the clerk has it." She sat down. "Then go on. Tell the room what you did and why, in that order, and do not read from the paper, because I have read the paper and I want the man."

Rell was fifty and had been in the town's watch nineteen years and he had a watch captain's way of standing which was not parade-ground and was not casual, and he had not looked at Aren Kest once since he came into the room and he was going to not do it now and everybody could see him deciding to.

"I received a sealed paper from a courier at the evening of the eighteenth of Mudmonth, at my own door, and I was told to keep it and not to open it, which was not an instruction I was ever issued and I have thought about that for a long time. It is dated the sixteenth. It names an object — a bell taken out of a river after the fever at Bellweather and thereafter hung where men walk — and it directs this watch to take that object into custody for examination by the office that sealed it. It came with a seal for the seal." He turned the paper over and did not open it. "It also names a person. It describes him as the road-keeper of Merefen and the holder of the Bellweather hinge, and it summons him to the provincial town to give account of the keeping. There is no keeper. I have said that in three rooms and I have had three answers and no two of them agree."

"And you executed the seizure anyway," said the magistrate.

"I did, and I would do it again tomorrow, and I have thought about that in the last nine days more than I have thought about anything." Rell did not raise his voice and did not need to. "The town is in this county. The county is under the authority that sealed the paper. The seal is the seal. There is nothing in that paper that a watch captain in a fen town is competent to refuse, and I would like it understood that I have looked for the thing that would let me refuse it and it is not there and has not been there for eleven years."

"You took the bell at first light on the nineteenth," said Lysa Fenn. "Say how."

"Four men and a cart. Off the abutment at the sixth hour, the cradle it was on had been grown into by eleven years and I sent a man with a chisel and it came off in three pieces, and the split in the bell rang when it was moved, which is a thing I had not expected and which the men heard, and I have never pretended to them that I did not hear it. We swept the stone under it. We put it in the storehouse by the fish weir and I put a seal on the door with the office's seal, which came with the paper, and it has been under seal since the nineteenth and it is there now."

"Three days," said Lysa Fenn.

The room did not make a sound.

"The paper is dated the sixteenth," she said. "It was delivered into Captain Rell's hands on the evening of the eighteenth. The bell came off the abutment at the first light on the nineteenth. That is three days from the day written on the paper to the morning the thing was done, and the paper was written before the storm and before the anniversary and before anything whatever had happened in this town except a bond and a chalk mark. I am going to have that written down as a finding of fact in the record of this court, and it is going to be the first thing on the page, and I am going to say now, in this room, in front of the people in the stair, why."

She put her hand flat on the order.

"Because it is the one number in this matter that cannot be argued about. The seal may be good or bad. The authority may be produced or not produced. A Crown paper in a county town is either enforceable or it is void, and either way a man's lawyer can fight it for a year. But a date is a date. Sixteen to nineteen is three days, and every office above this room that ever sees this record will read that line first, because it is the line that makes the rest of the document look like what it is, which is an instruction written in advance for a thing that had not happened yet. The Crown can un-find that a bell was lawfully seized tomorrow morning. It cannot un-write three days."

"You are ruling it unlawful," said the toll-holder's man, with enormous hope.

"I am ruling it lawful," said Lysa Fenn. "I told Mr Kest in this room on the thirtieth of Mudmonth what my answer would be and I gave it to him in advance, because I would rather be fair in advance than merciful later, and I am going to be neither. A Crown paper sealed by an office with authority over this county, delivered to an officer competent to execute it, describing an object that is in this county — that is lawful procedure. The seal is the seal. The town is in the county. The Crown may do what the Crown's paper says, and this court has no power to say otherwise and I am not going to pretend to a room of market people that a magistrate can overrule a provincial office with a bad breakfast and a good opinion."

The room sat with it.

"What I am going to say next is worse," said Lysa Fenn, "and I want it said in this room rather than in a corridor by a man who has heard it in a tavern. This court cannot order the bell returned. Nobody in Merefen can order the bell returned. The three lawful ways to undo that seizure are all of them expensive and all of them slow, and the guild should know which one it is choosing before it spends anything. One: produce the authority. The paper names an office and a person. If the marshal's emergency office can produce the instrument by which that office took a merchant's bell out of a bridge in a town, the seizure is good and the town is paid. Two: a superior court. The county's own court cannot review its own officer's execution of a superior instrument, and a superior court is two hundred miles away and takes a year and costs more than the bell is worth. Three: the Crown withdraws it. The Crown wrote it, and only the Crown can take it back, and a Crown that withdraws it has admitted it needed to."

"So the guild's application is refused," said the guild clerk, who had been standing since the start and had not sat.

"Your first part is refused," said Lysa Fenn. "The bell stays where it is under the Crown's seal. The second part of your application is not refused, it is worse, and I will do it and I will do it in words you can write down." She turned to the guild clerk. "The Old Toll crossing is held by this guild in municipal trust. That trust was in abeyance from the nineteenth of Mudmonth, when the object on it was taken by a lawful officer of this county, and I am asked to restore it. I am not going to restore it, and here is the reason in one sentence: a municipal trust cannot be affirmed over a lawful Crown holding on the same stone, and while that bell is under that seal the trust of the crossing is a question that nobody in this county can answer, and it will be asked again in a year, and if the Crown has withdrawn the paper by then it will be a different room and a different argument and this one will have cost you a year for nothing."

"You are telling us the law was on its side," said the toll-holder's man, "and that we have lost anyway."

"I am telling you the law was on its side, and that being on the side of the law is the cheapest thing that has ever happened to this town, and that you are going to be told so in a market square this afternoon, and that being told it is going to be the most expensive thing that happens to Merefen in Greenmonth." She squared the three papers. "Because every man on that stair who came up here for justice is going to go down it with a sentence in his ear, and a sentence in an ear is a thing that repeats, and in a month a third of the market square is going to be saying that there is no bell and never was a bell and that a man made it up to keep a bridge busy, and there is no power on this earth that can un-say it in a morning, and I am telling you that now, before it happens, so that at least one person in this town heard it coming."

---

She called the road warden at the seventh hour and he had been in the room since the sixth, standing against the wall by the press, and he came and stood where a man stands who is being examined, and he did not look at the exhibit wrapper.

"You will not testify about a road," said Lysa Fenn. "I have told you and I have told your keeper and I meant it when I said it and I am not going to be moved in a room like this by anybody. You will testify about the object. I want a tradesman's account of the object and of nothing else, and I want it in the trade's words, and I want the sort of thing you would say to a man who was going to cut stone near it."

"Yes, ma'am."

"How heavy."

"Nine stone. Lifted by the headstock, never by the body, the same as any bell of that size anywhere in this kingdom. The cradle on the Old Toll abutment is iron and it was welded to the stone by a fitter who has been dead eleven years, and the bell had been on it since it was put there, and the tongue had settled into the lip of the split and the whole thing had grown together, which is not a fault in the fitter. It takes two men and a bar through the headstock loop and about three minutes, and the conversation is not about the minutes, it is about which of four places nine stone lands in if a man lets go, and two of the four are the feet of the man holding it."

"And the split."

"From the lip to the shoulder, and it is not a blow. A bell that is struck hard enough to be answered splits outward; a bell that is dropped or struck on the lip splits inward, and you can see the difference from the edge in a good light. It was outward. The shoulder is sound. There is a founder's mark on the shoulder, on the inside where a caster puts his mark, and it is legible and it is a mark I have read before on other metal in this town." He stopped. "That is the account of the object."

"That is the account of the object," said Lysa Fenn, "and the guild clerk may put it in its file, and Mr Kest, I am going to rule on a thing you have not asked me for."

"I would not presume."

"You have not asked and I am doing it anyway, and I will tell you why, and then you will understand the whole of what I am able to do with this morning." She turned to the clerk. "Enter as findings of fact: the bell was of nine stone; it stood upon an iron cradle at the Old Toll abutment; the cradle was continuous with the stone; on the thirteenth the road warden repacked three lengths of the sill beneath it; at the sixth hour on the nineteenth four men of this watch lifted it with a bar through the headstock loop and a cart, and the bed of the stone was swept afterwards, and there is now a clean rectangle in the moss. Enter also that the split in the bell is outward from the sound bow, and that the guild's notice of the eleventh of Mudmonth had already been issued in respect of it, and that no person had produced the authority by which the object's custody was claimed at any time before the hearing of this court."

"That last one is a strange finding," said the guild clerk.

"It is a strange morning," said Lysa Fenn. "Enter it."

The clerk entered it. And then he cleared his throat and said, "There is a second paper, ma'am, entered in the record of this court on the thirtieth and not yet read into the proceeding. It came to the town trust at the ninth hour on the twenty-ninth of Mudmonth by the provincial post, and it is of the same office and the same hand and the same seal, and it instructs the trust to provide the attendance of the road-keeper of Merefen at the provincial town on the ninth of Greenmonth, and to permit the removal of the Bellweather hinge from its present place of keeping to the provincial store, and it refers throughout to the Bellweather hinge as a thing held in the keeper's charge."

She did not look at Aren. She looked at the wall of the room.

"Then I will ask the clerk to enter the second paper and the first paper together, and to enter with them the finding that this court has searched the charter of this town, the county's list of appointments, and the Crown's schedule of provincial offices, and has found no office of keeper of the Bellweather hinge or of road-keeper of Merefen, and no person holding either, and no commission conferring either." She said the rest of it to the room. "That finding is the honest answer to the two papers. A Crown seal is a Crown seal. Two Crown seals are two Crown seals. And neither of them has anybody standing behind it, and I have spent a morning in front of a room full of this town and I have not been able to find the name of the man who told either of these bells what to do, and neither has anybody else in this room, and I am going to write that in a record in my own hand with my own name at the top of it, and it is the only thing I am going to leave behind today, because it is the only thing that will still be true in a year."

The magistrate's clerk was writing.

"Captain Rell."

"Ma'am."

"You have a watch-sergeant in this room. I have had him in the stair since the seventh hour and I have sent for him twice and he has not come up. Why?"

Rell did not move.

"Sergeant Ottery has been in that stair since the seventh hour, ma'am, because I told him to be in it, and because he is in the stair and not in this room by my instruction, and I would like that entered."

"By your instruction."

"By my instruction, and I will answer for it, and you may enter that I gave it this morning before the sitting and that I gave it for a reason and that the reason is that I have not yet read what he has written."

"There is a document," said Lysa Fenn.

"There is a document," said Rell. "Sergeant Ottery wrote it at his own hand on the night of the seventh of Greenmonth, at his own table, in his own house, and he brought it up this morning and gave it to the clerk at the seventh hour and it is in your file, ma'am, at the bottom, under my statement, and I have not read it, and I am not going to read it in the last half hour of a sitting and speak afterwards. Read it or do not. That is his and yours. I have a duty in this room and it is to tell you that the order was executed by four men of this watch on a paper and not on any opinion of mine about a fever, and I have told you that already and I will say it again if you want it."

"It is in the file," said the clerk.

"It is in the file," said Rell.

"Then I will read it," said Lysa Fenn, "and the room will hear it, because the room is full and the stair is full and this is a public sitting in a building with a wedge in the door. Sergeant Ottery — send him in."

He was forty-seven and had been in the Merefen watch twenty-two years and had been a sergeant nine, and he came into the room with his cap in his hand and did not sit, and he had the appearance of a man who has been sick with something that is not a fever and is sorry about it.

"Sit down, Sergeant."

"I'd rather stand, ma'am, if it's the same."

"Sit down." He sat. "You have written a statement. Nobody asked you for it. Is that right?"

"Yes, ma'am."

"State, for the record, that nobody asked you for it, and then read your own statement in your own words, and do not read it as you wrote it, because the clerk has the writing and I want the room to hear a man say his own words twice."

"Sergeant," said Bram Ottery, "on the night of the seventh of Greenmonth I sat down at my own table and wrote out what this watch did, and why, and who was standing where, because I have been a watchman in this town for twenty-two years and in all that time I have never once seen a thing done in this county that was written down, and I did not write mine down either, and I have been the officer of this watch and I signed for it in my own hand and I did not write it down, and I have had a bad four days about it."

He turned a page, and did not read it.

"Here it is in short. On the evening of the eighteenth a courier gave Captain Rell a sealed paper. On the nineteenth at the sixth hour the captain sent for me and told me what the paper was. He read it to me. He did not read it as an order to believe anything; he read it as an order to take a bell, and he told me so, and I wrote that down as being what he told me, because it was. Four men and a cart. A chisel for the cradle. The stone swept under the cradle afterwards, because I sent a boy with a broom and the boy swept it, and I have thought about the boy with the broom more than I have thought about the bar.

"Nobody in that crew spoke about the fever. I want that entered. I have heard it said that this watch took that bell because of the fever, and I have heard it said in this town, and it is not true of the men who carried it, and I will answer for the men who carried it because they were my men.

"There is one other thing and I have put it down because it is the only part of it I cannot answer for." He looked up, and did not look at the stair. "From the seventeenth of Mudmonth to the present, a man has been telling the market square of Merefen that that bell carries the fever, and that taking it off the bridge was right, and that a bell that has been near men walking for eleven years will bring it back into the low quarter. He has said it in the market. He has said it on the wharf road. He has said it to my men at the weir on the morning of the twenty-first and my men did not write it down, and that is on my men and it is on me.

"He said it to me. On the morning of the twentieth, at the watch-house door, before the bell was a day in the storehouse, he said the low quarter of this town was going to take the fever again in Mudmonth and that it would be the bridge's fault and not the water's. I heard him. I have thought about it every day since. I did not write it down, because I did not think it was a thing anybody would want in a book, and because a man saying a thing in a market square is not a man saying a thing in a court, and because I was afraid of being the officer who comes to this room and cannot answer a question." He squared his cap on his knee. "That is the whole of my statement and I wrote it at my own table and nobody asked me for it and I would like the room to have it and I would not like it read out, and I have made my peace with that, because a thing in a book is the same thing out loud in twenty years' time."

"What's his name," said the toll-holder's man.

Ottery looked at the stair.

"He is in the stair," he said. "He has been in the stair since the seventh hour, same as I have. He sells eels off a barrow on the wharf road and he has a pitch at the market and he is called throughout this town by his trade and I do not know that he has any other, and if anybody in this room wants to put a name to him, they may do it, and it will be a worse name than eels."

The room turned around.

It was not dramatic. It was thirty-one people on a stair all shifting their weight at once, and a man on the second step up, in an apron, with his hands at his sides, who had been standing with his back to the wall and his hat under one arm, looking at the floor.

He was fifty-eight or so. He had a barrow's habit of standing and a seller's habit of not being surprised, and he looked up the stair at the people looking at him, and he did not go out of the door, because a man going out of a door on the fourth of Greenmonth in front of a magistrate is a man who is admitting something, and he knew it, and he stood.

"I said the fever," he said. "I'd not say it different now. There's a dyke on a street in this town that came from water off a bridge, and the Crown's own men said so eleven years back, and the Crown's own men were wrong, and I'd not be doing my job to hold that they've always been right." His voice carried in the small stair better than anybody expected. "And I'd say this to the bench and not to him. If the bell's the fever, and the crown's taken it, then the crown's right and there'll be no fever. If I'm wrong, then somebody's here who's paid for the taking of it, and the best thing that can happen to a town is a Crown officer paid for a bell."

Nobody in the stair made a sound. It was, as Lysa Fenn had said twenty minutes earlier, the most expensive sentence said in Merefen in Greenmonth, and it was said by a man selling eels, and it was said for the reason that everybody in a market says anything, which is that it had always been said and he had no reason to be the first man to stop.

"Enter it," said Lysa Fenn, in a voice that carried down the stair. "Enter that the sergeant has named the man and that the man is in the building, and enter that nobody had ever thought to ask the man, because he was not holding a document; he was holding a story, and he has been selling it in this town since the seventeenth, and that this court cannot un-sell it in a morning, and that it is now in a record with a name in it, which is the only thing a court can do about a story, which is put it in a book and go home."

She closed the folder.

"Sergeant Ottery. You wrote a statement that your captain had not ordered and that your captain has not read, and it has gone into a record in a magistrate's court in the county of Merefen, and it names a man who was standing in this room's stair. From this hour that document is the reason a Crown office, or a council, or a person with a seal, can look at what the watch of this town actually did and why, and it is the reason the captain of it did it on a paper and not on a belief. And the man it names is a man selling eels who has been saying in a market square that a bell carries a fever, and that is the belief that is in four hundred heads in this town, and it is not in a finding and it is not on a Crown roll and it is not Rell's, and it came out of a man's mouth on the seventeenth of Mudmonth and it has been fed ever since, for nothing, for free, out of a stall, and Rell and this court have been arguing about a paper for four days while that went round the square like a cold in a school."

"Yes, ma'am," said Ottery.

"One more thing, Sergeant, and I ask it as a magistrate and not as a woman who is glad of a document." She did not soften it, because softening it would have been an insult. "You said you did not write it down because you did not think it was a thing anybody would want in a book, and because you were afraid of being the officer who could not answer a question. Both of those are the ordinary reasons and they are the reasons this watch has never had a rule instead of a captain. You have written a rule down now and it has cost you instead of saving you, and I am going to say the second part of that in front of thirty-one people instead of saying it in a corridor, because the first part is worth nothing without it." She turned to the room. "The watch of this town has no written rule for an officer who believes his captain has done a lawful thing and has a reason besides the law. Sergeant Ottery had one and it came out of his own head and it has put his name on a document that will be read in the provincial town. Nobody sent him. Nobody rewarded him. There is no promotion in this and there will not be one, and the bench of this court is not going to pretend otherwise."

---

The stair cleared in about a minute, which was quicker than anybody expected, and the door at the bottom was still open afterwards and the man was not in the square.

Rell was in the corridor with Ottery when the room came out, and he did not raise his voice there either, and Aren Kest was four feet away and could not have gone anywhere else without walking through both of them.

"I did not read it," Rell said to Ottery, "and I am not going to argue with a word of it, and I am going to say the thing that is mine to say, and then you and I will go and do the other thing."

"Captain—"

"You are relieved of the sergeant's post as of this hour, on the ground that a statement made without instruction is not a watch record, and it will be written that way, because I am not going to write down that you were right and I was wrong and put it in a file that goes to the provincial town in front of a man who would enjoy it." He had his cap in his hand. "You are not to be paid as a sergeant from today and you are not to be paid as a man at all until this is disposed of, and I will say so in writing, and I will be in the room when the watch fund is asked whether the fund is owed you for nine and three months of service, and I will tell it what you did, and I will not be able to get you the nine and three months, and that is the price and I am sorry for it in the only way I am permitted to be sorry in a corridor."

"That is not what I—," Ottery started.

"It is exactly what you did," said Rell. "You put a man's face in a judge's record on a night when you were not asked to, and the finding of this court is that it was true, and a finding of this court does not pay anybody's wages, and you have lost the stripes and the accrual and I have not got the power to put any of it back. Go home. Not to the watch-house. Home."

Ottery stood in the corridor of a salt-loft house with his cap in his hands for the length of four breaths, and then said, "Captain, I wanted the watch to have a rule," and Rell said, "I know exactly what you wanted," and went down the stair.

The magistrate's clerk came out last, with his ledger under his arm, and stopped in the corridor to square a page against his chest the way a man squares a thing that has cost him nothing and cost everybody else a great deal.

"The finding of the court is recorded," he said, to nobody in particular. "The bell is not returned. The trust is not restored. Three days from the sixteenth to the morning of the nineteenth. Two seals and no office behind either of them. And a sergeant's statement naming a man with a barrow, in the file, with the hour on it."

"And the ninth of Greenmonth," Aren said.

"Is in the file too, ma'am's word, as a date and an instruction, and it stands," said the clerk, "and the instruction is addressed to the town trust, and the trust is not going to be in a position to comply with anything, because a trust that is in abeyance is a trust with nobody to receive an instruction, and I have entered that as a further finding on the clerk's own motion, which is a thing I have never done in five years and which I have done today because it seemed to me to be the only sentence in this matter that nobody in the building had thought of."

He went down the stair.

The market on the wharf road was still on, and it was thinner than it had been at the ninth hour, because a market empties at noon whatever anybody has been told in a room, and the marshals had already chalked the notice of the ruling on the toll-house board, in a small hand, with a rule above it, which meant it was official, which meant that from that hour a man in Merefen who said the law had taken an unfair thing would be a man arguing with a line of chalk.

The chalk said: *On the fourth of Greenmonth, in the county court, the seizure of the Bellweather bell was held lawful procedure. The trust of the Old Toll crossing is in abeyance.*

There was no room on the board for the rest of it, and the board did not have the rest of it, and the rest of it was that a man in an apron with a barrow had said a thing in a stair and had been right about the arithmetic of a Crown's convenience in the middle of it, and that thirty-one people had heard both, and that the town would spend Greenmonth repeating the chalk and forgetting the stair.

Aren Kest came down the half-landing and into the street and stood in the cold with the market going on around him and a man selling eels somewhere behind him in the town, and the day went on at the shilling a day, which was a real number, and there was a bench mark on Sedge Reach that was a quarter of an inch wrong, and a shear pin to fit by Thursday, and eleven days of wages owed by people who had just been told in writing that the law had been on their side.

Somewhere under the Old Toll crossing, a mile up the road, the river went over four bays that had never been shut, past an empty iron cradle with a clean rectangle in the moss under it, and there was nothing at all in the stone to answer anybody.
