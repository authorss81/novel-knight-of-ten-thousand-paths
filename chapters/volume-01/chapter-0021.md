# Chapter 0021

The ninth of Greenmonth was a Friday and a market day, and by the fourth hour the wharf road was two hundred yards of awning and barrow and voice, and Tovan Kest's yard was shut.

Not idle. Shut, which is different, and the difference is a thing a carpenter can feel in a yard at the fourth hour of a market morning the way a man can feel a change in a river. Four men had a half day's pay coming and no cutting to bring them in for it, and they were in the yard anyway, because a yard that empties on a market morning is a yard that has told its men where they stand, and Tovan had them on the kerb re-dressing a tool handle that did not need dressing, on the ground that a man who is sent home on a market morning comes back on a market morning.

"I am not going," said Aren Kest.

"You said you would." Tovan had a chisel in his hand and was not using it. "You said it on Thursday and I have it, and I have told four men you said it, which was a lie, and I would like that written down somewhere that a clerk could get at."

"Then write it down."

"I have got a week-book and a pencil and four men, and a thing I write in the week-book is a fact about a yard, and a fact about a yard is worth exactly what a yard is worth, and on a Friday morning in Greenmonth a yard is worth about a fortnight's work less than it was on the twenty-seventh of Mudmonth, and I have four men on my roll and a factor's stone and a brother on a wage advance, and I have done the sum twice and it comes out the same both times." Tovan put the chisel down. "Go and be useful somewhere."

---

The Old Toll crossing is four hundred yards up the road from Weir Street and a road warden can cover it in nine minutes at a walk. Aren Kest went up it at the eighth hour in a coat that had been wet on the eighth and had dried badly on the ninth, with a notebook in the inside of it and nothing else in his hands, because there is a point in carrying tools up to a bridge you have no intention of working on and the point is that it saves you explaining yourself at the top.

The seal was on the post. Grey wax, a hand laid flat on a book, rained on and not run, and the post itself had gone the colour of the stone it stood on since the nineteenth of Mudmonth, and the wax was the only thing on the whole structure that had not gone grey.

He did not touch it. He wrote down the state of it instead, in the notebook, with the hour against it, because a man who puts his thumb on a Crown seal in order to look at it is a man who has decided that curiosity is a thing that outranks an order, and he had already spent one bond on a different curiosity, and the roll still had it.

The cradle was empty. The iron was rusted into the stone where a fitter had welded it eleven years before, and the moss had come back over the corner of the swept rectangle and stopped about two fingers short of it, which told him it was the eighth of Greenmonth and not the ninth, and which was the sort of thing he now found himself doing without deciding to.

The seat was where he had cut it. That was the part he had not expected to want. He got down onto the sill with a hand on the parapet and put his face close enough to count the joints, and it was there: a single block of the fourth course, six inches long and two and a quarter deep and an inch and a half in the face, cut square to a line he had struck twice off the parapet on the eighth of Greenmonth with the moon not doing anything, and the two discs across it, and the pin through both, and the oak collar on the head with three hammer blows in it.

Quarren's mark was under the near disc. He could not see it and he did not try to see it, because it was cut to be under the disc and the disc was there to hide it, and a man who lifts his own maker's mark to check it has misunderstood what a maker's mark is for.

He wrote: *Old Toll, near abutment. Eighth hour of the ninth. Seat in fourth course, two discs set, pin and oak collar, collar struck three times. Iron of the cradle rusted into stone, none of it moved. Moss over the swept bed, stopped about two fingers short, so the last sweep was the eighth. Seal on the post, grey, not run, not touched.*

Then he shut the book and did not try the sill.

He had been tempted for four days. That was the honest name for it and he gave it the honest name, standing on a cold bridge in a wind off the fen with his hand in his coat pocket where it was not touching anything: he had been tempted to put his hand on the cross under the two discs, because it was the only seat in the world he had cut himself and because a man who has found a door twice is a man who checks it. And he was not going to, and the reason was not the glass and was not Quarren's page and was not the tally of two against three.

The reason was that he had not prepared anything on this side of it. He had a sheet of paper in a yard's week-book with three hands on it and a maker's account bound in a press over a salt merchant, and he had a date and an hour, and every one of those was a fact about the last time. He had found in the last sixteen days that the road does not read a record, and that a road which is opened by a man with nothing prepared opens onto the wrong place at the wrong hour in the wrong weather with no papers, and that the whole difference between the first time and this one was not the glass and was not the money and was not a priest.

It was that on the last time there was a man at the far end who knew he was coming.

He had told three people that on a bridge on the eighth of Greenmonth and none of them had been able to do anything about it, and the reason none of them had been able to do anything about it was that it was true.

So he wrote in the notebook, at the bottom, in a hand that was not in the habit of writing for anybody: *Did not try it. There is nobody on the other side who knows the date. Trying it now is a man pulling a bell in a house where nobody has been told there is one.*

---

The bench of the Merefen bridge guild sits at the ninth hour on a market day in the long room over the wharf, with the market audible through the shutter, and on the ninth of Greenmonth it sat with eight men and one empty place, and the empty place was not anybody's fault, and the clerk entered it in the roll before anybody spoke.

Aren came in at the ninth with the market still going on the other side of the shutter and the wind still coming under the door, and the guild clerk — a careful man, forty-odd, who had come into a yard instead of sending a boy on the sixth of Greenmonth and had not stopped being decent since — put his hand flat on the ledger and said:

"You are eleven minutes late and I have entered you as present."

"Thank you."

"Do not thank me. I entered you as present because a roll that says *absent* for a man who is standing in the room is a roll that is lying, and this guild has had one good week and I would like to keep it." He turned the ledger round. "Two things of yours on the roll before anything else. The wage advance, entered as a debt and not as a surety, two marks and three quarters, at eight pence a day, with your brother's name and a factor's and mine. And the second."

"Second."

"A witness's sight of a thing set on the eighth, in a tradesman's hand, with the date and the hour, and the seat, and the maker's hand, and one line at the foot that says a page cannot be asked to turn round." The clerk read the entry and then read it again, which he had not done for anybody's line. "I have entered it and I have entered the whole of it, and I have not entered a reason, and I will not be asked for a reason by this bench or by any office, because I do not have one and a man in my position who makes one up is worse than a man who writes none."

"Correct," said Aren Kest.

"I have it written as *no reason given*," said the clerk, "which is the second time this month that phrase has gone on a roll in this town, and there will be a third before the month is out. I can tell you that much of it now, and I would rather told it than have anybody surprised."

Then the door at the back opened, and two people came in, and the room's whole weather changed, and Aren understood immediately that the reeve of Merefen had come to the guild bench on a market morning and that this was not a thing she did often, and that she had sent for it rather than arrived in it.

Reeve Aldis Fenn was older than her sister by nine years and had held a thing nobody appoints you to for eleven years, and she came in with her cloak still on because the room was cold and full, and she did not shake hands, and the first thing she said was not about the bell.

"I have come about the road," she said. "There is nothing I can say about the bell that this bench or this room has not heard, and I have read the finding and I agree with the finding, and I would like it entered that I agree with it, because a reeve who agrees with a finding in a room and argues with it in a corridor is worth nothing to anybody and I have been one of those twice."

"Then the road," said the man in the chair.

"The Old Toll crossing's trust has been in abeyance since the fourth of Greenmonth and it will be in abeyance for a year, and there is no owner on that stone, and the wharf road is four hundred yards of that crossing, and a bridge with no owner is a bridge with no crew, and a wharf road with no crew is a market that does not happen on the ninth and a factor who does not come in June and a town that cannot pay anybody." She put one hand flat on the table, and it was a reeve's hand, and it did not stay flat. "I want the record to show that I said that on the ninth, in this room, in front of the surety, and that the surety was not in front of the bench when I said it, and that I said it anyway, because a reeve who waits for a man to be in the room to say a thing the man already knows is a reeve with nothing to do."

"Which thing," said Aren Kest.

"Which thing," said Reeve Fenn, "is that the bench is not going to fix this. You know that. I have watched you know that for a fortnight. And I have come because I am the reeve of this town and I am obliged to say what the town loses, and the town loses a market, and I would like somebody in this room to have said so out loud before the vote rather than after it, because after it is a complaint and before it is a reason."

Nobody spoke for a moment, and the market went on outside the shutter.

"Your sister is a magistrate," said Aren Kest.

"My sister is a magistrate on a salary the reeve's office has called a discouragement since the year she took it, and she has never once said so out loud to me, and I have never once said it out loud to her, and that is a family arrangement and it is not in this room." The reeve turned round. "Master Kest, I have got a hundred and forty questions for this bench about a road and a market and a yearly toll and a town that is fed off four hundred yards of bridge, and I have come to the wrong room, and I have come anyway, because your man wrote a town off in a public document on the fourth and then sat down and offered to be a case, and a reeve is supposed to know where those two men are, and in this town they are the same man, and I do not know what to do about that either."

"Thank you," said Aren Kest, which was the wrong word and he knew it before it was out.

"It is not a thank you," said the reeve. "It is a minute. I have already sent the minute to the town clerk at the half hour and it says that the reeve of Merefen attended the guild bench at the ninth hour of the ninth of Greenmonth, on the business of the wharf road, and that the surety was not in front of the bench, and that the reeve said so in the room."

Then she sat down, which she had not asked to do, and put her gloves on the table, and after that she said nothing at all for forty minutes, which the man in the chair afterwards described as the most useful thing that had happened in the room.

---

The bond of the eleventh was taken last, because the clerk was a careful man and because the reeve's minute wanted it taken last.

"Six marks," said the clerk. "Surety, a tool-chest, entered on the roll at three and a half. Object, a cracked bell, given over on the eleventh of Mudmonth, since removed from this town by a Crown paper on the nineteenth and not returned, and the notice of removal of the eleventh standing unexecuted. The bell's value on this guild's own scrap roll is six marks, and the roll is right about the value, and the six marks is what the guild is out."

"I am in front of it," said Aren Kest.

"You are in front of it, which is a different thing." The clerk squared the ledger. "A Crown seal on a post does not discharge a bond. A Crown takes a bell. The bell was worth six marks on the eleventh and the guild has a roll that says so, and nothing has come back for it, and there is no office in this county that anybody has been able to name that took it. So the bench has six marks of a hole and it has to decide this morning what to do with a hole."

"What are the options," said the man in the chair.

"Three that are real. One. The guild pursues it and names no man, and goes to the provincial court, and waits. Two. The guild writes off the six against the scrap roll and says so, and every man in this room is remembered by a town that has written off six marks on a cracked bell. Three." The clerk stopped and looked at his own page and then went on, because he was a careful man and he had read the line out and was not going to soften it. "Three. The guild takes the tool-chest, three and a half, against six, and the man stands surety for the difference out of his own wages for as long as it takes, and the roll is clear by the frost."

There was a sound from the end of the table, and it was Tovan Kest, and he had said it in a single sentence without standing up.

"Before you vote: the bench is voting a bond and the road is shut either way, and the six marks is not going to mend that bridge, and I am telling you so now so that nobody can say afterwards that I stood in here and let a thing happen for a bridge."

"Tovan," said the clerk.

"I am a party to it," said Tovan Kest, "the advance on the roll is mine, and a party does not vote, and I have said the one true thing I came in with and I would like it minuted that I said it before the vote and not after, and I am going to sit down."

He sat down.

Aren Kest stood up, which he had not planned to do.

"Take the option the clerk called three," he said. "It is the honest one and it is the only one of the three that is about me. Six marks does not go to a Crown that has not been named. Three and a half comes off the chest and is a payment, not a surrender, and the rest of it at a shilling a day until there is a shilling a day. And I want the roll to say the debt is mine and not the guild's, and that it was not proposed by me, and that the clerk put three options on the table and I took the third."

"You are asking the bench to take your wages off a road it does not have," said the man in the chair.

"I am asking the bench to take my wages off a road it does not have," said Aren Kest, "because the alternative is that the guild writes off six marks and a man at the other end of it never finds out. And I am asking for it today, on the ninth, in this room, with the reeve in it, because on the fourteenth I could not have had it, and on the twenty-seventh I was in a fever, and a thing that is only ever right when somebody can be got to a room is a thing that is not right."

The reeve of Merefen did not move and did not look at him, and that was the correct thing for her to do, and he noticed it, and it was the first time in eleven years that a reeve of Merefen had sat through two and a half hours of a thing and let a man in it be silent.

"Very well," said the man in the chair. "The bench will take the third. Does the surety wish to be heard."

"No," said Aren Kest. "I have been heard. I want to withdraw before the vote, because I have been in front of it and I am about to be somewhere else, and I would rather leave the roll with the number of men who were in the room than have it carry a man who was not listening to the last of it."

He got to the door and the reeve of Merefen got there before him, which she had no business doing and did anyway.

"You are going," she said, not as a question.

"The ninth of Greenmonth is a date and not a mood," said Aren Kest, "and a man in this county who says he is going to be in two places is a man who is in neither."

"Then go," said Reeve Aldis Fenn, "and I will tell you the one thing I came for and did not say, because you are the only road warden in this county and there is nobody else in this town to say it to. The bench is voting on a bond, and the bond is yours, and the vote is a vote about six marks. It is not a vote about you. In a year, when a man opens that roll, he is going to read a vote taken in a room and he is not going to be able to tell that the man it was about was not in it, and that will be worth nothing to you and it will have cost this town something, and I have wanted it written down twice this morning and I have written it down once and I will write it down again if I have to."

He went up Weir Street at the ninth hour and the bench voted behind his back, on a Friday, in a room with the market on the other side of the shutter, and the chest went to three and a half against six with the balance on the man's wages, and the reeve of Merefen sat through all of it and said nothing, on purpose, having said the true thing first.

---

He wrote the sheet in the yard, standing at the bench with the wind off the wharf and four men who had all been in the room pretending to be interested in the tool handle.

*I, Aren Kest, road warden of Merefen, on the ninth day of Greenmonth, YR 312, at the ninth hour and a half, going from the Old Toll crossing above the seat cut on the eighth, where two discs of Steppe glass are set, whose maker's hand and whose account are bound with the copy of this in a press in Merefen. I am going out of this world and I intend to come back. I have prepared a record and not a person. I have a sworn account in a magistrate's court in another country and a document of a Crown office sealed by an office that court has searched three lists for and cannot name, and I have told the people holding those papers what is on them and what they are worth, which is nothing for five years. I do not know the road. I do not know what is at the other end of it tonight, and I have not asked anybody, because I do not know who to ask. I am going because I said I would.*

Tovan Kest read it standing up, twice, the way he read everything, and signed it at the foot with the hour.

"Mark the ninth page of the week," he said. "Not the general one. The ninth. Because the general one gets copied for the factor in June and I do not want a man in a counting house in a city two hundred miles off holding my brother's road in my handwriting with a date on it."

"The Archive has a copy."

"The Archive has a copy in a press over a salt merchant and the salt merchant weighs salt and has nothing to lose." Tovan turned the sheet over and looked at the back of it, which was Tovan's yard's back page, and which had a tally on it in a hand that was not his, four marks and stone and lime and a boy's hour. "There. And I'll tell the clerk tomorrow, in the yard, and he'll put it in his book and the clerk will have it in a book that isn't a copying book, and then it's in three books and a press and that's the whole of what I can do with it."

He gave the sheet back, and took his own coat off the hook, and said, without looking up:

"There's no road. There's a bridge. And I've written that distinction on your paper and I'll write it on every piece of paper that comes out of this yard about you. It doesn't make you right, brother. It makes you checkable."

"I know what it does," said Aren Kest.

"You know what it costs. You don't know what it does."

He went up the Old Toll road at the tenth hour with a bar over his shoulder out of pure habit and a wind behind him, and a market in his ears for a quarter of a mile, and the seat in the fourth course waiting four hundred yards ahead of him with two pieces of Steppe glass in it and the maker's mark hidden under the near one and a page in three books and a press.

He had not touched the seal. He had not tried the sill. He had a sheet in a yard's week-book with three hands and a date on it and no person at the far end of the road, and he had left a town that was not waiting for him, on purpose, with a bench voting behind his back and a reeve minuting the fact that he was not in front of it, and the difference between that and a man who was taken was the whole of the difference and he was going to have to be able to keep it in his own head for nineteen days without telling anybody which end of it he was standing on.

A quarter of a mile up the Old Toll road the crossing stood with its cradle empty and its post sealed and a seat cut square in the fourth course of the sill by a man who had wanted to try it and had written down that he had not, and the wind came off the fen and there was nothing in the air.

Then he stopped, in the middle of the road, a quarter of a mile from his own bridge, and did the last arithmetic out loud the way a man does it when he has decided that doing it silently is how men get themselves killed.

"Six marks," he said. "Three and a half off the chest today and the rest at a shilling a day until there is a shilling a day. Two marks and three quarters at eight pence a day to your brother and a factor. Eleven days of wages the trust will not pay. A provincial ledger in a building two hundred miles off with a clerk putting *absent* in it at this hour. And nineteen days, or the saying's three, or nine days like last time, and a bell under a seal I cannot touch and would not touch."

He picked the bar up off his shoulder and set it back on his shoulder, which is a thing a man does with his hands when his hands have nothing to work on.

"And nobody at the other end knows the date," he said. "That is the whole of it. That is the one I cannot carry."

Then he went up the last four hundred yards, and put his hand flat on a cross of dry stone under two discs of glass, and got nothing — no warmth, no fold in the air, no word, a hand in the cold and a road in the hand and nothing whatever — which was the correct condition of things, and which he had written down in a notebook on the seventh and had now written down twice, and which was the last honest sentence he was going to get to say before the next one came out of him that he had not made.

The city on the other side of it had been up since the fourth hour, and there was a bench of nine men who had asked him for a promise, and a third bell in a founder's yard, and a girl of about fourteen who would be sitting on the third step with a basket of onions between her boots.

He went through.
