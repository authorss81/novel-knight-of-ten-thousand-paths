# Chapter 0013

The finding came down on the second of Greenmonth by the ordinary post, on a sheet of good paper with a county seal on it, and it was read aloud in the trust room above the toll-house at the ninth hour because that is how a finding reaches a fen town.

There were eleven people in the room, which was half the number who would have been in it if it had been a market day, and one of the eleven was the man who had carried it up from the wharf office and put it on the table face down, and he was a county clerk, and nobody in the room was ever going to know his name because he was the office and the office reads.

"Merefen is in the county of the Sable's mouth," he said, before he turned it over. "The finding is read where the county is. I will read it and then I will answer nothing, because I have no authority to answer anything and the last man who tried to improve a finding with a conversation found himself writing memoranda for a month."

"Magistrate Fenn is in the room," said the toll-holder's man, unnecessarily, in the way of a man who thinks a name in a room is a kind of armour.

"I am aware of who is in the room," said the clerk, and turned the sheet over.

He read it as a clerk reads, which is to say without stopping and without any of the parts that mean anything. There was not a man in that room who had not heard a version of it, and none of them had heard this version, and the county's copy was the version with the year on it.

"*Finding of the county court in the matter of the Alder Vale, renewed at the provincial town on the second of Greenmonth, YR 312, the finding being in its eleventh year.*

"*One. That no claim to Bellweather, or to any person of Bellweather, survives.*

"*Two. That the crossing of the Alder Vale, and its dependent places, was not built, and is not maintained.*

"*Three. That the emergency powers granted to this county in respect of the Alder Vale are renewed for one year from the date above, upon the same terms, and to the same officers, as in the year last past.*"

He squared it. "That is the finding. It is a copy. The original is in the book."

Nobody spoke for a moment, and then a man at the back, who was a carter and had no business in a trust room and had come in anyway because the door was open, said:

"It says not built."

"It says not built," said the clerk.

"Nine days I could not get into the vale. I have said to my own wife that it was the fever, and so has she, and so has my mother, and I have heard it out of this window by the boys on the wharf every market for eleven years, and the boy has just read me a piece of paper that says it was never built." The carter's voice went up about an inch and stopped. "Which of those is the town to believe, Mr Clerk?"

"That is not a question for me, and I will not pretend it is a foolish one." The clerk put his hands flat on the table. "I will say what I am instructed to say and nothing else, because the next sentence out of my mouth in this room is a matter of my instructions and not of my opinion, and I have an office. A finding is not a statement of what happened. A finding is a statement of what a court will entertain. What happened is not before this court and never has been."

"Eleven years," said the toll-holder's man.

"Eleven years of a copy, sir, taken out of a book by a man at a desk, and every year it says the same three things, and this year the year is on it, which is the only thing that has changed, and it has changed because somebody in the provincial town has to sign it and signing takes a fresh piece of paper." He looked round the room without any expression at all. "I am not going to be a man in a story. I am a clerk. You may now do as you please."

---

Aren Kest had been standing by the window with his notebook out and the clerk's words going past him in the order a clerk says them, and about a quarter of the way through the second clause he had stopped hearing the room.

*Was not built.*

He had been in the county eleven years. He had read the finding in Embermonth of YR 301, at twenty-one, in a room with a different floor, and he had read it four times in debriefs, and the word that had carried the whole thing for him — the word that made a boy's failure into a boy's fault and a murder into a season's weather — had been *fever*. He had said the word fever in three years of debriefs, and the fever had been true, and the true thing was on this page, in clause one, where it had always been.

And the sentence doing the work was clause two, and nobody in the room had heard it.

"It says not built," he said.

The clerk turned his head. "I am aware of what it says."

"No. Sir, I beg your pardon, but the room heard it and the room read it as *the city went away*. That is the reading and it is the wrong reading and it is wrong in our favour, and I would like the room to be told why, because there is a man from this county going to the provincial town on the ninth of Greenmonth and he will be given a copy of this." He had the notebook open and had not written anything. "Not built is not destroyed. Not built is a sentence about a thing you could put your hand on. It is a sentence about *masonry*. It says the crossing was not constructed, and it says nobody is maintaining it, and it does not say one word about whether anybody was standing on it. Every year for eleven years this county has read that sentence as a description of a catastrophe, and it is not a description of anything. It is a description of a bridge that was never laid, and a man who has never seen a bridge that was never laid will believe it, and a man who has seen one will not."

The room had gone the way a room goes when a tradesman starts talking in it.

"And I am the man who has seen one," said Aren Kest, "and I will not be able to say so, and the reason I cannot is in clause one, and not in clause two. Clause one is a finding that no claim survives. That is not about the dead. That is about *claimants*. It does not say nobody died. It says nobody who comes forward with a claim will be heard, and it says that in a court, in a year, with a seal on it, and it is the most successful sentence anybody in this province has ever written, and it has taken eleven years and it has never once been argued with, because arguing with it requires somebody to stand up in a room and say *I claim*."

"Sit down, Kest," said somebody.

"I will not sit down yet, and I am not doing this for a case. I am doing it for the loading." He put his hand flat on the notebook. "A sentence in a finding is a structure, same as a bridge, and it is put up to hold one weight, and you find the load path by asking what it was built to carry and not what anybody would like it to carry. This sentence was built to hold up *a thing that was never made*. Which means it is a sentence about a physical object. Which means it is the only sentence in eleven years of this county's findings that a man with a trade can answer, and there has never once been a mason, a smith, a carpenter or a road warden in a room when it was read, because nobody has ever read it as being about stone." He looked round at them. "If the Crown asks this sentence to hold up one more weight — and it will, and it will be a heavier one, and it will be asked of a bridge and not of a fever — then everything this court has said for eleven years rests on a question of building. And that is a question a mason can be asked in a room where I am not."

The toll-holder's man said, "Then why has it never been asked?"

"Because nobody wanted to be the man who asked it," said Aren, "and I have been that man twice, and it cost me a city and a career and eleven years of a wage, and I am telling this room so that nobody in it has to find out."

Nobody was looking at the clerk. The clerk had picked up his copy of the finding and was holding it at the corner, and he did it the way a man holds a paper when he is deciding whether to look at it again, and he was not looking at it.

Magistrate Fenn spoke for the first time.

"Mr Kest." She had not moved from the table. "I am a county magistrate and that paper renews an emergency power in this county for another year, and the second clause of it is a statement about a bridge, and the two of those facts are in one document, and the document is a building, and you are the only man in this room who has described a document as a building to me before and I was not expecting to be grateful for it." She turned to the clerk. "Is the third clause of that finding usual?"

"It is in the finding. It is renewed with the finding."

"It is in the finding every year?"

"Every year, ma'am. Upon the same terms, and to the same officers, as in the year last past."

"And the officers are not named."

"No, ma'am."

"Then the county's emergency power in the Alder Vale is renewed annually, to whoever held it last year, by description, and there is no document in this county that says who they are." She said it exactly the way she said everything, which is as though she were reading out a load table. "That is how a watch ends up on a private bridge at first light on an order dated three days earlier, and I intend to put that sentence in front of a bench on Sunday, and I would be obliged if nobody in this room repeats it in a market square before I have put it in a record, because a sentence that has been in a market square is a sentence a man can deny."

That got the room back to the level it belonged at, and the county's clerk wrote something in a small book, which he did quickly, and Aren noticed it and did not know yet what it was for.

Then Mara Vey said the thing that was actually on the table, and she said it from the far end with her day-book under her arm, and she had not raised her voice in four years.

"No," she said. "It is not better news and worse news. It is worse news twice, and I would like somebody in this room to stop being pleased."

She opened the day-book on the table without asking anybody's leave, which was the first breach of the room's manners anyone had committed all morning and it was committed by the person who owned it.

"He is right about clause two, and he is right that it is a sentence about masonry, and it is worth a year of anybody's life to have that said in a room. And he is wrong about what it is for, in the one direction that matters to me, and I am going to correct him in front of eleven people because the correction is the whole of my work and I would rather it were on the record now." She found the page. "On the thirtieth of Mudmonth this office entered a claim exhibit with a chain of custody and a number on the wrapper, and it is a document from the fourteenth of Mudmonth, YR 301, with a seal on it, and it is a good document. As of this morning it is not that any more. Not because anything has happened to it. Because clause two says the crossing and its dependent places were not built, and therefore a document about the crossing is a document about a thing that never existed, and a clerk in an office four counties away can now write one word on my wrapper, in his own hand, and the word is *unverified*, and my exhibit is a claim and not a record, and nobody has to lift a finger, and the seal is still on the stub, and it still weighs nine ounces."

"How long does the word take?" asked the toll-holder's man.

"Eleven seconds. It has already been written somewhere; this morning's finding told them where to write it." She closed the book. "And the second thing, and I will not be popular for it. He thinks the Crown will use clause two against the villages. It will not. The Crown will not touch the villages. *Its dependent places* is a phrase that fits a city. It fits the villages too, and it has been used for both, and it is used for both because it does the work of both. A dependent place is a place that exists only in relation to a principal. If the principal is a crossing, and the crossing was not built, then the dependent places were never anything either, and every claim for every one of the seven of them falls on the principal without ever being argued about. Not refused. Dropped. Refused you can appeal. Dropped you cannot, because there is nothing to appeal to."

"Then why does your office spend its life naming them?" asked the carter.

"Because a name is not a dependent place," said Mara Vey. "Alder Cross. Saltbrim. Wrenlow. Cobbett. Nine Elms. Stonebarrow. Hushwater. Seven names, written on a page, in a hand, with a page behind them. That is all this office has. And the Crown's answer to a page of names is a phrase, and a phrase weighs nothing, and that is the entire difference between us and it and it has taken four years to get to a place where I can say it in one sentence in a trust room."

---

The clerk stayed forty minutes afterwards, and a clerk who stays forty minutes in a trust room is a clerk doing something.

He did not take a note, and that was the first thing Aren noticed. He sat down again and put his copy of the finding flat on the table, and he asked the room one question, and it was a good question and it was asked in an ordinary voice: whether anybody in the room had a *record* — a document, a roll, a register — that touched the Alder Vale crossing, and not an account of what they had heard.

Nobody answered for some time.

"There is a document," said Aren Kest.

The clerk looked at him.

"There is a document in this county. It is entered in the record of this court as of the thirtieth of Mudmonth, and it has a chain of custody and a number, and the magistrate has read it and it is under her hand in a file in the provincial town, and I am not going to describe it in this room because a document in a room is a document every man in the room can remember the words of, and the fewer people who can remember the words of it the better it is for everybody including me."

"That is a careful answer," said the clerk, "and it is also the answer of a man who knows that a court which has a document in it is a court that has to do something about it." He wrote nothing, and then he said, almost to himself, in the voice of a man reading a rule he knows by heart: "*A registry entry once made may be traced and exhibited in any court of the county without further fee.*"

"That is on your form?" said the toll-holder's man.

"That is on every form," said the clerk. "It is the only generous sentence in the whole of the Crown's printed law and it is generous by accident, because it is written for a court that wants a thing produced and not for a registry that wants a thing kept." He put his pen in his pocket. "Mr Kest. I am going to ask you the only question I am going to ask, and I have no authority to ask it, and I am asking it because you have described a finding to this room as a structure and I would like to know whether you have thought about who builds on it next."

"I have thought about who reads it next."

"That is not the same question and I would be obliged if you would hear the difference, because a reader is a man with a copy and a builder is an office." The clerk stood and gathered his papers. "The marshal's office sent your watch captain a paper about a bell on the sixteenth of Mudmonth. That is a fact everybody in this room knows. If you have spent any time thinking about it, you have been thinking about the marshal's office, and so has everybody else in this county, and the marshal's office is four hundred miles away and cannot do anything about a bell in a fen town without a county to stand on. The county renews its power over the Alder Vale in clause three, annually, by description, to unnamed officers. The county holds the file. The county's man sits in rooms like this one and writes memoranda." He said it without any weight at all. "So when you stand in a room next month and you name an office, name it correctly, and understand that the office that reads the copy of the finding is the office that holds the file, and that the office that holds the file is the one that will be asked what the file is for."

He was at the door before anybody had thought of a question, and he was down the stair and gone into the street in the time it took Lysa Fenn to take her hand off the table and look at the door he had gone out of.

"It was the county," said the toll-holder's man.

"It was the county," said Lysa Fenn. "Mr Kest, you have this afternoon to say something else in a room, and I would advise you to say it in a room with fewer people in it, because the man who just left this one will write a memorandum and the memorandum will be about you."

"I named the marshal's office in front of eleven people," Aren said.

"You named an office to a room of eleven people on the morning a finding was read in it, having spent ten minutes telling that room that you can find the load path of a document." Lysa Fenn put the finding down and squared it. "I could not have made it plainer. Do you understand what has happened to this office this morning? Nothing has been done to it. Nothing has been done to you. A man has written down that the keeper of an archive filed a document about a county matter on the day before a renewal, and that a road warden stood up in a trust room and said the county was about to be asked about it. That is a memorandum. It costs nothing to write and it will be read by a man who decides what is worth reading, and by then you will both be somewhere else."

"I know what a memorandum is."

"Then you know it is not a thing you can be tried for and it is not a thing you can answer, and it is the cheapest weapon in the Crown's armoury and it has just been fired in this room by a clerk who did not have to raise his voice." She looked at him for a moment. "You did the reading well. I want that said. The clause is a sentence about masonry and nobody in this county has said so in eleven years, and when it is read out in a court on Sunday somebody will be asked about it and they will be a magistrate and not a mason, and it will have started in a trust room, and it started with you. I shall have to have you somewhere nearer to a bench than this, and I have not decided where, and I have two days."

The room emptied in the ordinary way, in ones and twos, and the carter went out muttering about four thousand, and the toll-holder's man took the finding down off the table and gave it back to the clerk's man because it was not the trust's paper.

Mara Vey stayed.

She had not been able to leave the room with the other people because she had a thing in her hand, and she came and put it on the table in front of Aren, and it was a sheet of printed paper, folded twice, and the fold had been made with a bone folder because everything in that building had been folded with a bone folder since 301.

"It came on the post this morning with the finding," she said. "I have read it four times and I have not known until about a minute ago whether I was supposed to be angry at it, and I now know that I am not, and that is worse."

"What is it?"

"A notice from the Crown registry at the provincial town, in answer to a notice of entry I filed at the fourth hour yesterday evening in a hand you have seen me use." She turned it round. "It acknowledges the entry. It expresses no opinion on it. And then it sets out, in a ruled table, a schedule of charges for the production of anything touching the entry, and there is a fee against each item, and one of the items is a search."

"How much is a search?"

"Four shillings to begin, and sixpence a year thereafter, and the whole schedule comes to about a mark and a half if a man is patient." She put her finger on a line near the bottom. "And it is signed by a man, and the man has a name, and the name is Halven, and he is a registrar, and he is not a villain. He is a man with a register, a family, and a liability for what is on the page he keeps."

"He quoted you four shillings in Hearthmonth for a roll that was with the marshal's office."

"He did, and I paid it, and it was worth it, and it told me a thing about how that office behaves." She refolded the paper along its own folds. "I want to tell you what that paper is, since you have been in this building twice and have earned an opinion. It is a charge. It is not a bribe and it is not a threat and there is nothing crooked in it anywhere, and it is the reason a Crown registry does not spend its mornings deciding what to hide. Every office in this kingdom that keeps a public book is allowed to charge for a search of it, and every office that keeps a public book and does not charge for a search is an office that is waiting to be asked whether it has been paid, and that is a question no keeper of records ever wants to be asked, so they charge, and the charge is honest, and it is the price of being allowed to keep the book at all."

"And it is a wall."

"It is a wall with a door in it, and the door is the size of a magistrate's order, and the door takes eleven days once it is stamped, and the fee is four shillings." She looked up. "I have four shillings and eleven days. That is the whole of my position, and I am telling you it plainly because you are the only person in this county who has put anything in my file and I would like there to be one person in my file who knows it."
