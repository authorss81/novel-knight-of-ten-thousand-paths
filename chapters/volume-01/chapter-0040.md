# Chapter 0040

The twenty-second of Longlight was a Saturday, and the bag had gone down Weir Street at the eighth hour that morning with a county's brown paper in it and a tallow seal with his thumb in it, and by the eighth hour the room above the cooper had a man in it doing the arithmetic for the fourth week running, and it came out the same as the other three except for one figure at the bottom of it, and he wrote the whole of it out because a man who only writes down the figures that are wrong is doing a kind of arithmetic that gets him into a court.

*One crossing of the year. It is pledged to the twenty-sixth of Longlight, at the lowest water, on a bench's day, in a city four days off, and there are four days.*

*The seat it would come out of is a block of the fourth course of the Old Toll sill with two discs of Steppe glass in it, and the glass has been used twice, and the man who cut it wrote on a page he gave me and would not charge for it that a scar takes two and that three is not a number he would sell to a stranger or a friend, and there is no fresh Steppe glass north of Merefen before the Steppe road comes down in Frostmonth, and the slip wants four marks the pair, money before cutting.*

*Three figures have not moved since the twentieth of Greenmonth: six marks on the guild roll against a chest in a trust in abeyance that nobody in this county can now be asked about; two marks and three quarters on a cutting contract at eight pence a day, a debt of mine to my brother and not of his to me; and eleven days of crew wages owed by the trust, none of which will ever be paid out of it.*

*In hand: eleven shillings and fourpence in a jar on a shelf, being the county's schedule at a shilling a day and a penny a mile and a culvert at Four Elms at a shilling a day with the materials the farmer's, less three weeks of eating, and the two shillings for the room comes out of the cutting contract and not out of the jar.*

*And the glass: four marks the pair. Four marks is sixteen shillings. I am four shillings and eightpence short of two discs, and there is no man in this county who will cut them without the money in his hand, and there is no money in this county that is mine, and that figure has not moved in a fortnight and it is the smallest number in this book and I have looked at it every night for a fortnight and it is not going to move, and a man who looks at a number every night for a fortnight is a man who has not understood that the number is not the problem.*

He put the candle on the wrong end of the table and read it.

Two things had changed in twenty-four hours and both of them were in a room over a cooper and neither of them was a thing that could be acted on.

The first was that the letter had gone. It had gone on the county's post at the eighth hour that morning, when the man came for it, in a county's brown paper with a disc of tallow on it and his thumb in the tallow, and it would be in the provincial town on the third day, and a clerk in a filing room would enter it, and a man in a provincial office would read it, and there was nothing left in the world for him to do about it and there never had been.

The second was that a barge mate had sat in a filing room yesterday afternoon, two flights below this one, and given an account of a Crown's man taking a number off a mark cut in a coping twice a year on the second pier of a bridge at a toll-house on the northern road, and that the number had been eight and was now eleven. And that on the twenty-sixth of Longlight, in four days, at the lowest water, two men and a clerk were going to mark the outer ring of a second pier four days off and write a number in a book. And that the two numbers, eight and eleven, had arrived in this county three weeks after the stone was marked, in a wharf-man's mouth, in the wrong words: the words that had come off the stone were *pier* and *ring* and *gone off a bit*, and the words that had come down Weir Street were *bridge* and *going*, and a pier and a ring is a thing four hundred yards up this road as well as a thing four days off, and he was the only person in the world who could tell which of the two it was and had been instructed not to go and could not go anyway.

He got the notebook out. He wrote the hour, and he wrote the second line, which was not about the seat.

*Twenty-second of Longlight, YR 312, eighth hour. The lowest water is on or about the twenty-sixth. Four days.*

*And here is the thing I have been turning over since yesterday afternoon and I am going to write it down in the plainest words there are, because if I do not write it down I will go on turning it over and a man who goes on turning a thing over instead of writing it down is a man doing what he did at the second hour on a deck in YR 301.*

*Eight and eleven.*

*I do not know whether the eleven is my ring.*

*That is the first thing and it took me a quarter of an hour to get to it because I have been reading a report about a bridge for two days and I have not once asked myself which bridge, and I asked it at the eighth hour this morning with a candle in my hand and it is the whole of the thing. A Crown's man and a boy with a book at a toll-house on the northern road, on a second pier, taking a mark twice a year off a coping. Two men and a clerk, at the lowest water, on a second pier, four days off, twice a year, on a coping. **The description is the description.** And there is no Crown in the city where mine is, and there is a Crown in the one where that is, and the two things are not the same and I have got nothing in this county that will tell me which one I am holding.*

*And I am not going to be able to find out. I have gone at it from four sides this morning and I have got four dead ends, and I am writing them down because a man who only writes down the one he can act on is a man building a case.*

*From the ground: a pier on a bridge in this country is not a second pier in a city four days off, and a mark cut in coping at a mill race is a mark cut in coping at a mill race, and there are a great many of them, and a man who says *the second pier* in a tap-room has not said where.*

*From the book: a Crown's registry keeps schedules of anchors and marks, and there is a volume of that registry entered on a press register in this county and not on its shelf, with the nineteenth of Mudmonth, YR 301, and the word *removed*, and no name and no office and no authority, and if that book had been on the shelf on the fifteenth of Greenmonth a man in a county town could have had this report off it in an afternoon. It was not on the shelf. It is not on the shelf now. **A stone is marked by somebody and the paper about it is not kept, and those two facts are about the same office and they were put in a book in two different counties eleven years apart and neither of them is in my hands.***

*From the number: a ring stone opens in the cold and shuts in the warmth, and every bridgewright in this world knows that and no surveyor has ever once had to say it, and a bench marks a ring twice a year precisely because a ring has two readings and not one. So even if the eleven is mine, the eight and the eleven are almost certainly not the same season, and the crack has almost certainly not moved three inches. And the boring version of the report is worse than the frightening version of it, and I want that written down on the same page, in the same night, because in a year I am going to be tempted to remember only the boring one.

*Because the boring one is this. A bench has been entering two numbers a year in a ring stone for four generations and has never once compared them, and has never been able to, because there is no rule that says which of the two is the number. So a bench can hold a ring and cannot tell from its own book whether it is standing still or going, and has never needed to tell, because until a writ came down off a river eleven years ago nobody in this country was marking that ring for anything but a bench's own order.

*And the number I want is the one four years ago, in either season. And I do not have it, and I cannot have it, and there is nobody in this country whose book has it, and the man who has it is four days off and is going to mark that ring on Wednesday with two men and a clerk and write a number in a book, and I am not going to be there.*

*And the report itself cannot be acted on by anybody. Not by me: I am instructed not to have contact, and there is no court behind that instruction, and there is also no seat, and there is also no glass, and there is also a woman on a ferry who will write in a book on Wednesday night that I was not there. Not by a bench: a bench in Merefen has no jurisdiction over a stone four days off and cannot ask a question about it, and any bench that tried would be a bench writing to a Crown about a Crown. Not by a magistrate: a magistrate can order a production and cannot order a man to put a rule on a ring, and she cannot order a Crown's surveyor to do it either, because the marking is his office's own work and not a matter anybody can produce. Not by the keeper of records, and she knows it, and she wrote five limits on the report yesterday afternoon and the fifth one is that the description is capable of being read of more than one bridge in more than one country and that she did not ask me which, on purpose, in writing, with the hour on it.*

*So a true account of a moving crack is in this county in a fair hand with the hour on it, and I cannot tell whose crack it is, and it cannot be acted on by anybody in it including the man it is about, and that is the whole of the position and I have got four days to do something about it and there are three things I can do. Two of them cost something I do not have. The third one is the thing I have never done, and it has been sitting there the whole four months and I have not once looked at it, and I am going to look at it on the twenty-second of Longlight at about the ninth hour and then I am going to decide, and I am going to do both of those things in a room, and not in a notebook, because a man who decides a thing in a notebook has decided it alone and that is the whole of the last eleven years.*

---

The first thing was the road, and he had four days.

The second thing was four marks, which is sixteen shillings, and he had eleven shillings and fourpence, and the difference was four shillings and eightpence, and there was no man in this county who would cut a pair of discs of Steppe glass on four shillings and eightpence, and no money in this county that was his, and the glass road comes down in Frostmonth at the earliest.

So those two were the road and the money, and the third thing was a rule a man in a yard said out loud in front of four men in Greenmonth and has not taken back, and it was the only one of the three that cost nothing but his own pride, and it was the one he had not looked at in four months.

He did the third thing, and it took him nine minutes to work out how, and he worked it out at the ninth hour on the twenty-second sitting on the sill of the Old Toll deck with his back against the parapet and the bell-shaped hole four feet away with moss in it, and when he had worked it out he sat there for about half an hour and did not write anything down, and then he went down Weir Street.

*Every man in this county who has ever made himself useful to an office has done it by being the one who can be got at. And I have been the one who can be got at for eleven years and it has cost me a valley once and a bench and a bridge season and eleven days' wages and a hand I have not got back the use of in rain, and this morning I have understood that the whole of the trade of the last four months has been to be legible in one place at a time, and a man legible in one place is a man who can be reached in one place, and the two things are the same thing and nobody ever tells you.*

*The only power a man in my position has got is that people can find out what he did. That is all. He cannot compel a court or a guild or a bench or a toll house or a city or a Crown or a bell. He can make himself legible, and legibility cuts both ways and it is worth the trouble anyway, and the reason it is worth the trouble is that a thing nobody can find out what you did about is a thing nobody can hold you to, and a man who cannot be held to anything is a man who is told what he thought, and I have been told what I thought for eleven years and I would like a book in this town that says it was mine.*

*And a legibility of one is a man who can be found. Three is a thing a court has to answer three times.*

---

"Weir Street, the tenth hour," he said, "and I want to say this in the yard and not in a room, and I want it said before anybody asks me why I am here, because I have come up this street about nine times since the sixth of Mudmonth and every one of them was about me."

The yard had four men on a second load of paving stone and the trestles were up and the iron was going every four seconds, and Tovan Kest put the chisel down.

"Go on."

"You told four men in this gateway on the twenty-eighth of Greenmonth that nothing that belongs to a man goes in this yard's book, and only a thing set, and that if a man brought you a document you would tell him to take it to the salt loft and say so in front of them. And it is the best rule anybody in this family has ever had and I am not here to get round it and I am not here to ask you to break it. It is in a minute of this bench and a man in a provincial office is going to be able to read this yard's rule out of a court file before the end of the year, and it is the only reason your book is worth anything."

"Then what."

"I am lodging a paper with the toll-holder this morning. The toll-holder's roll is a creditor's book and the paper goes into his chest and his roll is the only book in this county that a court in a hundred years will order a thing produced out of, because the roll says the thing is there and the roll is a record of what a man is owed." He put his hand flat on the trestle. "I am not asking you to hold it. I am asking you for a line."

"A line."

"A line in the yard's book, in your hand, with the day and the hour, saying that on the twenty-second of Longlight a paper was lodged with Dace Millward, holder of the tolls of the Old Toll crossing, by Aren Kest, and that it is a chain of four documents in one order, and that the yard does not hold it and has not seen the contents of it. I will bring you the hour it went in and you will put that hour on it and not one of your own choosing. That is a thing set. It is a fact about a paper and not a copy of the paper and not the paper. If a Crown's man comes up this street in a year and asks you for the paper, you have not got it and you can say so in a room and be right. If a court comes and asks whether the paper exists, you have got the fact and the hour and the name of the man it is with. And if the paper is lost, then in a year a man can find out that it was not lost by nobody, it was lost after the hour I bring you, on a day he can name, by a person, and there is one person in this county who is going to be able to say who."

Tovan Kest stood at the bench and looked at the iron going on the stone and did not say anything for about eight seconds, and then he said:

"That is a fact and not a document, and you have worked out the difference between your book and mine in about nine seconds standing on a bridge, and I have been trying to teach it to you for eleven years and you did it in nine seconds because you wanted something from me, and I am not going to be a man about that this morning. Write the line and I will write it in my own hand and rule a line over it, and I will put it at the foot of the page with the two other things, and I will not know what is in the paper and I will not ask you and you will not tell me unless I ask you twice, and I am going to ask you twice in about a year, and you are going to tell me the first time, and that is the arrangement."

"Agreed."

"One condition of my own and it is not a rule, it is a want." He picked the chisel back up. "If a woman comes up this street with a Crown's hand on something and it is a document, I will tell her to take it to the salt loft, and I will say so in front of the men. Including Mara Vey. And if she argues with me I will say it twice."

The watch house at the eleventh hour, in the doorway, in the rain, on a Saturday, and Captain Rell was in the doorway because it was his doorway and it was a wet Saturday and he had a shift.

"You said you would come on the Thursday and I did not send for you."

"You did not send for me," said Aren Kest, "and you said you were going to wait, and I came on the twentieth and I have not come since, and I am going to tell you why and then give you the thing, and the why is short and the thing is four lines off the returnable order you filled in on Saturday the fifteenth, which I have read a copy of in a guild roll and which you did not send me and did not have to."

Rell did not move.

"You filled it in. I have read it. You wrote *the office of keeper of the Bellweather hinge* in a form, with your own hand, on the fifteenth, and you posted it, and it is in a building two hundred miles off, and the county's own clerk has since refused to carry a returnable order on the same matter, which means that in about a year somebody is going to read your form and the county's refusal on the same morning, and the refusal is dated the eleventh of Longlight and yours is dated the fifteenth and yours is four days later and in a file, and in a file the later one is the answer to the earlier one."

"Say what you came to say."

"I came to say the thing you asked me for in trade terms, and the thing is this. **A county clerk cannot execute an order of his own superior court, and cannot, and did not, and has said so in writing, and in saying so he had to write down what the order was about.** It is on the back. It is in the bench's roll of the Merefen bridge guild, at the fifteenth of Longlight, in a fair hand, read out in a guild hall to eight men and a reeve. And the guild has answered nothing and taken no notice of it and minuted that it will answer nothing, and that is on the same page, with the date, and the guild is a town's body and not an officer of anything and the county has said so in the county's own hand.

"So the Crown's superior court asked a town's bench to account for a bell, and a county officer refused, and the refusal put the name of the office on the paper, and the paper came back to this town, and a bench of nine men read it out loud and wrote it into their own book. **You did not need that and I could not have got it for you and it is not in any file that anybody sent you.** It is the only thing in this business that was given away for nothing, and it was given away by a Crown's clerk who had to be able to refuse properly."

Rell stood in a doorway in the rain for about four seconds.

"That is the fourth time," he said.

"What is."

"That is the fourth time in a month that you have walked up a street and handed me a thing I did not know I had. The first was the number of the deck. The second was an answer to a question of my own, on a step in the rain on the tenth of Longlight, about who holds it at three in the morning, and I have written that answer down twice since and shown it to nobody. The third was a thing you did not hand me at all, which is a seat cut square in a block of the fourth course with three blows in the head of a pin and two used discs in it, and which I have gone up and looked at twice without asking you, because I am a man who reads a mark before he reads a name." He took his cap off and put it back on. "And now a county clerk's refusal on the back of a Crown's own paper, and I have had it in my pocket for a week and did not know I had it, and you are telling me that this is now the fourth time, and I am a watch captain in a fen town and I have been handed four facts this month by the man the whole of it is about, and I have not asked you for one of them and I do not intend to, and I would like you to know that I have begun to think it is a habit on your part and not a kindness, and if it is a habit then it is a habit that has cost this town four marks and six a year and a bench and a Crown's commendation on my own wall, and I would like it stopped before there is a fifth."

"Then I will not come up this street again," said Aren Kest, "except to look at a bridge, and you will not see me, and I am telling you that because you asked me once in a court in Greenmonth to be put nearer a bench and I did not do it and I am not going to do it, and this is the last thing I am going to hand you that I went and got for you."

"That was not what I asked for," said Rell, "and I know it was not, and you have just said a thing to make a man feel better and it has taken you eleven years to learn how to do that as well and I have watched you do it about six times, and I am going to say the last thing I am going to say on this step and then I am going to go and do a shift.

"You are not doing it for me. You are doing it because you are the only man in this county who can get up a stair, and every one of us who is standing still in this business is standing still because you went up, and the day you stop is the day this stops, and I have been in this town nineteen years and I have never met a man who could see that about himself, and you are the first, and I have no idea what to do with it, and I am going to go and do a shift."

He shut the door. It was still raining. The notice with the four nails in it was still on the outside of the building, and the hand laid flat on a book had not run, and there was a bell in a room inside with a lock on it, and the county clerk's refusal on the back of a returned paper was in a guild roll four hundred yards up a road, and none of those three things had cost anybody anything yet, and the fourth one would.

The salt-loft rooms at the twelfth hour, and two stairs, and a landing with a chalked joke on the wall that he read going up and did not say anything about, which was the first time in a month he had been in that building and not said something about it and he noticed himself not saying it and that was worth a paragraph he did not write.

Mara Vey had the report of the twenty-first on the table with her own nine hundred words face up on it, and the day-book open, and Sarn Oyelaran at the other end of the filing room with a sheet, and they had been at the return of the ninth of Rainmonth since the seventh hour, which is seventeen days off and a day's hard ride, and there were four columns of writing on the table that had been there since the seventh hour and would still be there at the fourth hour tomorrow.

"Sit down," said Mara Vey. "I have not seen you since the sixteenth of Longlight and I have got four things to say to you in the order I have to say them, and the first one is that I know something, and I am going to start with it, because if I start with anything else you will manage it and you will manage it well and I have not got three months in me to be managed well by.

"There is a letter in this town. I have known there is a letter in this town since the eighteenth of Longlight, and I have not asked and I have not written and I did not send the boy up the stair on the nineteenth to ask, and the reason is that a postman goes up Weir Street at the fourth hour every morning in front of my own window and on the eighteenth he went up it with a brown paper envelope under his arm and put it into a door four doors below me, and Sarn Oyelaran watched him do it from the press, and neither of us wrote it down, and then you would have come, and you would have come on the nineteenth and I would have been angry with you and I would have got over it by the twentieth, and I want you to know that I have been standing in this room for four days making a series of decisions about not knowing something, and that I have a rule for it, and the rule is the third rule on that wall, which I have never had to use on a person and have now had to use on a person twice in eleven years, and the second time was you.

"And the second thing," she said, "is that I have written the fact of it in the day-book, in the fair hand, this morning, before you got here, and I am going to read it to you, and you are not going to like the second line of it."

She read it out.

*That a sealed instrument in the hand of the marshal's office, bearing a device of a hand laid flat on a book, was delivered to the rooms of a tradesman of this town on the eighteenth of Longlight, YR 312, at the fourth hour; and that the said instrument was not received into this office and is not held in this office and its contents are not known to this office; and that this office has entered the fact of its delivery and not the instrument and not its contents, for the reason that a copy is a second thing that somebody can lose and a fact is a thing that can be looked for.*

*And that the said tradesman was on the eighteenth of Longlight in the possession of a document which this office has been told does not exist and which this office has not seen, and that this office has not asked for it and will not, and that this office's not asking is on its own account and against no person and with no opinion, and that this office has cost itself nothing by not asking, and knows that it has cost itself something, and has entered that too, with the hour.*

Sarn Oyelaran read the second line back with the hour against it, and did not look up.

"You have taken the price," said Aren Kest, "before you knew what it was."

"I have taken the price before I knew what it was, because the price of not asking was the price of not asking, and I did not need to know the cost of asking in order to price the not-asking, and that is the only thing about this trade that nobody has ever taught me and I worked it out about four years ago at about the fourth hour in the morning." She capped the ink. "And the third thing. Say your chain out loud and do not improve it, because I have read a good many chains in four years and every one of them has been improved by the man it belongs to."

So he said it out loud, standing up, in order, with the date and the hour against each piece, and he said three pieces, and then he stopped.

"First: a sworn account in my own hand, filed in a magistrate's court on the thirtieth of Mudmonth, YR 312, with a claim number and a chain of custody with three hands, and a finding of the fourth of Greenmonth beneath it in a county file two hundred miles off. Second: a minute of the bench of the Merefen bridge guild of the fifteenth of Longlight, YR 312, with the resolution, and with a returnable order of the provincial court returned unexecuted by the county, and on the back of it, in a county clerk's hand, the matter, which names the office of keeper of the Bellweather hinge. Third: a report entered in this office on the twenty-first of Longlight at the fourth hour, of a Crown mark and a number taken twice a year upon the second pier of a crossing at a toll-house on the northern road, and the five limits, and the statement that this office does not know which crossing it is."

"And the fourth," said Mara Vey.

"There is a fourth," said Aren Kest, "and it is in a hand and not in a claim, and it is in my coat, and it is the fourth thing in this county that says that crossing is not a fever pit, and I have not got it in this room and I did not bring it up the stair, and I have not brought it up the stair on purpose and I would like that noticed before I am asked."

"And the letter," said Mara Vey.

"And the letter is the fifth," said Aren Kest, "and the letter is not in the chain, and the letter is a Crown's own paper of eleven hundred words that sets out everything the Crown knows about me, signed and sealed, and it is the only document in my possession that is the other side's own account of what I did, and it is in a room over a cooper and it is not going up a stair and it is not going into this press and it is not going into a bench's roll and it is not going into a yard's book, and I have not written its name down anywhere in this county except on an envelope that has gone to the post, and that is the fifth piece and it is in a rented room and there is no chain of custody on it and there never will be and in about a year I am going to have to say so in a room to somebody."

Nobody said anything for a while. The filing room was very quiet, with the light coming in sideways off the river.

"Now," said Mara Vey. "Give me the fourth thing properly. You have said there are three and you have said there is a fourth and you have not said what it is, and you have said the letter twice, and you have said it in the order you would want it read, and that is the order of a man who has been managing somebody for eleven years."

"I have not got a fourth thing."

"You have," said Mara Vey, "and it is that you have just stood in this room and laid three documents out on my table in the order you would want them read, and you have put a date and an hour on each, and you have told me there is a fourth, and you have told me the letter is the best one of the five and the only one of the five that is not going to be on my table, and you have not once handed me a piece of paper, and you have therefore not once done the thing that this office exists to do, which is to take custody of a document and put a chain on it."

"You have taken the price of not asking and I have noticed you have noticed it and I would like to be allowed to be a man who is allowed one thing."

"Be allowed it," said Mara Vey, "and here it is, and it is the only thing I have got and it is not mine. **You have come up this stair with five things and no paper.** You have done the most careful thing anybody has done in this building in four years and you have done it in a filing room with a woman in it who has spent four years telling men to bring her the paper.

"And the reason you did it, and I have worked it out, is that you have finally understood that the *record* of a thing and the *thing* are two different instruments and only one of them can be risked, and the record is the one that survives a person, and the record is the thing that does not have a Crown's seal on it, and the record is the one that a court has to answer three times."

"No," said Aren Kest. "The reason is that I have four marks a year of this town's money, of which eleven shillings is the second floor of a salt merchant's house, and a woman in this room who put four costs on a table in Mudmonth and got all four of them paid, and the record is the part I can afford."

"Yes," said Mara Vey. "That is also the reason. Both of them are always both of them and I have never in four years met a keeper who could hold both of them at once and I have not met a trader who could either, and you have just held both of them at once in a room in about nine seconds and I am going to be annoyed about it for a week."

She pulled the day's sheet round.

"Now the order of the four things, and then the cost, and then I am going to say no to something and you are going to have to carry it up Weir Street on your own."

"Go on."

"The chain is good. The order is wrong."

"The order is the order they happened in."

"The order is the order they happened in and a chain is not a diary." She wrote it out on the sheet in three columns and turned it round. "A chain has a beginning and it has the thing that goes with the beginning and it has the place the thing is kept, and every other piece has to be findable from the first one by somebody who is hostile. From the account of the thirtieth of Mudmonth a hostile man can find the finding of the fourth of Greenmonth, because the account is beneath it in the same file and a file has an order. From the finding of the fourth he can find the minute of the fifteenth of Longlight, because the finding is the notice clause and the notice is dated and any office in this county can look for a dated notice. From the minute of the fifteenth he can find the returnable order, because the minute names it. And from the returnable order he comes back to the office of keeper of the Bellweather hinge, which is the one phrase that is in three of the four pieces and is in none of the four by itself, and that phrase is the spine, and the spine is not yours and it is not mine and it is a county clerk's hand on the back of a refused paper, and it was put there for free, by a Crown's officer, on the eleventh of Longlight, to enable him to refuse properly.

"That is a chain. That is a thing a man in a provincial office can follow in an afternoon and every step of it is a document he can ask a court for and be given. And the fourth piece, your own account of nineteen days in a city four days off, is the only piece in it that nothing points at, and it is the only piece in it that is only in your hand, and if you lodge it with the toll-holder and the toll-holder's chest is shut or the roll is mislaid or he dies and his heir is a fool, that chain loses its only piece that is nobody's document but yours, and it was the only piece in the world that said a crossing was not a fever pit."

"So it goes in the press."

"**No.**" She said it fast. "And this is the no, and I have had it since the seventh hour this morning and I have been waiting for you to make me say it, and you have not made me say it, which is a thing I have been trying to get you to do for two months and I have stopped, and I am going to say the rest of it without being asked and then I am going to be angry with you about it for a day.

"The writ of the ninth of Longlight, in its second paragraph, forbids this office to move, copy out, or part with any record held by it, pending an enquiry returnable on the ninth of Rainmonth. And a chain of four documents in one order with a date and an hour on each, lodged in a creditor's book, is a record of the highest order, and if this office holds a copy of it, then on the ninth of Rainmonth I stand in a court with nine hundred words in a form and a return in the spaces, and one of the spaces is a list of what this office holds, and I fill that list, and in that list is a chain of four documents about the office of keeper of the Bellweather hinge in a matter in which a Crown's registry keeps a schedule of a crossing that the county's own finding says was never built, and the day I read that list out is the day the writ has what it was written for and I have handed it over myself, in my own fair hand, in a court, correctly.

"So the chain does not come in this press. And it does not go in the yard's book, because your brother has said out loud in a gateway in front of four men that nothing that belongs to a man goes in it, and that is the only reason the yard's book is worth a thing in a year, and I would rather have your brother right in a gateway than have one more document in Merefen."

"Then it is in the toll-house and nowhere else."

"It is in the toll-house and in **three** places, and the third one is not mine, and I have been working on it since the seventh hour and I have got it, and I am going to tell you the answer and then I am going to tell you the price, and the price is mine and not yours and I have been sitting with it since the seventh hour and I have got a name for it and the name is *a lending*."

She wrote it out and turned it round.

"**A lending is not a custody.** This office will hold a copy of the chain, and it will do so as a lender and not as a keeper, on the following terms, which I am going to enter in the day-book and read to you before I enter it.

"The chain is the property of a person and not of this office. This office holds one copy by lending, for the term of a year, and may produce it, may exhibit it, may read it, and may hand it to a court, and does not assert any claim, right, or custody in it. The holder is the toll-holder of the Old Toll crossing, whose roll is a creditor's book and whose chest is a creditor's chest, and to whom a document delivered on the twenty-second of Longlight, YR 312, at the fourth hour of the afternoon, is a matter of record with a date and an hour. And the person who made the chain is a tradesman of this town, on Weir Street, and not a party to any business of this office, and is not a claimant in this office, and this office has entered the seventh claim number in four years against nobody, on its own account, and it is not going to enter an eighth against the same man because he came up a stair with a piece of work.

"And on the ninth of Rainmonth, when I answer that writ in a form in a court, I am going to list the lending, with the date, and I am not going to lie to a court about what is in my building, and that is the last of the no and it is the one that matters, and I have been arguing with myself about it since the seventh hour and I have got it and it took me four hours and I am not going to tell you what the other answers were."

She read the terms back twice and put them in the day-book in the fair hand with the hour, and then she sat back.

"And now the cost, and I am naming it because that is what this room is for and because you have costed yourself twice tonight in a notebook and a man who is costing things alone in a room is doing a thing I have watched four men do and it never once came out right.

"It is this. When that writ is answered, on the ninth of Rainmonth, in a room in the county town, the list of what this office holds will contain a lending, and the lending will name a tradesman of this town, and the tradesman is not named in it, and the *date* is. **The twenty-second of Longlight.** And there is a provincial clerk with a schedule of that man's movements in a file four days off with nothing on it but dates, and a memorandum asking who else he has told, and there is a woman's name in that memorandum and it is mine, and a form with a column on it for names.

"So the answer I give on the ninth of Rainmonth tells that office, in my hand, that there is a document about an office that does not exist, made on a named day, held outside the office that is under enquiry, by a man in this county. And there is nothing I can do about that. I have known it since the fourth hour this morning and I have been at the return of the ninth of Rainmonth for six hours working on three answers and I have got one, and it is the right one, and it is going to cost me the only asset this office has, which is being dull, and I told a man in a yard in Greenmonth that I would never be able to be dull about anything again and I have been wrong about that by four months and I am not going to be sentimental about it in a room."

"It is not your cost," said Aren Kest. "It is being made your cost by me, and if I do not put the chain in at all then nothing happens and you have nothing to answer on the ninth of Rainmonth except nine hundred words of a return in the spaces and the writ runs out."

"Yes," said Mara Vey. "And that is the reason I am going to say yes, and I want it entered in the day-book that I said yes to the argument and not to the chain, because there is a difference and in about a year it is going to be the whole of the difference. **Write the fourth thing and bring it down and I will make the copy before the fourth hour tonight**, and then go and lodge the whole of it with the man who was sixty-one years old when I first sent a boy up that road and is sixty-one now and whose roll has three things in it that are not tolls."

---

The fourth thing took him until the fourth hour of the afternoon to write and it took him two goes, and he wrote it in the room above the cooper and carried it down Weir Street in his coat the way he has carried the field book for eleven years, and it was still in the coat when he handed it over.

The first go was nineteen days, in order, in the trade's flat hand: a gate bridge approach at the fourth hour and a third of the third bay not there; a slip and a hull and a working that failed and a boy of eighteen who opened his hand on the iron; a bench sitting on the eighteenth day at the ninth hour and a small oilcloth bundle on the table; a field book going into a hall's keeping; a counting house and four hundred leaves and a name read twice; a ferrywright's five terms; a woman's line on a bench's page.

The second go was the thing he had never put in a document in his life, and he put it in now, and it is four lines, and it is the reason the fourth piece is worth more than the first three:

*And that the crossing is not a fever pit, and that this is entered on my own account and against nobody, and that the man who made this has no witness to it and no copy of it, and that a record with no witness and no copy is the only kind there is that nobody can be got to change, and that the reason it has been kept out of every file in this county for eleven years is that it has been in a coat, and that it is in a coat still, and that I have this day for the first time decided that a thing in a coat is a thing that can be lost, and that a thing that can be lost is not a record, and that the letter stays where it is, and there is no sixth piece, and I have written that down so that somebody in eleven years will know that I knew it in this year and did nothing about it and why.*

He read it back in a filing room over a salt merchant and a woman wrote it out in a fair hand and it took her fifty minutes, and it was the last entry in the day-book that month that had no price against it.

---

Dace Millward was in the toll-house at the fourth hour of the afternoon of the twenty-second of Longlight with his hat on, and the chain was eleven sheets, because four documents do not fit on one, and he read the first one and then did not read the other ten, and he said so, and asked for them to be turned round in a bundle so that he could not read them by accident, which was the act of a careful creditor and the reason a hundred years from now there will be a copy.

"Entry," he said. "Say the words and I shall write them and you shall read them over my shoulder and not behind me."

"Seven lines. The first is what it is. The second is the day and the hour. The third is that it was made by a tradesman of this town and is his and not mine. The fourth is that it is four documents in one order, with a date and an hour against each, and I have said what the four are. The fifth is that the toll-house holds it in a chest under this counter in a wrapper, and that this roll is the record of it and not the paper. The sixth is that no copy has been made except one, by a lending, and that the lending is entered in a day-book in a room over a salt merchant and is not in my roll and is not in yours. The seventh is yours and I have written it and you may cross it out."

Millward read the seventh line, which said: *and this roll is the only place in this county where a thing is kept because somebody was owed money, and that is not an accident, and it is not going to be forgotten in a hurry.*

"That is my line," he said. "You have written my line. I said it at that counter to a woman with a sheet in her hand twelve days ago and I have been saying it in a counting house for two years and nobody has ever written it down for me, and you have put it in a claim." He put the pen down and picked it up again. "I am not going to cross it out, and I am going to put it in my own hand underneath, and I am going to initial the thing twice."

He wrote the seven lines, and then the eight, and then he said, without looking up:

"Now I am going to ask you what it is worth to you and you are going to say nothing, and I have asked twice now and I have had nothing twice, and I want to ask it a third time in a room in a year, and I want you to have three weeks to think of something better than nothing.

"And I am going to tell you what it costs me, because she told me the cost of a thing on the tenth before she handed it over and I have not forgotten it and I am not going to let a woman hand you something for nothing while I sit here and take a fourth.

"My winter half was eight marks and ten shillings and it is suspended from the first of Longlight by a letter with a seal on it and a word in it that does not exist, and I have not taken a farthing of it and I am not going to. In that roll there are now **four** things that are not tolls: a suspension, a direction about correspondence, a town's charter, and this. And I have money out on the wharf frontage that has been out on the wharf frontage eleven years, and a man in a counting house reads my roll once a year in Harvestmonth and has read it for eleven years and has never once stopped at a line, and this year he will stop at four, and the year after that it will be three times, because that is what a book does. **A book with three things in it that are not business has become a record, and a creditor does not want to be a record.**"

"Then do not do it."

"**I have already written it in**, and I wrote it in before you came up the street, and I told you in this office in Mudmonth that I would rather be a man who did that than a man who found out in a year that the road shut for a reason nobody wrote down." He put the pen down. "And the reason is not courage. It is that there has never in two years been a single day when anybody in this county wanted a page out of my roll, and there is now, and I am sixty-one, and I would like to be a man who was useful to something once, in a way that cost somebody, and did not lie about it."

---

The third place was the yard, at the fifth hour, and it was a line and not a paper, and Tovan Kest wrote it at the foot of the page under the two other things and ruled a line over it.

*A paper was lodged with Dace Millward, holder of the tolls of the Old Toll crossing, at the fourth hour of the afternoon of the twenty-second of Longlight, YR 312, by Aren Kest. The paper is a chain of four documents in one order, with a date and an hour against each. It is not held in this yard and has not been seen in this yard and its contents are not known in this yard. The yard knows it exists, and the day, and the hour, and the man it is with.*

And under that, in his own hand, a little after the fifth hour, in the space he had ruled at the foot of a page in Greenmonth without knowing he was doing it:

*The river is down and the lowest water is on or about the twenty-sixth. The seat is cut and it has two used discs in it and there is no glass to be had for a third before Frostmonth. I am not going to say anything else about it in this book.*

---

He went up the Old Toll road after the yard had shut, with a lamp, and put his left hand on the sill in the fourth course, and there was nothing in it: cold water, and stone, and a two-disc seat cut square in a block by his own hand on the eighth of Greenmonth, used twice, with a maker's mark under the near disc, and a bell-shaped hole in the parapet four feet away with the moss back over the whole of the swept rectangle at last, and a seal on a post that has not run and will not run in a hundred days.

Four days. He had known there would be nothing in it, and doing it anyway was the fourth day of a thing he had decided was a habit and not a trade.

A road that shuts when the money stops, and the money stopped for a reason a magistrate wrote down, and an instruction from a room two hundred miles off that says he shall have no contact, and does not need a court, and cannot be answered and cannot be refused, because it is a sentence in a letter and not an order.

A crack in a ring four days off that is eight inches, or eleven, or eleven inches on a bridge in this county that a Crown's man puts a rule to twice a year, and he cannot tell which and there is nobody he can ask, and the man who can read it is going to mark it on Wednesday with two men and a clerk, and the woman who keeps that road is going to write in a book at the end of Wednesday that he was not there, and a date is a thing that side keeps.

And then there was a third place, on a bridge, four hundred yards from his own bed, and a woman on a ferry had put it there without being asked and without wanting anything for it, and the only thing he had done about it was to be nine minutes late.

He did not go. That was the decision and it was made at the ninth hour, on the deck of a bridge four hundred yards from his own bed, and it had not moved since, and it was not a decision of courage and it was not a decision of despair, and it was the first decision of his life that he had made on a day with no crossing in it at all, and there was nothing on either side of it and nobody to see it, and the road did what it always did.

---

*Four days off. From the north side, and nothing of this reaches that room and nothing of that room reaches this one, and there is no door between them today and there was no door between them yesterday, and the man in Weir Street does not know any of what follows and is not going to be told.*

*On the morning of the twenty-sixth the river was at its lowest in eleven years, and the bench went down to the second pier and did the thing a bench does at the lowest water, which is the thing they had promised a date for in a hall, and two men and a clerk put a rule on a ring stone and wrote a number in a book, and the clerk read it back, and it was eight inches, and it was eight inches at the last marking as well, and nobody in the hall found that interesting.*

*And the woman who keeps that road stood on the coping with her hands open because she had been told twice to keep them open on this one, and she kept them open, and nothing came, and there was nothing to come, and that is the whole of what that morning was.*

*What Bellweather-Living spent that morning arguing about was not the number. It was whether the road they had agreed in a hall on the eighteenth day, in writing, with a date on it, was a road, or a door, or a hole in a wall that a man on the other side could put his hand through whenever it suited him — and it was asked out loud, in a hall, by about forty people, and the woman who kept the road stood up and said that it was the second question and not the first, and that the first had been answered on the eighteenth day in a book with a clerk's entry in it, and that she had a name and the road was visible and the date was a date, and that anybody who wanted the other question answered could come and ask her it in daylight like a person.*

*About nine people did.*
