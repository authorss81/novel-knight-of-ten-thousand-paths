# Chapter 0006

The third slip of Bellweather was cut into the north bank below the fish steps, and it smelled of hot pitch, and the water in it was the colour of a knife.

A hull stood on the blocks in the middle of it: a ferry, thirty-two feet, flat-bottomed, hauled out on her ways with her bilge up and a jury of shore timbers under her, and her garboard split from the garboard knuckle aft nine feet, which is the worst place on a flat-bottom to open, because that is where the boat's whole argument with the water lives.

Eleven of the crew stood about and did not touch it. A hull-hand of perhaps eighteen sat on the slip wall with his knees up, and he had a bar across his knees that he had not been asked to fetch. Two children were on the shed roof. A woman in a leather apron was rolling a two hundred pound stone up a plank ramp on a handbar, which is a job for two men and which she was doing alone out of what could only be temper.

"That's the stranger off the steps," somebody said.

"He says he's a bridgewright."

"He says a great many things. I heard him give the arch a lecture to a washerwoman."

The woman with the bar put the stone down in the cut of the slip wall where a bollard stone has stood for a hundred years and a new one had just been made, and turned round.

She was thirty-four and she had a ferrywright's forearms and a ferrywright's way of standing, which is with her weight slightly back and her hands loose, and she looked at Aren Kest and then at his bar and then at the split in the garboard, and did not ask who he was.

"You've been in the city a quarter of an hour," she said. "I'll save you the introductions. I'm Quen. Ferrywright. That hull is mine, the crew is mine, the timber's mine, the block-wood for the scarf is cut and stacked and the new strake is bent and there's nothing in this job that anybody who isn't standing in it can do anything about except tell us how we are doing." She nodded at the hull. "The bench sent for a reader of timber. You'll do. You'll stand there and tell us what's wrong with a boat, and if you're right, you'll be useful, and if you're wrong, you'll be back over that wall and nobody will have wasted an afternoon."

"I can read timber," Aren said. "And I'm telling you the split is not a split, it's a scarf that opened. There's a scarf in the garboard and the fastenings have let go aft of it, and the fastenings were never renewed, and the fastenings are iron and the boat's had eleven years in salt water."

"Two hundred and eleven months," said Quen.

"Then it's twice as long as I said."

"And you'll want to put a new scarf in and a new strake and four new fastenings and a new rubrail on the port side because it's the same age and the same water, and you'll want to have it on the water by the tide at the sixth hour tomorrow, and you'll want a man on the frame holding it while the block goes in, because a hull's whole weight changes when you take a plank out of her and every man in this yard has been doing this for eleven years and knows which way she'll move."

"Yes," said Aren. "That's right. Who is holding the frame?"

Quen looked at him for a moment.

"I will tell you the last of it, because you are going to be worked into this yard like weather," she said, "and then I will tell you the thing I have been waiting to say, and I would rather say it now while you have nothing to be grateful for."

She put one boot on the side of the hull.

"Here is what I am not going to do. I am not going to be grateful to you. If I am grateful to you, then in six weeks there is a thing between me and you that I cannot put a price on, and you will have it, and you will be able to use it, and I would rather be in your debt for a bell I have already heard four hundred people describe to me this morning, and a day of holding a stone, than in your debt for something I cannot write in a ledger. A debt owed to a grateful stranger is not a debt. It is a leash. I'll hold the leash or I'll hold the account, and I know which of those two is worth more in a year, and it is the account." She took her boot off. "Say that back to me so I know you heard it and not just that you heard words."

"I heard it," Aren said. "I don't know that it's wrong."

"That," said Quen, "is the part I expect to be arguing with a stranger from the north for a fortnight. Come and stand on the ways."

He got up on the blocks.

From up there the job was a different shape. The split was open along the garboard knuckle for nine feet, and the fastenings had corroded back into the timber, and the bilge was full of rain and black water, and the two shore timbers under her were chocked in a hurry by somebody who had wanted her up and off the slip more than he had wanted her level.

"We can hold her," Aren said. "Let me hold her. Give me the frame and a couple of minutes and I'll keep her while you take the plank out, and then the block goes in and nobody has to hold his breath about the ways."

Two of the crew looked at Quen. The hull-hand on the wall looked at Aren.

"Which working?" said Quen.

"The usual one," Aren said. "You anchor on the frame and I hold it, and it holds while we work. You've got a working on that hull already, from the fastenings; I'd put my own promise in beside yours and it'll be twice the strength."

"It's not strength that's the problem." Quen came up on the blocks beside him and pointed with two fingers at the bilge, at the split, at the timber. "You have not looked at what you'd be anchoring on. You've looked at my boat. My boat is the thing to be mended. The thing to be bound is that scarf and the eight feet of garboard round it, and if you put a promise on *the frame* of a hull with a wound in her garboard, the frame is the wound's next doorway. Where do you think the force goes, friend? It goes into the thing you promised. That is the whole law of it. You promise a thing to hold, and the hold is paid for by that thing, and if the thing is the frame of a boat that has a split in her bottom, the force goes through the split and out through the garboard and you will find out exactly where by hearing a young man scream."

"Then bind the scarf," Aren said. "Narrowly. Eight feet."

"And what holds the scarf?"

"The block," said Quen. "A new scarf in soft green timber and a four-inch block under it will hold the whole weight of this hull off the joint for as long as it takes to fasten her, and it will hold it because it is four inches of oak and it is in compression, and it does not ask anything of her. That is what the block is for. That is what it has always been for, and it is the oldest thing in this trade and nobody who does this work for a living will ever do it the other way, because the other way puts a promise into the one part of the boat that is already open."

"And if I promise the frame?" Aren said. "In a narrow line. Just the frame, just the joint, just until the block is in."

"Then I will have to say no to you twice," said Quen, "and the second time I will have to say it with my hands, and I would rather you took the first time." She was not angry. That was the thing Aren had not been ready for. "The bench will not take a foreign command, and neither will I, and neither would your marshal's clerk if you had one and if he came here and told me my own yard was a Crown asset, which is a thing that happened to the foundry two years ago and which the foundry still will not discuss at a full table. We maintain it. We do not hold it. Do you know the difference, in your language?"

"A steward maintains," said Aren. "You've got stewards here. I've used the word for a man who keeps a road."

"A steward maintains, and a keeper is a word for a man somebody else has appointed to own it." She said it the way a person says a thing they have said before, in rooms, more than once. "You'll hear the other word a great deal in the north. It is not ours, and it is not the same, and if it comes up here you will want to know whose mouth it came out of."

The hull-hand on the slip wall had not moved, and now Aren noticed that he had not moved and that he was about eighteen and had the two hands of somebody who works rope.

"You. Get a bar and stand where I point," Aren said, "and when I say *down*, put your weight on it and do not move it whatever happens."

The hull-hand did not ask why. He got a bar off the rack and stood where the finger pointed, which was on the far side of the plank, out of the way, where a boy stands when a bigger man has not yet decided whether he is allowed to be there.

Aren Kest went down into the bilge with his right hand flat on the frame, on the timber above the scarf, on the eight feet of garboard that a stranger had told him twice this morning was the wound's next doorway, and he made a promise, and it was the promise he made about a batten at Sedge Reach, doubled, the one he had been making for eleven years in one form or another, and the four parts of it went into place without anybody teaching him, which is the thing about a craft and the thing that makes a man dangerous in it.

The anchor was the frame. The bound was the joint and the eight feet round it, and it was as narrow as he could make it. The counterweight was his right hand, and the scar in it, and the fact that he was going to be the one who paid for it and nobody else. The witness was the hull-hand, with a bar, who was going to be able to say afterwards that a man in the bilge had held the frame while a plank came out of it.

He had a witness, and he had chosen him himself, and he did not consider, for one moment, that a witness chosen by the man doing the promising is not a witness at all.

The frame held.

He felt it go, the way you feel a load take up, and it was a good strong hold, better than a batten, and the timber came up to the work with him, and he heard the boy's breath go short over his head.

"Take her out," he said. "I'll keep it. Take the plank out and do it slow."

They took the plank out.

The fastenings came out of the timber the way a tooth comes out of a jaw, and the plank was a long green strake of eight feet with nine fastenings in it, and two of the men had it on the blocks and were lowering it when the frame let go.

It let go because the frame had nothing left to promise with. The bind did not fail. It had never failed. It did what a bind does when the object it is bound to is itself losing the argument with the river: it transferred.

The eight feet of garboard opened like a shutter. The force came down the frame and out through the wound and the seam of the split ran three feet forward in one piece, and the whole side of the bilge jumped inward, and the hull-hand, who was holding a bar over a shifting piece of green timber with a two hundred pound stone on the other side of it, had his weight on it at the wrong moment and his right hand went along the bar and opened on the iron.

It was not a bad injury. It was a hand that would be stiff by morning and useless for a week and would leave a young man who worked rope for a living with a bad joint in it forever, and it was made in one second by a man who had promised a boat what he should have promised a block of oak.

The slip went quiet in a way that Aren had heard four times in eleven years and had never once been on the other side of.

Quen said, in a level voice: "Get him off the ways. Don't lift him, roll the timber — "Get him off the ways. Don't lift him, roll the timber — the chock, put the chock under his hand. Off. Off the ways, all of you."

They got the boy off the ways. Somebody put a rag on the hand. Somebody else started laughing, high and wrong, in the shed.

Quen came down off the blocks and stood in front of Aren, who was still in the bilge with his hand on a frame that was not holding anything.

She did not shout at him. He would have preferred it.

"You promised the wound's doorway," she said. "I told you twice. A man with a bar in his hand is not a witness, friend. He is a boy you have chosen to be standing there. A witness is somebody who can say *I would have said no to this* and mean it, and you picked one who is eighteen and has never refused a stranger in his life." She looked at the open seam, and then at the hand on the slip wall, and then back. "Now. You are going to want to do it again properly, and I am going to let you, because you can read timber and we are four days into the season and the tide is what it is. Understand the terms first."

"Terms."

"My name is Sera," said Quen, "and I am not going to say it again, and you will use it once and then you will use the work. You are the counterweight," said Quen. "Not the anchor. Not the bound. The cost. You hold the stone, you hold the scarf, and the cost of this working comes out of you and not out of that boy. And the witness is not chosen by the man who does the working. He will not stand there again, and that is not a punishment, that is the truth of what he is. The witness is whoever would have refused. If I do the working, the witness is you — and you would have refused, and you should say so in front of the crew, and you should say it now, in words, with your mouth, because when it is asked for later the words are what will be there."

Aren Kest stood in a bilge in a foreign city with a stranger's ruin nine feet in front of him and said, to a woman he had met an hour ago, in front of eleven people:

"I would have refused it. He did the right thing with the bar. I should not have put him there."

"You," said Quen to the boy on the wall, "did you refuse him?"

"No, ma'am," said the boy.

"Then it stands, and the working has no witness in it, and I will not pretend otherwise in the ledger." She turned back to Aren. "You understand what that means. It means the first thing that happened on this slip today cannot be denied by anybody who was standing here, and it will go in the yard book under my hand, and there is now a line in this city's record that says a man from the north came to a hull in Bellweather and put his own working into a boat's frame and opened her side. You will not be able to have that back. Do you understand me?"

"Yes," said Aren.

"Good." She shouldered the bar off the chock and nodded at the stones. "Now do it my way, and hold still, and hold nothing that can be asked a question."

They cut a new scarf and fitted it, which took until the flood tide, and the fastenings were iron, and the block-wood was already cut, and the new strake was already bent, and the only thing in that yard that was not ready on the morning of the eighteenth was the man.

The block went under the scarf. Quen put her own hand on the new scarf and made her own promise, narrow, out loud, in the trade's words, so that the whole yard heard the bound and the terms of it, because that is how a working is witnessed: not by a signature, but by other people who know what they heard and will say they heard it.

Aren Kest put both hands on the bollard stone in the cut of the slip wall, which was a hundred years old and had held boats before, and he held it, and he did not ask anything of it, and he let the working be hers.

The hull took her new plank. The fastenings went home, warm, and were clenched and caulked, and the strake went on, and by the fourth hour of the afternoon the death bell had rung and the slip was quiet and the boat was a boat again with nine feet of new garboard and one bad joint in a boy's hand.

Aren came up off the blocks with his right hand cut open along the second joint where he had put it on the frame, and blood dried in the crease of it, and the pain would be there for a week and the stiffness longer.

Quen walked with him to the gate.

"You have learned one movement," she said. "Hold the stone. That is all you have learned, and you have learned it in my yard, and it works here and it will work at the end of my slip and it will not work at the end of any slip you have not stood in twice. Anyone who tells you a thing travels in you is selling you a thing they have not taught." She stopped at the gate. "A man came once before you, from the west, about six years back, and he told us that a working could be carried and used anywhere, and we have been short of hands ever since, because we sent four of our best out to try it."

"Did it work?"

"It worked at the end of his own slip," said Quen, "and nowhere else, and he did not want to hear that, and he left in a temper, and the four of them did not come home in a good frame of mind and we lost two. If you are honest, you will be more use to this city than he was, and you will be no use at all in a place where nobody has ever seen you stand."

At the gate of the yard the bench-clerk was waiting with a slip of paper and a boy holding a lamp, and the boy was a boy and the clerk was a clerk and neither of them was a person, and the clerk said:

"The bench sits at the ninth hour. You will attend. You will be asked one question and you may answer it once."

"And if I want to ask one?"

"Then you may be asked a different one," said the clerk, "which is how it works, and which the bench has found to be the only method that has never yet produced a lie."

He put the paper in Aren's hand.

"The bench does not want your money," said the bench-clerk. "The bench cannot use your money. The bench wants something, and it wants it before it will answer the question, and it is not a thing you can buy, and I am instructed to tell you that, so that you may come at the ninth hour knowing it and not be insulted in front of eleven witnesses."

Aren Kest read the paper. It said a time and a room and four words underneath, in the clerk's careful hand, which were:

*The bell remembers the night.*
