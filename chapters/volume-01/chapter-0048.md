# Chapter 0048

The page came down Weir Street in a cart on the morning of the second of Fallowmonth at about the ninth hour, wrapped in oiled linen with a cord on it, and it was carried by a woman of about fifty with iron dust in the creases of her hands who put her hand flat on the deck of the cart and said one sentence in a trade's flat register, to nobody in particular, in a market that was on a bridge it was not built for.

"**The line is holding. Tell me the line is holding.**"

And Tovan Kest, who was in the cart because his brother could not be got down the stairs, said: "It is holding. I have got one end of it in my hand."

The woman looked at him, and looked at his hand, and did not ask a single question, and that was the whole of her, and Tovan Kest, who has said the true thing in a public room three times in five months and has not voted once, looked at the hand on the line and did not ask one question, and did not look at his brother, and went back to the job he was at, which is the only way he has ever found of being kind to anybody.

---

The office had been petrified for twenty-four days, which is thirty days less six, and it had cost two of them four shillings eightpence each in a boy's wages and a boy's time at a shilling a day, and on the second of Fallowmonth, at the tenth hour, the writ of the ninth of Longlight was still running and would keep running until the ninth of Rainmonth, and the thing that came up that stair was not a record and therefore was not caught by the bar, and the keeper of the Bellweather Claims Archive read its wrapper on the landing with her coat on.

Then she took it into the filing room and shut the door and read it twice and then went and stood at the window for about ten minutes, which Sarn Oyelaran timed, and which is the longest she has ever stood at that window in four years, and after which she came back to the table and wrote two lines and then did not write anything else for about an hour and a half.

The two lines were this.

*Entered on the office's own account, against no person, on the second of Fallowmonth, YR 312, at the tenth hour, that this office has received a paper from outside this county bearing four lines of a fair copy of a copy of a line in a creditor's register, and a press-mark, and a name; and that the said paper was not produced in this county by any person in this office and was not asked for by any person in this office; and that the conditions on its wrapper are binding on this office and are entered as binds, and that this office is not the office that gave them and cannot waive them.*

*And that this office has entered the fact that a person is a party to nothing in this matter and that this office is a party to nothing in this matter, and that the paper names a person who is not a party to anything and is not to be treated as a party because he is named in a document that this office has received in the course of doing its duty.*

And then she said out loud, to an empty filing room, the sentence that is the whole of her trade and that she has believed since she was twenty-three years old and working in a county office:

"Two copies outside Crown custody and one in a creditor's book. And the fourth place is going to be the hardest one in this county and I have not got a person for it and I am not going to invent one."

---

The copies were made that day and the next and the day after that, and they were made by hand and in the fair hand, and there were three of them and each of them is a different object, which is the point and which Sarn Oyelaran explained to a boy of fourteen who came up the stair on the third with a message and would not go away.

"One is ours. Ours is a *custody* and it is a claim with a number on it and it is a thing a court can order us to produce, and that is the whole point of having it and the whole of the danger of having it.

"One is the toll-holder's. That is a *lending* and it is a chain of eleven sheets in a chest under a counter and the roll says the day and the hour and says the office may produce it and does not claim it, and a thing that is lent can be taken away by the lender and the lender is sixty-one and has four things in his roll that are not tolls.

"And one is the yard's. The yard's is a *fact* and it is a page in a book with a date and an hour and a rule over it and it is not a copy of anything, and if a Crown's man comes up that street in a year and asks for the paper the carpenter will say he has not got it and be right, and if a court asks whether the paper exists the carpenter can say when it was lodged and with whom and at what hour.

"**Three different kinds of holding, because one kind of holding is one kind of failure.** If it had been a copy in three places it would have been three places that could all be lost the same week by three men who all read the same form."

The fourth place was the one she had not got a person for, and she got one on the fourth of Fallowmonth, in a corridor of the county court, from a woman who was sixty-eight and had weighed salt for forty years and whose second floor she rented and who had asked for his second floor back in Longlight and told her three things in the worst order on purpose and had then not asked again.

"The press here is a press," said the salt merchant. "There is a bar on this office and the bar is on the office, not on the room, and there is one thing in this building that is not a record and is not yours, and it is the weighing book, and it is in my hand, and it has been in this room since before you came."

"Then I would like the weighing book to be the fourth place," said Mara Vey, "and I would like the fourth place to be a book about salt."

"It is a book about salt," said the salt merchant. "And I will tell you what it is going to cost, because you will not guess it and I will not have you guessing it in a stair. If there is a copy of that page in this room then in a year a Crown's man comes up these stairs with a form and says *produce the page* — and I have got a lease, and you have got a lease, and a lease is a thing that can be ended, and this is the second floor of a man who weighs salt and there is one other second floor on this street and it is let. **You are going to have to move, or somebody else is going to have to lose a roof.** And I am sixty-eight and I have a wife and I do not want to be the reason a building in this town has a Crown's mark on its door twice."

"Then it goes to your brother," said Mara Vey.

"My brother is in Harvestmonth and has wanted the canal wharf since I was thirty," said the salt merchant, "and will hold a paper for a year for me and for a farthing of salt, and that is a transaction and not a favour and I would like it in your book as a transaction, because a favour is a thing you cannot audit and a transaction is a thing you can."

That is how the fourth place happened, and it is in the day-book as a transaction, and the salt merchant's brother has never in his life been asked what the paper is, and the farthing of salt is entered, and the farthing is in the sum line of a day-book in a filing room over a weigh-house in Merefen and it is the smallest paid item in the whole of this business.

---

**Lysa Fenn** read the four documents together on the sixth of Fallowmonth, in her own room at the county court, at the tenth hour, and there were five people in it, and she had chosen them herself, and the choosing is the chapter.

She had asked for two. The first was Rowan Petch, because a provincial clerk who wrote a particular of her exhibit into a file of her superior court in Greenmonth had a right to hear what his particular had turned into, and he had come down the road on his own time and had paid his own way and had not asked for a fee and had entered that in the margin of a sheet he left on her table, which is what he does.

The second was Captain Rell, and asking him was the most dangerous thing she did in the year.

"I am going to tell Captain Rell in a room," she said, to nobody, on the fifth, "and I have written the sentence out four times and got it wrong four times, and I am going to do it anyway, and the reason is that a document which names the office behind a lawful order and is then kept in secret by three people in a county is a document that only three people are frightened of, and a thing three people are frightened of is a thing that changes the next three decisions. **I would rather there were nine people in this county who are frightened of it, one of whom has to fill in a form in Rainmonth.**"

The room was her own and not the court's, because the court's is a public room and what was in it was a copy of a copy.

On the table, in the order she had put them in, were four documents.

The first was **a page of a provincial ledger.** It was a leaf out of a bound book, and it had been cut out of the file by a clerk and copied by a clerk, and the copy was certified in a provincial clerk's hand, and the line in it said: *Alder Vale. Summons to the road warden of Merefen to appear at the provincial court on the ninth of Greenmonth, YR 312. Absent.* And under the word absent, in a different ink and a smaller hand, somebody had written *no reason given* and drawn a line under it, and had entered the date on which the entry was made, which was the tenth, and had entered that the entry was made of the officer's own motion and not on a fee.

The second was **a finding of that county's own court.** The finding of the fourth of Greenmonth, five clauses and a private minute at the foot, in a magistrate's hand, with a seal on it and a claim number.

The third was **a minute of a bench of a town's guild, of the fifteenth of Longlight**, voted on a Saturday, with the reeve's four lines at the end, and the returned order of the provincial court on it, and on the back of the returned order, in a county clerk's hand, the matter.

And the fourth was a fair copy of a fair copy of a line in a creditor's register four hundred miles off, with a press-mark and a name, and four conditions on the wrapper, and an arrest entered against a stranger on the twenty-second day of the ninth month.

**All four are about a bell. All four are in a county file within a day's ride of a gaol. And not one of the four was written by the person the Crown blames.**

"Read them in that order again," said Lysa Fenn, "and this time do not stop at the end of each one, because the end of each one is the only part anybody has ever read."

They read them again.

And when the last of them was finished, Captain Rell said a thing that nobody in that room had expected, and he said it quietly, to the page on the table and not to anybody in it.

"That is my form," he said. "The ninth of Longlight. The column at the top, for the name of the officer holding the object."

"Yes," said Lysa Fenn.

"That column is mine and it is a county form and I have filled it in four times this year and every one of them went into a file two hundred miles off within a month, and I have never once been asked by anybody what I wrote in it and I have never once been shown a copy of the file." He put his hand flat on the table, the way a man does with a thing he has kept. "And there is a page on this table which says that a warrant in that matter was sealed by an office called the marshal's emergency office, and the office has produced nothing to this court for five months, and the finding of the fourth of Greenmonth asked for it and was not answered. **And I filled in the column.** And I knew in the ninth hour of the ninth of Longlight that no officer of this county held anything, and I wrote a phrase into a space so that the form would be complete, and I did not write a name, and I did not know that the phrase was the name of an office, and a county clerk has since written the same phrase in the same hand on the back of a paper in order to refuse the paper properly."

Nobody in that room said anything for about four seconds.

"Captain Rell," said Mara Vey. "I have to ask you a question as a keeper of records and I am going to ask it in the third form and I am sorry for the third form, and the reason I am sorry for it is that I have no right to be, and I am asking anyway because it is the only question in this business that has an answer that can be checked."

"Go on."

"When you moved the bell on the tenth of Longlight at the fourth hour," she said, "**in what order did you do the three things?** Not why. The order."

Rell was quiet for a while.

"Clock," he said. "Crown's cloak. Chain paper."

"And what was the chain paper."

"It was a sheet with three columns and a column for the hour of each handling, and it wanted the hour and the name and the seal, and there was no column for a reason and there was no column for a place that was not a room in a building with a door." He looked at her. "I have filled in nine hundred of those forms in nineteen years and there has never been anything on them that a man could not read back and understand in a morning, and I have wanted, exactly once, for a form with a column I could not fill in. That is the whole of what I have to say and I would like it in your book in those words, and I would like you to read them back and not improve them, because I know what you do."

"He is right about the form," said Lysa Fenn. "And he is also the officer who executed a seizure on the morning of the nineteenth of Mudmonth off a paper dated the sixteenth, on a bell that four hundred people walked over every fourth day, on a Crown's office that has never produced anything, and I found that seizure lawful on the fourth of Greenmonth and I said so in a public room and I have never once said anything else about it, and I am not going to now."

"No," said Captain Rell. "You have never said anything else about it and I have read the finding and it is the only document in eleven months that anybody in this county has written about me that is not wrong."

The claim was entered on the seventh of Fallowmonth at the eleventh hour, in the filing room, in the fair hand, on the office's own paper, with a claim number and a date and an hour, and it is the eighth claim number this office has entered in four years, and the seventh was entered on her own account against nobody on the sixteenth of Greenmonth.

It was entered **against nobody.**

*In the matter of the crossing of Alder Vale and its dependent places, and of an outgoing writ register kept by the toll-houses of a city in respect of which no court in this county has jurisdiction, and of a page of that register, a copy of a copy of which has been received by this office; and the office has no jurisdiction in the matter and asserts none, and enters this claim on its own account, against no person, with no opinion, for the reason that a fact can be looked for and an argument can be answered and this office has four years of experience of the difference and about eleven days of experience of the other kind.*

*Against no person. And the reason it is against no person is entered, and the reason is that the page names a person, and that a person named in a document is not a party to that document, and that the day a keeper of records enters a name and a claim in the same sentence, the name becomes a party, and a party can be served, and a person who can be served can be made to answer, and **this office has one claimant already in a provincial file who did not know he was in it until a woman told him before anybody else and did not apologise, and this office is not going to do that to a marshal of a Crown two hundred miles away who does not know this office exists.***

Two copies went out of the building on the seventh. One to the toll-holder, in a wrapper with the terms of the lending on the outside in a fair hand, and one to the yard, with the fact and not the paper. The third was the press, and the fourth was a house on a canal wharf in Harvestmonth in a salt merchant's brother's lock-up, entered as a transaction at a farthing of salt.

And the **name went on a separate sheet, with a separate number**, and this is the whole of what Mara Vey did in the last six days of Fallowmonth that she will still be able to defend in eleven years:

*That the keeper of this office has entered in her day-book, and not in this claim, and in her own hand, and with the hour, and against no person and against no office, and on a sheet bearing a separate number from the claim's number and no other mark of identification, a name.*

*Edric Hunn.*

*Entered at the eleventh hour of the seventh of Fallowmonth, YR 312, on the office's own account, on a separate sheet, under a separate number, with no opinion attached to it and none to be attached to it by any court that may read it, and with the reason for the separation entered here and nowhere else:*

*That a claim and a name may be read together by a court that wants to and by nobody else. That a clerk who wants to know whether there is a claim has to go and find the claim. That a clerk who wants to know a name has a document with a number on it and a sheet with a different number on it, and a rule about which is which, and a keeper's signature on both. And that a man whose name is entered in a public record in this county in YR 312 is a man about whom anybody in this county may read a fact and ask a question, and is not a man who has been charged with anything, and cannot be, by anybody, ever, from this office, whatever anybody tells them this office has done.*

*And that this is the only name this office has entered in four years that is not a name of a person who came up a stair and asked.*

And on the same day, in the same room, at the third hour of the afternoon, she wrote the line about the weights, and it is the second of two lines that are read together or not at all.

*That the claim entered this day cites the brine weights of Ferris Oat of Saltbrim, being thirty-one days of salt weights with the weighmaster's arithmetic intact, as an authority upon the quantity of provisions carried into the sealed limit and the use made of them; and that the said weights are held by this office and are cited and are not filed, and are not entered as an exhibit, and are not a part of this claim, and remain in the wrapper in the counter drawer in which they have been since the sixth of Mudmonth.*

*And the reason, entered here and nowhere else: that a roll of the dead built out of that ledger is the whole of what the case is for and it is the only instrument the people of that city are owed, and it belongs to the city, and the city keeps its own dead in its own order in its own hands, and has twice refused this office a copy of a book of its own and has been right both times. And that the first of those two refusals was given to this office by a man with a counting house and a bundle of string, on the fourth day of a month in the year before last, in his own voice, and the second was given by a woman with a crossing book on a channel in this world who would not let it be copied and said why in trade's words, and the reason she gave was that a copy is a second thing that somebody can lose.*

*And that on the seventh of Fallowmonth, YR 312, at the third hour of the afternoon, this office had a claim in front of it which would have been stronger by a factor of nothing that can be measured with the weights in the other hand, and this office did not file them, and the reason is entered here, and the reason is that a keeper who has claimed the dead cannot afterwards stand in a hall in that city and be a person with no opinion.*

She read that back once and did not read it back twice, and put the hour on it, and it went in the day-book and not in the press, beside the line of the second of Fallowmonth, and the two lines are four lines apart and they are the first four lines in four years of a book that a person in another world is going to read in about eighty years, and they will be read together, because the second one is meaningless without the first, and Mara Vey knew that when she wrote them and has never said so out loud.

**The villages** were served on the eighth of Fallowmonth by the county's own process, in the county's spelling, with each figure separately, and `sum not stated` in the total line, and a space left where an eighth figure would go.

It went out on a sheet with a claim number on it because a claim cannot support a schedule that is not part of the claim, and the schedule had seven lines filled and eight ruled, and the clerk who ruled it was a county clerk in the county town who did not know what the eighth line was for and asked, and was told, in writing, with the hour, by the keeper of the Bellweather Claims Archive, who is not a county officer and has no jurisdiction in anything and has just been made, in effect, the county's correspondent in a matter nobody in the county will hold.

Alder Cross, two hundred. Saltbrim, ninety. Wrenlow, sixty. Cobbett, a hundred and forty. Nine Elms, a hundred and ten. Stonebarrow, forty. Hushwater, eighty.

*Sum not stated.*

And the eighth line, ruled, empty, with the rule still on it, and the reason entered under the total: *that the form of the Crown's finding of the second of Greenmonth is ruled for eight places and this schedule is ruled to the form, and that the eighth place in the Crown's finding is the city, and that the city is not entered on this schedule by this office, and that the figure for the city is the figure of the Crown and remains the figure of the Crown, and that this office has in four years never entered it and does not enter it now.*

Nobody in that room asked her why.

That is the part she has thought about since. Four people were in the room — the magistrate's clerk, Sarn Oyelaran, a boy from the county office, and the salt merchant's wife, who had brought a chair up two flights because she is sixty-eight and had wanted to see it — and not one of them asked her why, and every one of them wrote the reason down as she gave it and read it back, and one of them, the boy, who is nineteen and is the son of the county clerk, looked at the ruled eighth line for a while and then said: "It'll still be on the sheet in a year, will it."

"Yes," said Mara Vey.

"Huh," said the boy, and copied the reason, and initialled it.

---

**The instrument** was entered on the ninth of Fallowmonth at the tenth hour, in the county court, by Lysa Fenn, and it took nine minutes and it is four lines and it is on the record of that court for ever.

*That the letter of the twentieth of Greenmonth, YR 312, addressed to the holder of the tolls of the Old Toll crossing, suspending the tolls of that crossing from the first of Longlight and directing the holder to prevent passage, entertainment and correspondence concerning the crossing, was produced; and that the instruction of the ninth of Longlight, YR 312, second hour, received by the watch of this county on the tenth and carried out on the same day, directing the watch to prevent contact with the object and to prevent the assembly of any body of this county in respect of the object, was produced.*

*And that this court finds both instruments lawful, and has no power to make either otherwise, and has no jurisdiction to do so, and that the office which sealed the first and the office which issued the second have produced nothing to this court on being asked, and the asking is dated.*

*And that this court has nothing further to say about either instrument at any time, and that neither instrument is mentioned again in this court, and that this minute is the last entry concerning them.*

"That last clause was mine and it is not law and I would like somebody to tell me what it is for," said the county's own clerk, afterwards, in the corridor.

"It is for the next woman who sits here in nine years," said Lysa Fenn. "She will open this file in about the fourth year of a reign nobody in this county can name and she will find two instruments that were lawful, and the file will go on saying so for a hundred years, and what she will be looking for is a date. **A lawful instrument with a date on it can be looked at. A lawful instrument with a date on it and a court that has said it has nothing further to say about it is a document a person can be held to in a room, and that is the whole of what a county court is for, and it is not a speech and it is not a finding and it is nine minutes long.**"

And that is the last of the Crown's instruments in the county file, and there is nothing after it about either of them, and the file has been read eleven times in the twenty years since and every single reading has stopped at the same four lines and gone on to the next page, which is a page of a bench's roll, and which is about a bell-shaped hole in a parapet, and which somebody in a room four hundred yards from that parapet wrote on a Sunday in Fallowmonth of a year that did not matter.

And on the ninth of Fallowmonth, at the fourth hour of the afternoon, a woman in a filing room over a salt merchant read a form through twice and then filled it in, and the form was going to the county's own council, and it had been sent to her because the council has to consider whether a body of a town's making is a body corporate, and the question had been raised by a writ of enquiry into a record office, and there is a printed space on that form, ruled, in the middle, and the column over it is headed —

*Function of the office.*

And Mara Vey wrote into it the words of the instrument of foundation of the founding of this town, as they stand at the head of her own book of instruments, in the founding's own order and in the founding's own hand, which are four sentences and about sixty words and which say what the office is for and do not say what it is called.

And she left the space above the ruled line empty, and initialed the bottom, and sent it to the county town on the county's post with a witness, and did not put anything in a press.

It is the only thing she has ever sent out of that building that was not a copy of something, and there is a printed space in it that is still empty, and it went to a council of men who govern a town of a thousand people, and they are going to sit on it in Fallowmonth of a year nobody now in Merefen will see, and one of them is going to read four sentences about the restoration of names and last known wishes, and one of them is going to ask what the office is called, and the form will not tell him, because a form does not have a space for what a thing is called, it has a space for what a thing is *for*, and she filled that one in, and that is the only space on it, and it was enough.
