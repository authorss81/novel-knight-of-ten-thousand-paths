# Chapter 0027

On the fifteenth day of his being in Bellweather-Living, YR 312, the bench of the Bellweather Bridge Guild sat with the shutters up.

It had never done that in the eleven years of Aren's absence that there had been. The hall sits over the arch and the arch runs under the floor of it, and a hall with the shutters up is a hall that can be seen from the deck, and a hall that can be seen from the deck is a hall that anybody in the city who wanted to stand on the crown of the crossing and look at the river could walk into, and about two hundred of them did, in ones and twos, over two hours, and stood at the back, and were not asked to leave.

The bench had decided to do it because a name had been read out in that room five days ago and the city had gone on for five days with the whole of it in everybody's mouth and nobody to say it in a room, and the man with the mallet said the thing that decided it, which was that a thing a city has to say in a market ends up as a market, and if it is going to be said in a market it should at least be said once in a hall with a clerk in it first.

So the shutters went up, and the hall filled, and the bench sat, and Aren Kest stood at the end of the bench-clerk's table with a notebook he did not open, and for three hours he did not make a single speech, and that was the decision of the day, and it took him until the second hour to understand that it was a decision.

He had come back to this city with a case.

---

The first business was the bench's own account, and the bench read it out, and it was eleven lines long, and it was not his account, and it was not worse than his.

It was in the guild's hand because the guild's hand was the only one that had a bench behind it. It said: that a writ had come to the north gate on the evening of the fourteenth of Mudmonth, YR 301, from a Crown's emergency office, sealed; that it was read aloud in this room by a man who was not a guild man and who was told to read it and to say nothing else; that it required the severance of this crossing and of all places dependent on it and the removal of the persons found within the sealed limit and the handing of the arch to officers of that office; that it gave five days, counted from the day before it was read; that the bench refused it on the deck of the arch on the nineteenth, at the second hour of the night, and refused it with the third bell, rung once and long, at the crown, by a boy of seventeen who had a bar in his hand and was told he had a full minute; that the tender came back to the gate at noon on the twentieth and found two hundred people on the deck with the bar stock, and nobody had ordered those two hundred, and its captain was an old man in a Crown cloak with a written duty and no instruction to use it, and he sat down on the toll stone and held there until somebody with more than himself told him to begin; and that on the twenty-second a second boat came up with a written recall in it and the crew went down the river and nobody on the deck was hurt.

That was the whole of it. Eleven lines and a date at the foot and the reading of it by the man with the mallet in a hall with the shutters up and two hundred people at the back.

"Now you," said the bench-clerk.

"I am not going to say our night," said Aren Kest. "You have just read it, and it is a better account of that night than anything I have ever said about it in any room, and I would like to say that once and then never again, because I have been in this hall twice now and I have twice been a man who came in with a story, and both times the story was about me and neither time it was about you."

The hall was very quiet.

"I have a document," he said. "I told you on the ninth that I had and I have. It is a sworn account in a magistrate's court in a county of a country four days off, entered on the thirtieth of Mudmonth, YR 312, six weeks ago, with a claim number, in a chain of custody with three hands on it. I have no copy of it; I gave the only thing I carried out of this city to that court with no condition and kept nothing. What I have is the fact of it, and a date, and the fact that a magistrate of that court has since found that the instrument which sealed a Crown order on a bridge in my town is lawful, and that the office that sealed it has produced nothing to her, and that there is no office of keeper of the Bellweather hinge in that county's charter, in the county's list of appointments, or in the Crown's own schedule of provincial offices, and that two papers before her describe a person as holding it.

"That is all I have got, and I would like to be extremely clear that it is not more than it sounds. It cannot name anybody. It cannot name a bell. It cannot name this city. It says a thing about a paper and it says the hole behind the paper, and the hole is dated, and I cannot carry the hole into a court and win anything with it inside four years. I have been told so by a woman who is better at this than I am and I have believed her."

"And the line in Merrow's book," said a voice from the back of the hall.

Aren Kest turned round.

The man who had said it was standing among the ferrymen at the back, about fifty, in a good coat with a ledger under his arm, and he had not been in the room when the shutters went up and had come in late, and he was the only person in the hall that Aren Kest had not met before.

"I am Hollis Dree," he said. "I keep the toll rolls of the north and south houses and I keep the city's own books, which are not the same books, and the difference between those two things is my whole employment and it is a thing the Crown's office in your country would find interesting or incomprehensible according to which of its clerks you talk to." He came up the side of the hall. "You said you have a document. I would like to say that I am not here about your document, and then I am going to be here about your document, and I would like it noted that I told you first."

"Go on," said the man with the mallet.

"Master Kest has spent fourteen days in this city behaving like a man bringing a case," said Hollis Dree, "and the bench has let him, and the toll houses have let him, and I have let him, and every one of us has let him because a man bringing a case is a man we can all be sensible with, and I have been sensible with him for fourteen days and I have come to tell the room that I am not going to be sensible with him in front of two hundred people, because they have come to hear a thing and I am not going to let them go home having heard a thing I did not say.

"You want us restored. I am going to say that word out loud in this hall because nobody else in it will, and then I am going to say what it means, and then I am going to say no.

"You have in your country a Crown, and a finding in it that says this valley was not built and is not maintained and that four thousand people are dead of a fever, and a town that has been told the law was on its side, and an office two floors above a salt merchant that keeps one set of records outside that Crown's custody so that the Crown cannot own the only copy. I know all of that, because you have said all of it in this hall, in order, twice, in fourteen days, and I have written it down, because I am a clerk and that is what I do with a thing that is said in a room.

"And here is the thing your office would say if it were here, and I have read what your office says on a wall, because you quoted it and I asked a man to write it out and I have it in a book in a room in this city. Your office keeps one set of records outside the Crown's custody. *That is the whole of your institution, and it is a magnificent thing, and it is an argument about custody, and it is why you have spent a year of your life with a press with a bar on it and a rule in your own hand and no money at all — and it tells you exactly what a restoration is.*

"A restoration is not a repair. A repair is done by the people who own the thing. A restoration is done *to* a thing, by the person who pays, and the person who pays owns it afterwards, and there is no clause in the world that says otherwise, and there is not one clause in your finding that says otherwise either, because your finding is a finding about a fever and a bridge, and a bell put back on a bridge in your country is a bell on a bridge in your country. It is municipal iron on a municipal crossing, and we have never asked for it, and it is not a small thing, and it is not about us.

"And then it is eleven years. And in year twelve, some man in your province who has never heard of this hall sends a clerk, and the clerk writes down that a bell stands on a crossing in a town called Merefen, and what it means, and the clerk is not a cruel man and the clerk is doing his duty, and in year twenty a thing that was in a fair copy on a wall is a precedent, and in year forty it is the law, and by then the Crown does not have to own us. The Crown owns the fact about us, which is worse, because a fact is harder to take off a man than a bridge."

Somebody at the back said something. It was a woman on a step near the door, and she did not take her hood off, and there was a woman on Ferry Lane whose shutters had been shut since the thaw and had not been opened since, and everybody in this city had known for eleven years not to knock.

"Speak to the man," said the woman.

"I am speaking to the man," said Hollis Dree, and he did not turn round.

"No. You are speaking to a room. There is a difference and you know the difference, because you have spent your life in it." She had not moved and her hood was still up. "You have told him that a Crown restoration is a Crown ownership, and that is true, and you have not told him that a refusal is also a kind of ownership, and that whatever is in your books about us is in a city that has made its own arrangements for forty years, and that the man would carry it to a Crown, and that the Crown would then have a book, and that the book would be a *number*."

The hall was very quiet, and Hollis Dree took about four seconds, and the four seconds were the longest anybody in the room had seen him spend on anything.

"Madam," he said, "that is a good argument and it is not mine, and I have had it since the sixth day and I have not used it, and I want to say why, because if I do not say why then this room will have heard a keeper of books say that he was frightened of a keeper of books, and that is a small thing and it is a thing this city does not do."

---

"It is not about fear," said Dree. "It is about a copy.

"Every man in this hall has heard the phrase *restored* and has gone straight past it, because we are all of us tired, and I have not gone past it, because I am a clerk and I have spent fourteen days writing down what this man has said in a country four days off, and one of the things he said on the first day is a name, and I have looked it up, and it is the Bellweather Claims Archive, at Merefen, in a fen county, and it is a claims office, and it keeps copies outside the Crown's custody.

"So. There is a book in your country called the Bellweather Claims Archive, and it is full of copies of things, and it exists in a city of about four thousand people, and it is kept by a woman I have never met, and by law it keeps one set of records outside your Crown's custody.

"Here is my sentence, and I would like it taken down, because I have never said it in a room and I have been a keeper of books for nineteen years.

"*A copy is a claim.* That is what a copy is. When you take a thing that is here and put it there, you have not made a second copy of a thing, you have made a claim on the only one there is. Every copy in every office in every kingdom is a claim on the original, and the only difference between a good office and a bad one is whether the office admits it. Yours admits it. Yours has a rule on a wall that says so, in the keeper's own hand, and the rule is *an anchor is recorded on the word of a sworn tradesman, in his own hand, and not on a clerk's word* — and Master Kest, you are the only man in my world who has ever been believed in a court on the strength of a hand.

"You are not a clerk and you are not a soldier and you are not a governor and you are not anything I would put my name under, and I would not be in a room with most of the Crown's officers in this kingdom, and I would not be in a room with the Crown's officers in yours. *But if you carry a copy of anything out of this city, then a court four days off, in a country with a finding in it that says this valley was never there, has a piece of this city in a drawer, and it is mine, and it is yours, and there is a very great deal of that city that I would not want a court to have at a moment when your Crown is in the mood it is in.*

"And I want to be exact about what I am saying, because I am a clerk and if I say a thing wrongly it becomes a thing people repeat. I am not saying no. I have never said no to you and I am not going to, and the city has no way of saying no to you, and I have a bench here that cannot order me to say no to a man with a knife, let alone a man with a road. I am saying that this office gives out nothing. Not a register, not a roll, not a bound of the great arch, not a page of the crossing books. Nothing leaves this city in my hand and nothing has left it in nineteen years, and the reason is not the Crown and it is not your man and it is not a wound eleven years old.

"The reason is this. Every book in that room upstairs is a thing somebody here kept because there was nobody to keep it for. When the fever came we buried two hundred and eleven of the low quarter ourselves in Mudmonth, two years ago, and there was no Crown office and no northern clerk and no money, and a woman kept the ledger in a hand other people could check, and that ledger is the only account of those people that exists in the world. The north prints a different number, and the north's number is a number for a crossing, and it is not this, and I have watched three clerks in this city be asked to give it up and refuse, and I have never once thought less of them for it.

"*We are not waiting to be believed. We are keeping what we have because being believed is not a thing that lasts and keeping is.* That is the whole of my office and I have said it in a hall with the shutters up because there were two hundred people at the back and I have not been able to work out in fourteen days whether I wanted to say it to them or to you, and it turns out I wanted to say it to both and I have wasted the fourteen days, and I would like to be given a job."

Nobody spoke for a moment.

Then the man with the mallet said: "That is the archivist. That is what he is for."

"I am the toll-clerk of the north and south houses," said Hollis Dree, "and I keep the city's books, and I have just said in a public hall that the most useful thing in this city is a set of books that nobody outside it will ever be permitted to see, and I would like a bench order that says so, in a book, with a date, and I would like it signed by nine men who mean it, because in about nine years there will be somebody in this city who says we have kept everything out of spite, and I would like the date on it to be a fortnight earlier than him."

---

The room broke up at the twelfth hour, and Aren Kest was in the middle of the floor with eleven different people trying to say something to him at once, which is a thing that has happened to him four times in eleven years and every one of the four times he was being asked to be responsible for something.

He did the thing he had decided to do, which was to stand still and let it happen, and then he said, to nobody in particular:

"I have been in this room three times and each time I have come with a case. I would like to say the thing I have not said, and then I want to stop saying anything for a while.

"I believe you. That is the honest shape of it. I have read a Crown finding and a Crown order and a Crown hearing, and I have read a line in a creditor's book, and I believe this bench and the woman with the crossing book and the counting house and the two hundred and eleven in a ledger, and I do not believe the finding, and it is not close, and it has not been close since the sixth day I stood on the north steps.

"And it does not entitle me to a single thing. Not a page, not a name, not a copy, not a restoration, not a market, not a bell. I said in Mudmonth that I would not put this city in a document in the north without the bench's word. I said it before I had read one line in the counting house. I would like it noted that I said it before, because I know exactly what a room like this one does with a man who makes a speech after he has been shown something, and I have watched a Crown do it to a city and I would not have anybody here think I have learned how to do it in nine days."

The man with the mallet had his hands flat on the stone in the floor of the room, on the crown of the third arch.

"So the bench will tell you what the bench has," he said, "and I would like it understood that this is not a gift, because if it is a gift then in nine years somebody will say the city was bought, and the answer to that is that nothing in this room can be bought and nothing in this room is anybody's to give, and what I am about to put on the table is a piece of work.

"You want a road kept visible. Fine. A road is a road and a road is a thing that can be *seen*, and the reason we have said for eleven years that a stranger with a strange story is a nuisance is not the story. It is that nobody here can tell from one morning to the next whether there is a road or whether there was a man once. We are a city that is easy to reach and impossible to rely on, and every one of us has spent eleven years being careful about that.

"So the bench cannot give you a road. The bench can give you a person.

"There is a woman in this city who maintains the ferry at Hushwater Reach and the approaches to it, and her name is Sera Quen, and she has been a ferrywright since she was fourteen and she has been in this hall more times than I have, and she is on this bench's books as a steward, which is a word that means she keeps a thing and is answerable to the people who use it, and there is no other word for her in this room and there is not going to be one. She has agreed to hear you and she has set her own terms and I am going to read them out because she wrote them and I am not going to improve on her in a hall with the shutters up.

"First. It is about the road and not about the city. A person on the far side may come; a person on the far side may not ask this city for anything, and if he asks, she says so, and it is entered here and it is not a discourtesy.

"Second. Whatever passes is visible. A date, an hour, a place, and the name of a person on this side who is standing there, in a book kept in this hall. She is the person who stands there. Not you. You cannot be the person who stands at this end of a road, because you are not a person who lives here, and a road that a man opens on his own account and closes on his own account is a man's door and not a road, and this city has had a man's door in it for eleven years and it is called a Crown's finding.

"Third. It is hers and not the bench's. The bench cannot stop it and the bench will not pretend it can. If she says no in Longlight then it is no in Longlight and this room will not argue with her, and if she says no and one of us nine disagrees, then the one who disagrees is the wrong one.

"Fourth, and she was emphatic about this and I am reading it as she gave it to me: any paper that comes out of the north addressed to this city comes to her hands and is answered by her, or not at all. Not to a bench, not to a house, not to a stranger who is passing through. To the person who keeps the road.

"And fifth," said the man with the mallet, "and this one is hers and not ours, and I am only carrying it, and I would like it heard in her words if she is in the room —"

"I am in the room," said a woman at the side, with a lamp in her hand, and she was younger than he had expected and there was iron dust in the creases of her hands and she had not been at the front.

"The fifth is that the fourth camp on the north bank keeps its name on the gate bridge approach for as long as there is a camp," she said, "and that if a man comes out of a road and goes into a camp and is a stranger for a fortnight, he does not get to decide what the camp is for, and the camp is for a road surface and not for a road, and I have not got the men for a road."

"I have not asked the camp for anything," said Aren Kest.

"I know you have not," said the ferrywright. "That is the fifth term and you are the first man to hear it and not ask, and I would like it entered that I noticed, and I would like it entered *now*, and not on the morning you go."

---

He got out of the hall at the twelfth hour and down the stair and out onto the crown of the arch, where the winch on the gallery was up and the wind was doing nothing at all, and he got about thirty feet and a man came out after him and matched his pace exactly, which is a thing a man does who has poled a flat-bottomed hull for a working life.

"You are the stranger who came up the north steps with a bar and no farthing," said the man.

"Yes."

"My name is Ruen and I run barges on the far channel, and I am fifty-four, and I have a boat with a split garboard I would like you to look at in the morning, and I would like to pay you for the day, and that is not a favour and it is not a courtesy, it is a day." He kept walking. "And I would like to say the other thing now while we are both walking and not looking at each other, because I have thought about it for three days and I am not going to say it in a room again.

"I was on that deck. On the nineteenth of Mudmonth, YR 301. I was forty-three and I had a bar and a boat and no business on a bridge, and I came up because a hundred and forty other men came up, and there is no order in this world that says I did, and I have heard the story about two hundred people so many times that I have begun to hear it in my sleep.

"And I was very good to you, and I want to say so out loud, because nobody in this city has said it to you and it is a debt and you should know you owe it. You came off a road in Mudmonth and you went and put a hull right for six days, and you did it the hard way, and my boy has the garboard and the shear pin in it and it has been two years, and it has not opened, and I have watched that pin for two years and I have said nothing to a single person about it, and if you ever go back to your country I would like you to know that a man in this city has been saying nothing for two years on purpose."

"I am grateful," said Aren Kest.

"I know you are," said Captain Ruen, "and that is the part I have been thinking about for three days, and here it is, and I am going to ask it properly and I would like you to hear it properly and not to be kind about it.

"I have got a daughter," he said, "who was four in YR 301 and is fifteen now, and she knows there is a road in the world, because everybody in this city knows there is a road, because a man came out of one and mended a boat, and she has asked me every year since what is on the other end of it, and I have said: nothing, there is nothing, it is a story, and she has taken her own opinion about that over ten years and she has taken the sensible one.

"And now there is a name in a book in the counting house that a Crown's office put there, and a toll-house man read it out in that hall eight days ago, and half the north road had it before the second hour," and the toll houses have been arguing about a season for a week, and there is a man building a fourth camp on the north bank who came out of a road in a city that has a *finding* in it, and my daughter is fifteen and she has worked all that out and she came to me on the ninth day and asked me the question, and I could not answer it.

"So here is the question, and I am putting it to you the way I would put it to a man I have paid to mend my boat, and I want it answered as a tradesman, and I want it answered in the open," because I have been in two rooms this week where a thing got said in a corner and came out of the corner four days later with a different shape.

"*The man building the fourth camp on the north bank, this month, is he the same man who was on the deck of the arch on the nineteenth of Mudmonth, YR 301?*"

They had stopped walking. The arch was under them and the water was going under it at the rate it went under it on an ordinary night, and the sheets had gone over at the sixth hour and the winch was up and the market under the second arch was shutting.

"Because if he is, then my daughter has a road," said Captain Ruen, "and that is a thing a child of fifteen can get half hold of, and it is going to get hold of her, and I have had three days to work out what I would say to her and I have not got a sentence, and the only reason I have not got a sentence is that I have been waiting to ask a man who was there.

"And if he is not," said Captain Ruen, "then somebody in this city has been trading on a lie for eleven years and it is not me and it is not the guild and it is not the toll houses, and I would like to know who it is before she is sixteen."

He did not raise his voice, and he had a lamp in his hand, and he held it down.

"I have got a question," said Aren Kest.

"I know you have. Everybody in this hall has had one since the hall eight days ago and I have heard four of them, and none of them were that one, and I have been in this city for thirty years and I have never once been unable to tell the difference between a man with a question and a man with a speech, and you have not had a speech. You have had a silence for fourteen days, and I have watched a silence for fourteen days from a hundred yards off, and I would like to know what is in it before she is sixteen."

And Aren Kest stood on the crown of the great arch of Bellweather with an iron-grey man of fifty-four in a good coat and a lamp, and worked out that he had about four seconds, and that he had come back across a road to be believed, and had found a bench that wanted a witness and a counting house that wanted a figure and an archivist who had said the sentence that turned the whole book over, and a ferrywright who had put a name on a camp and a road, and a girl of fifteen in a city of about four thousand people whose father was asking him, correctly, the only question in the world that mattered.

And that the answer was one he had given in a written account in a magistrate's court in a country four days off on the thirtieth of Mudmonth, and had given to a woman in a filing room and to a brother in a yard, and had not given to a single human being in eleven years.

"Yes," said Aren Kest.

"Then say the rest of it," said Captain Ruen, "and say it now, and I will take you to the hall and you can say it in there tomorrow if you like. But say it to me tonight, because I have to go home and I have to have something to say to a fourteen-year-old and I am not going to make one up."

The lamp went along the deck, and the two of them stood under an arch in a city that was going to bed, and Aren Kest opened his mouth to say the true thing first, which was the ordinary true thing, which was that a man is a man and a road is a road and no one man could be both.
