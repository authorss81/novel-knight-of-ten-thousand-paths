# Chapter 0036

He went up the Old Toll road first, on the tenth of Longlight, at the fourth hour, because the wharf road was shut and a man goes where the thing is.

The river had come down another four fingers in a fortnight and the Old Toll crossing stood a foot and a half higher out of the water than it had in Mudmonth, and the whole of it was legible in about four seconds if a man knew what he was looking at: two courses of the cutwater on the upstream nose had come out of the summer's low water and were dry and grey and had a green line along the top of them; the moss on the parapet coping had come back over the swept rectangle in the bed of the stone and had taken the two corners and left the middle, so that the shape of what had been lifted off it four hundred yards below the weir was still there in a slightly different green; and the iron cradle was gone entirely. Not rusted. Gone — pried out of the bed in three pieces on the morning of the nineteenth of Mudmonth and taken away in a cart, and what was left was four square holes in the stone with moss in them and no iron in any of them, which is the difference between an object being removed and an object being removed and the site being described.

The post was grey. The wax had not run. It had been put on in Mudmonth in a cold wet month on cold wet stone, and wax laid in those conditions does not slump, it goes hard and grey and stays, and it will not run again until a hot dry month comes down on it, and there has not been one, and there is not going to be one before Fallowmonth. He put a thumbnail on the edge of it and it did not move and it did not mark.

The seat in the fourth course was under the water line at that hour and he got down to it off the third step with a hand on the setts, and the two discs were in it, and the pin, and the oak collar with three blows in the head of it, and the maker's mark under the near disc where a maker's mark goes, and the stone around it was dry and clean and the colour of the inside of a shell.

He put his left hand flat on it.

Cold. Water. Stone. Nothing at all, no more than on the seventh of Greenmonth and on the nineteenth of Mudmonth and on every morning since, and the river went over the fourth course and went on, and he took his hand off it and dried it on his coat and wrote nothing anywhere, because there was nothing to write.

Then he went down the road to the watch house, and the thing that had happened in the four minutes at the sill was this: he had gone up a road he was instructed not to have contact with, and had touched a thing that is the matter, and had been in a county where a Crown's clerk in a provincial office was writing memoranda about a bell, and the whole of it had cost him four minutes and had produced exactly nothing, and there was no one in the world he could tell that to.

---

The watch house on Weir Street had a door that was painted the watch's colour and a bench outside it and a board, and the board was not a market board and it had a Crown's frame round it, and on the board on the morning of the tenth of Longlight there was a notice dated the eighth and nailed up by the watch captain himself on the ninth at the second hour, with four nails and a seal, and it was the second time in eleven weeks that anybody in Merefen had put a Crown seal on a thing in a public place, and the first had been the grey wax on a post four hundred yards up the Old Toll road.

**The watch of Merefen is commended.** By the marshal's office, the eighth of Longlight, YR 312, for the execution of a lawful instruction in the matter of an object, at first light on the nineteenth of Mudmonth, in a county in which the object had been held in use among a company of persons.

And at the foot of it, in the same wax, the same device: a hand laid flat on a book.

Aren Kest read it twice on the outside of a watch house at the fifth hour of the morning and worked out, standing up, in about nine seconds, that the object had been described to two hundred miles of Crown paper as being in use among a company of persons, and that the bell of a bridge that four hundred people crossed every fourth day had been written down in a provincial building as a thing in the middle of people, and that this was the second time a Crown instrument in this county had used the same phrase about the same object in about two months.

*Held in use among a company of persons.* That is the phrase in the seizure order. That is what has been on the storehouse door since the nineteenth of Mudmonth. And the bell was on a cradle on a bridge abutment and four hundred people crossed that bridge over it and no one had ever in nineteen years given it a thought, and somebody in a provincial office has read *held in use among a company of persons* and understood it to mean *in the middle of people*, and has built a commendation on it, and the commendation is nailed to the outside of a watch house with four nails and a seal.

And the same office has spent eleven weeks writing to the road-keeper of Merefen in a drawer in a toll-house with three *noes* in chalk on the outside of it, and has instructed a magistrate's court by a returnable order, and has put a writ of enquiry on a record office, and has gone past all four of those in four days to nail up a sheet of paper commending a watch for having done exactly what it was told in a matter nobody had to be told about.

**It has found the easy half.** It has found an officer with a lawful custody and a documented performance and no questions of his own to answer, and it has put a seal on him in a public place, and it has made him the Crown's man in this county, and it has done it for nothing, and it took eleven weeks and it worked.

He was still standing in front of it when the door opened.

---

Rell had not sent for him. Rell had come out onto the step at the fifth hour of the morning, in his coat, in the rain, and looked at the board, and then looked at a man reading it, and then said, without any preamble at all:

"You have read it."

"Captain."

"Yes." Rell came down off the step and shut the door behind him, which shut the board out, which was not a thing a watch captain does on a wet morning outside his own watch house. "I have had it since the sixth hour of the eighth and I nailed it up myself at the second hour of the ninth because I have had a week to think about where it should go and I have decided that the answer is *here*, in the open, with a nail in each corner, and that it is the only notice I have ever put on this wall in nineteen years and I put it up myself.

"I am going to be asked why I nailed up a commendation in a public place, and I have a week to write the answer and I have written it and I am going to say it to you now, in the street, and you are the first man I am going to say it to, and I would have said it to a magistrate if a magistrate had come up this street in the last week.

"I have been relieved of a sergeant and of nine months and three weeks' accrual. The sergeant made a statement at his own table on the night of the second of Greenmonth and brought it in on the fourth and I took his stripes at the second hour of the fifth in a corridor and stopped his pay as a man at all until it is disposed of, and he has a wife and a boy of nine, and I have not had a promotion out of it and there is not going to be one, and that was said to me by the county's own clerk's office in writing and I have got the writing. I have not been thanked. I have not been advanced. I have been correct and I have paid for it and I have gone on doing the work of a captain for five weeks on a captain's pay with a hole in it.

"And on the eighth of Longlight an office that has never once produced an authority in this county sent me four nails and a piece of paper saying that the watch of Merefen is commended, and it is nailed up outside my own door, and I have thought about it for two days and I have arrived at the only conclusion available to a man in my position, which is that this is not an honour. **This is a man being picked.** And I have been picked, and there is one Crown's man in this county and I am him, and it did not take a bribe or a threat or a conversation, it took a bell and eight days."

"You could take it down," said Aren Kest.

"I could and I will not, and I want you to understand the shape of why, because you are the only man in this town who will do anything with the shape of a thing. If I take that paper down, then I am a watch captain who nailed up an honour and took it down, and that is a story, and a story is a thing a person can be got to change. If I leave it up, then it is a fact, with a date on it and four nails in it, and the whole of what it is doing in this town is being done to a piece of paper and not to a man. A fact with a date on it can be looked at. I have read that out of a drawer in a toll-house this month. I would not be surprised if somebody in this county has written it down."

Then he took a folded paper out of inside his coat and he did not unfold it, and he kept it folded, and Aren Kest understood that a man was about to be told something and wanted to have asked a question first.

"I have had this since the second hour this morning," said Rell. "It is not the same paper as the other one. The other one was sealed and came by a Crown's courier out of the marshal's office and I was told to keep it shut, and this one is sealed and came by the county's own man on a horse from the county town with a watchman's hand on the wrapper, because the county does not trust its own bag with a Crown's wax and has not since I came, and it is from the same office, because the wax is the same wax, and I have now seen that device three times in this town in eleven weeks and I have got it in a book of my own at home with the three dates on the same page.

"You are going to want to read it, and I am not going to let you, and I am not going to tell you what is in it, and I am going to tell you the reason for both of those and then I am going to tell you what I have done with it, and I would rather tell you on this step than in my office because in my office it is a thing a watch captain has done and on this step it is a man standing in the rain next to another man who has come to him on his own."

"Rell."

"Captain Rell, and you know it."

"Captain Rell," said Aren Kest. "Then say it."

Rell unfolded it, read four lines of it, and said the four lines, in order, without lowering the paper, because a man who reads a paper he has been given and has read before is not the same as a man quoting something from memory, and both of them understood that and neither of them said so.

"It is dated the ninth of Longlight, which is yesterday, and it is stamped at the second hour, which is the same hour a writ of enquiry was stamped yesterday morning in a room over a salt merchant, and I do not know that and I am telling you that I do not know it, and you may go and find out and I will not have said otherwise.

"First: the object is held under the seal of this office, in the custody of the watch of Merefen, in a storehouse by the fish weir, and this office is satisfied that the custody has been lawful and is content that it continue so.

"Second: the watch of Merefen shall prevent contact between the said object and any person, and shall prevent any person from approaching, examining, or speaking with any person concerning the said object, and shall use its office to that end.

"Third." He stopped for a second. "The watch of Merefen shall prevent the assembly of any body of this county in respect of the said object, and shall report to this office as it is required."

Nobody said anything for a moment. It was raining on the step of a watch house in Weir Street and the water was going along the channel under the boards.

"It is not a threat," said Rell. "I want that understood before anything else, because you have been waiting eleven weeks for a threat and there is not one in it and there is not going to be one, and I have read it four times looking for a threat and there is none. It is an instruction. It does not name a law. **It does not require a court.** It does not name a law and it does not require a court and it has been executed in this town in one day out of a letterbox, and there is no officer of the Crown in this county who could be asked whether it is lawful, including me, and I have been a watch captain in this town for nineteen years and I have never been frightened of a paper and I am now, and I would like that entered anywhere you put it.

"And the town is not required to be told. That is the fourth part and it is the part I have read eleven times. The town is not required to be told. So a bench of nine men in the Merefen bridge guild will sit in their own guild house at the ninth hour on a Friday in Frostmonth, and they will talk about the trust of the Old Toll crossing, which is the oldest thing in this town, and a watch captain in this county will be the officer of this county who prevents it. And I have known since yesterday morning what that is and I have not told them, and I am telling you instead of them, and you will understand why in about a minute, and it is not because you are the road warden. It is because if I tell them they will come here and I will have to refuse them at a door in public, and if you tell them they will do it in a market, and neither of those is a thing a man arranges on purpose, and both of them happen anyway."

---

"I have carried it out," said Rell. "I have carried it out this morning at the fifth hour, and I want to tell you how, and I have had two hours to decide how, and I have not slept, and I did not sleep last night either and that is a different matter, and I have decided to do it the way you would do it.

"First: the seal on the storehouse door stays and does not get renewed, because renewing a seal on a storehouse door is a thing a frightened man does and I am not going to do it. Second: the storehouse door stays open in the day, as it has been since the nineteenth of Mudmonth, because a locked door is a change and a change is a record and a record is what I have been made into. Third." He put the paper into his coat again. "I have had the object moved this morning at the fourth hour, before I came out on this step, from the storehouse by the fish weir into a room in this building, and the seal has been carried with it and re-laid on the new door, and the room is one this watch uses for a prisoner, and it is the only room in this building that has a lock and a man in it at all times, and I have done that for one reason and I am going to give you the reason and then I am going to tell you why it was the wrong reason and I have not worked out how to do both of those in the same breath.

"I moved it because your instruction says *contact* and it does not say *approach* and it does not say *custody*, and a storehouse by the fish weir has a door that stands open all day on a lane that anybody in this town walks down, and I have read the word *contact* four times and I cannot make it mean the seal. So I moved it into a room with a lock and a man in it, and the custody is unbroken, and the seal is on the door, and there is no gap in it from the nineteenth of Mudmonth to this morning at the fourth hour.

"And that is the right answer and it is the wrong answer, and I have worked out why. I moved it to make the record unbroken. **A record that is unbroken is a thing somebody can use.**"

He said it out loud on the step, in the rain, to a man he had arrested in a different sense of the word eleven weeks before, and then he stood there.

"You understand what I have just said to you."

"I do," said Aren Kest. "You have just told me that the Crown has spent eleven weeks building a chain, and that the whole of the chain is the lawful seizure, and that a record of custody is a thing an office can put in a file and read in a room, and that you have just made the chain unbroken on purpose, and that you have done it because a man in a provincial office used the phrase *held in use among a company of persons* and pinned a commendation on your wall."

"Yes."

"And the one gap in it is nine minutes long on the twenty-seventh of Mudmonth and it is me."

"Yes," said Rell, "and I have had three days to work out what those nine minutes are worth and I am going to tell you, because you are the only person in this county who has asked me a question about it and it has taken you eleven days and I would like to be useful for the rest of the day.

"Everything else in the chain is paper, and paper is cheap and paper is deniable and a Crown can lose a year of paper. **The one thing in it that cannot be got round is a man who came up out of a bridge at a quarter to seven in the morning in front of a market and told four hundred people a city was standing.** That is in a watch sergeant's book and in a magistrate's court and in your own sworn account, and it is the only part of this business that is *falsifiable*, and I have thought about that for eleven days and I cannot get round it either, and neither can you, and if a room ever takes this apart, that nine minutes is what it will be about, and everything else is furniture."

"Then tell them what you have just told me," said Aren Kest, "in a public room, on the record, in your own hand, with the date and the hour on it."

"And be a man who has declared a doubt in the custody of an object he was complimented for keeping, on the eighth day after a commendation with a seal on it was nailed to my own wall," said Rell. "No. And I will tell you why not, and this is the last of it and then I have to go and do a shift.

"Because a doubt is only worth what the record it is in is worth, and the only record in this county worth a thing is the magistrate's, and the magistrate is Lysa Fenn, and she is my reeve's sister, and the two of them have written two minutes in eleven weeks that do not agree with each other, and I have been in this town nineteen years and I know exactly what that means, which is that if I put a doubt in front of a county magistrate this month, the county magistrate has to decide it, and the deciding of it puts this bell in a finding, and a finding is a document, and a document is a thing this town will spend a year arguing about, and while this town argues about it the object sits in a room in this building with a lock on it and a man in it, and a room with a lock on it is a Crown's room by the end of a year whatever anybody says.

"I have thought that through in about eleven seconds and I have got it wrong on four counts and I know that, and I have not got a better one, and I have not slept, and if you have one, Captain, in the next five minutes, then I would rather have it now than in a year, and you are the only man on this street who has ever been in a room where a thing like that was argued, and I have been aware since the twenty-seventh of Mudmonth that you are the mechanism and I have never once resented it, and I am asking you for it because I have run out of my own ideas at about the fourth hour this morning and I would like to go and do a shift."

---

"There is one thing I can give you and it is not an answer," said Aren Kest, "and I am going to give it to you in a doorway and in trade terms, because it is the only language either of us has for this, and because you are asking me a question and I have owed you a question since the twenty-seventh of Mudmonth.

"You have just told me the chain is unbroken and the gap is nine minutes. Both of those are true and I have got nothing against either. I have been in this business for eleven years and I have got one question that is about custody, it is answerable, and I am going to ask it in the plainest words I have got and then I am going to go and do a culvert at Four Elms.

"**Who holds it at three o'clock in the morning when this building has four men on and one of them is asleep?**

"Not who is responsible. Not who is to blame. Who holds it. Because in my trade that is not the same question and it is the only one that has ever mattered, and I have watched a great many men lose an argument because they were asked who was responsible and answered that instead.

"And here is the trade's reason, and it is the only reason I have got, and it is not clever and it is older than I am. A thing that is held by nobody at three o'clock in the morning is a thing that was not held. Not unlawfully. **Not held.** And there is no paper in the world that fixes it, because paper is written by people who are awake, and a chain of custody is a claim about a world in which somebody is always in the room, and that world stopped being this world's world the moment the last man in it went to bed.

"You have done the right thing. You have made the record unbroken, and I have never in eleven years known a man in your position do that, and it is the correct professional act, and it is a legal lie, and both of those are true at once, and you knew that when you did it at four o'clock this morning and you came out on this step and told me, and I do not think there is another watch captain in this county who would have told me."

Rell looked at him.

"It is returnable on the fifteenth," he said.

"Say the rest of it."

"I have carried out an instruction. That is all that paper asks and I have carried it out and I have done it in one day out of a letterbox, and I have done it correctly, and I am now in a position where the only thing left in this county that is unfinished is the paper itself. **It wants an answer.** It is not a letter. A letter is a thing a man may read and think about, and this is an instrument, and an instrument wants a return, and the return has to be filled in, and it has to be filled in by the officer, and it has to say what the object is, who holds it, on what authority it is held, and by whose direction, and where it is.

"And I am going to fill it in. I have had it for a day and the answer is due on the fifteenth and I have not sat down with it and I have not had anybody in this county who can tell me whether I should, and there is nobody in this county who can tell me, and there is nobody in this county who can tell me anything about a Crown's paper, and I have been that for eleven weeks.

"And it is returnable on the fifteenth of Longlight, which is a Saturday, and the return goes on the county's post, and I am going to have to write in a form in a room in this building the words *the office of keeper of the Bellweather hinge*, because the form has a line that asks what matter the object is held in respect of, and that is the matter, and that is the office, and the office does not exist, and there is a magistrate in this county who has asked for the authority and has not been answered, and the asking is dated on the fourth of Greenmonth, and I am going to have to write it out with my own hand on a Saturday and put it on a cart."

He put his hand on the door and then took it off again.

"Now I am going to go and do a shift, and you are going to go and do a culvert, and on Thursday you are going to tell me one thing in trade terms and it is going to be about a form, and I am not going to ask you for it and I am going to wait for it, and that is the arrangement and there is nothing in it for either of us and both of us are in it."

"I will come on Thursday," said Aren Kest.

"I know you will. That is what I have been frightened of since the twenty-seventh of Mudmonth, and it has not stopped me, and I would have liked it to have stopped me, and I have to tell you that about myself because in a month somebody is going to sit in a room and ask me whether I acted on my own judgment, and I want there to be one line in this county's record that says *he was asked by the man the whole of it is about, and I answered him on a step in the rain, and I had been three days awake, and I told him anyway.*"

"You have just told me," said Aren Kest, "and I have heard it, and there is no line in this county's record that says it, because I am not going to be the man who writes it, and you are not going to be the man who asks me to."

---

The rain came harder at the seventh hour and the two of them stood on a step for about a minute and a half afterwards without saying anything, which is the correct amount, and then Captain Rell went in and shut the door on a sheet of paper with four nails in it, and Aren Kest went down Weir Street with a Crown's commendation in his head and a Crown's instruction in his ears and nine days of reading in his legs and a culvert at Four Elms in front of him.

The town had been told nothing. The town was not required to be told, and would be told by itself, out of a market board and a barrow of fish, inside a month, and the sentence it would arrive as would not be *an office has instructed the watch*, and would be *the road is shut and the road warden has gone to the front of a watch house*, and there was no instrument in this county that could get in front of that sentence, and there was not one in the world.

He got to the weir at the eighth hour, because the culvert was at Four Elms and the weir was on the way, and the sill he had dressed on the twenty-first of Greenmonth was under four inches of water and the arrises were holding and the hollow in the third stone was the same hollow, and a lighter went over it at the eighth hour without anybody thinking about it, which was the whole of the point and the whole of the four hours.

Then he went up the Old Toll road in the rain at the ninth hour with his hand on the seat in the fourth course, and there was nothing in it, and the moss had taken the middle of the swept rectangle at last, and the wax on the post had not run, and there was a bell in a room in Weir Street with a lock on it and a Crown's seal on the door and a watch captain in the building who would fill in a form on Saturday the fifteenth of Longlight and write out the name of an office that did not exist, and the road was shut, and the river had dropped, and it was the middle of Longlight and there were sixteen days to the lowest water in the year.
