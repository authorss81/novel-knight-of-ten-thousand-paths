# Chapter 0025

The counting house of the tolls of the great arch of Bellweather is a hundred and forty yards down the wharf from the guild hall, and it has a shuttered window over the water and a door that is shut when it is shut, and it is not shut in the morning.

Aren Kest knew the rule. A woman in an apron with a thumb wet of ink had told it to him on the way back up the channel, and she had got it from a cousin in the north houses, and she had told it flatly and without any pleasure in it, as a thing that is simply the way a counting house is: you do not go in because you want something from it, you go in because it owes you and the book says so and the debt has not been discharged, and nothing else has ever been a door in a hundred and forty years.

He had no claim on that building. Not one. A man who was never owed anything may stand in the doorway and be answered civilly, and if he is answered civilly twice he is a nuisance, and a nuisance on this wharf is a thing that gets a clerk to shut a shutter on you, and a shutter is not the same as a door being shut because the debt is paid.

He stood on the wharf on the twelfth day at the first hour, in the rain, with his coat on his arm because he had been told to come without a coat so that the clerks could see he had nothing in his pockets, and he stood there for an hour and forty minutes, and nothing happened to him at all, which was worse than being turned out.

At the second hour the door opened and Alis Merrow came out onto the step and looked at him for a while without saying anything, and then went back in, and left the door open, which is a different thing from inviting him and is the way a merchant invites a man.

---

The counting room was one room and it was not a small one and it was not beautiful, and there were four desks in it and three clerks, and every desk had a book open on it and a lamp standing at the left-hand corner of it, because a counting house does not have an hour and does not want one.

The room smelled of ink and lamp oil and cold ash and about eighty years of men who had sat at those desks and not been in a hurry. Everything in the room had a case on it. The presses along the far wall were full of lettered boxes. There was a board on the wall with the four seasons and their dues on it in a hand that was two hands old, and under it a list of what the city was forbidden to do without the counting house's entry, and that list was four lines long and the fourth line was about the north road.

"You are the road warden," said Merrow.

"I am a bridge repairer who is paid as a road warden."

"That is the north's whole customs house in a sentence and I am going to hold on to it." He put a chair against the wall with his foot. "There. You may sit. Nobody in this room will ask you a question you have not brought an answer to and I am not going to be a man who asks a stranger to talk in a counting house, because a stranger who talks in a counting house is a man who has been asked what he knows and has found out what he knows."

"I have not come to talk."

"You have come to read a page."

"I have come to read a page and I have not come to buy it and I have not come to plead for it and I have not come to offer anybody anything," said Aren Kest. "I do not have anything. I want to be exact about that, because I have been in rooms this month where a man was offered something by a man who had nothing, and I know what it looks like from the other side of it, and I am telling you in the front of your counting house that I arrived with an empty hand and I am going out with one too."

Alis Merrow looked at him for a moment and then laughed, once, without any pleasure in it at all.

"Six days," he said. "You have been in this city six days and you have worked the fourth camp and the second pier and you have not once asked me for the page, and I told a bench-clerk to your face on the ninth that you were going to come and ask me for it, and you have not. So either you have forgotten, which I do not believe, or you have decided to want it more."

"I have decided to want it more," said Aren Kest, "and I would like to be able to tell you that I have not noticed myself doing it."

"That," said Merrow, "is the only sentence anybody has said in this room in six years that I did not see coming."

---

He explained the book, then, because he was a merchant and merchants explain the thing they want, and because he had decided that this man was going to be in the room eventually and the cheapest way to get there was to stop pretending the door was the point.

"The guild keeps a counterfoil. The stub that comes back. Your writ — the one that was refused, on your side of the world, on a deck — came to us as a tender. That is the word. A tender is a thing offered in satisfaction of a debt, and a writ is a tender, and the only reason a writ is worth anything to anybody is that it is a way of saying *this, in place of money*." He turned round and put his hand flat on a press without opening it. "And so this office keeps the register of what was owed, what was tendered, what came back, what was withdrawn, and who said so. In a column. In a hand. With a number against it. Four hundred leaves. A hundred and forty years.

"And here is the thing that has to be said to a man who wants to read it, and I have said it to three men in eleven years and two of them were Crown officers and the third was a fool. *This is not a secret. Nobody has hidden it. A secret is a thing somebody put somewhere a person cannot get at, and I have never once in twenty-two years found that to be a useful way of keeping a book.* The reason the register has not been read out of this room in a court is that a page in a creditor's book is a receipt, and a receipt is the one document in the world that a person can produce without anybody's permission, and the day it is produced, the person who produced it is not the person who is owed. The person who is owed is whoever the page says is owed.

"You want the page for the Crown. I have read you correctly and do not look pleased about it. You want it because a marshal's paper took a bell off a bridge in your town and you would like a court to be able to say his name out loud. And here is the whole of my objection, and it is not about you, and I want that understood in the room."

He turned and looked at the three clerks, who had not moved.

"You read out one line of a register in a court in a foreign country, and the court asks the next question, and the next question is always the same, and it is: *what was the name in that line owed?*" Merrow put both hands on the back of a chair. "Because a writ is a tender, and a tender is a thing offered in satisfaction, and if the Crown's office tendered a writ against the Alder Vale crossing, then the office had a claim. And a claim that was tendered and then withdrawn is a claim that was *released*. And the only way to find out what a claim was is to ask who was owed by it.

"And if you find out that the name in that line was not owed by the arch, and not owed by the bench, and not owed by this office, and not owed by one of the forty-one houses — then the question after that is what he *was* owed, and by whom, and the answer to that is somewhere in the four hundred leaves, and a court in your country will not stop at the first page, and neither will I."

The rain came off the wharf and went along the boards.

"So that is why nobody has produced it," said Merrow. "Not the Crown. Not a conspiracy, and not a clerk with a fee in his hand, and not a single man in this city who has wanted to hide anything. Forty-one houses and a bench and an abbey have spent eleven years managing a writ, and they have all of them, without ever once saying so in the same room, come to the same conclusion, which is that a name in that book is a war, and this arch is the only crossing in a country of four days, and there are no other crossings, and there is no army in this kingdom, and I have run out of ways to make that sentence sound like prudence and I am going to stop trying to."

---

"Then tell me what is in it for me," said Aren Kest, "since it is not a favour and it is not the truth and I have no money."

"You have no money," said Merrow, "and you have no claim, and you have nothing I want, and you are correct about all three, and if you had said one of them to me in the first minute I would have had you off the step."

He picked up a pen, and then put it down, and picked it up again, and Aren Kest understood that he was about to hear the actual offer, and that the actual offer was a piece of trade and not a bargain.

"Here is the want," said Alis Merrow. "It has nothing to you in it and I am not going to pretend otherwise to make you comfortable, and I have found that men who are being comfortable are more expensive later.

"Three toll-houses on the north road, shut since the nineteenth of Mudmonth, YR 301, with the books open and a man inside twice a year to keep the frost out of the ink. Four feet of paved causeway under the shutter of the second one, in good order, and the paving has not moved. Thirty years of it, reckon it: one house, a good season on the northern run, is about eleven marks a year at the dues my grandfather's board sets out, and the northern run is the only run in this kingdom where a four-ton cart pays and a two-ton cart does not pay enough to be worth stopping. Eleven years shut is a hundred and twenty marks of uncollected toll, and it is not in this office's chest and it never will be, because the house was shut and a shut house takes nothing.

"I want that road open. Not the arch. I have said the arch and I mean the arch, and it is mortgaged to the ninth of a grandson and I have no interest in the arch at all except as a piece of road over a river. *I want the north.* And I want it the way a man wants a thing he can put in a book: as a date, and a season, and a number of loads, and a set of dues, entered in advance, in a hand, with a number against it."

"Why in advance?"

"Because I am not a fool and neither is the clerk who keeps the northern roll, and neither of us is going to bet a hundred and twenty marks on a stranger's word about a road we have never seen. The whole trade of this office is entered *after*. In advance is faith, and faith is a currency I do not take, and if I take it I am a man who bets, and I am not a man who bets. I have been a man who bets once and I did it in the year before last and I am still carrying it."

He put the pen down again.

"So I have been the whole of the time since the ninth trying to buy a date and I have not got one, and a man came out of a road and said the word *road* in a guild hall, and I argued for it in three houses on the strength of it. And here is where you have been standing on my step in the rain for an hour and forty minutes doing it badly."

---

"I did not know what to sell you," said Aren Kest.

"You offered me a road."

"A road is a fact about a bridge and not a wish about a river. I said so myself in the hall, and I have a maker's account and a carpenter's signature and a date on the sheet and I have said in three books that the paper is not the road."

"Correct, and worthless." Merrow stood up. "A road is a thing about stone. A toll is a thing about *risk*. You have not understood what is being bought in this room and you are a bridgewright and you should have understood it in the first minute, and the fact that you did not is the single most northern thing about you.

"Who carries the loss when a four-ton cart goes onto an embankment with four feet of paving on it and the paving is nine winters old and nobody ever had it looked at? Not you. Not me. Not the driver, because a driver who cannot pay does not pay. The man who loses is the man who underwrote the season, and the man who underwrote the season is a man who wrote his name under a figure of loads in a book in this room on a day in Greenmonth, YR 312, and the figure is his and the season is mine and the failure is his. *That is what a toll house is. It is not a gate and it is not a table of dues. It is a man who puts his name under a number about a road he cannot see.*"

He turned round and looked at the door and then back.

"So do not sell me a road. I have four hundred yards of causeway and a city that can lay it, and what I have not got is anybody's hand under a figure. In your country, on a bridge, when a man says four tons, and another man says no, two tons, and one of them is a mason and one of them is a carrier — whose hand is on the load, and what happens to him when the deck is in the water in Fallowmonth?"

"Mine would be," said Aren Kest.

"Yours would. And you have said that out loud in my counting house in front of three clerks, and you have just become the most useful object in Bellweather-Living, and you have no idea what you have done and I am not going to be the man who tells you, because if I tell you, you will go away and think about it, and a man thinking about it is worth nothing to me at all."

A clerk at the second desk said, without looking up: "It is a purchase on credit. A man's name, a figure, and a season, entered as bought and not given, with the goods described. It puts him in the room."

"That is my clerk," said Merrow. "You can be a creditor for one afternoon. Here is what it is and here is what it is not. It is not a contract and I will not write one and if you ask me for one I shall stop this conversation. It is an *entry*. A line in the northern roll, in ink, in a hand, with a number against it, and against your name a figure of loads for one season and a season's date. The goods are a certificate: that on a day you name, on a crossing you name, you read a load, and a man may go on it, and if the deck goes you have said in your own hand that it would carry it. It is a thing I can produce in six months by a clerk who does not like me. And it is not a licence, and it is not a treaty, and it is not a restoration of anything, and it is not the arch, and it is not the page, and it is not even the beginning of the page, and if you say in the next hour that it is the beginning of the page I shall have you put out of my counting house and I shall not be sorry."

"Then I will not say it," said Aren Kest.

"I am going to be a creditor for one afternoon," said Alis Merrow, "and I am going to enter it in the northern roll in a hand that is not mine, and I am going to make it worse for you than a gift would have, and then I am going to open the press, and you are going to read a page, and you are going to say it out loud in this room, because I have found that a man who has said a thing out loud in a room with a ledger in it is a man who cannot go and un-say it, and I have been trying to get somebody into that position since the first day and I have been very nearly getting it out of you every day for twelve days."

He wrote the entry, and he wrote it slowly, and the entry read, in the plainest trade's Latin a clerk in that room could manage:

*Bought, on credit, of Aren Kest of Merefen in a country four days north, the season's certificate of a load on a timber crossing named by the buyer, figure of four tons, season to be named by the buyer and not by this office. In the north. Witnessed. Not a contract, not a licence, not a restoration.*

And then Merrow put it in front of Aren Kest and said, "That is yours. I am not asking you to swear to it. I am asking you to write your own name under it, and if your hand is not the same in this room as in yours, one of my three clerks will say so, and it will be in the book that he said so, and I will not deny it."

Aren Kest read it twice, and it was a good entry, and it was honest, and it was a trap with a shelf on it, and it put him in the room.

He wrote his name.

"You will want the date," said Merrow, and did not supply it, because he had found out on the eighth of his own month, in a shed on a road going up out of a fen, that this man would not buy glass from anybody who supplied a date he had not been asked for.

"The date is when the season opens in the north," said Aren Kest, "and I do not know it, and I will not guess at it in a room where a clerk is writing, and if I put a wrong figure in your northern roll you will have a bad season and I will have told a lie in a business book, and I have never in my life been in a room where that was recoverable."

"The season opens when the water drops," said Merrow. "Say when you think it drops, and be wrong if you have to be wrong, because a wrong figure a man put in a book on his own judgement is a figure he can be held to, and a figure I put in for him is a figure he was given. I have never won an argument in twenty-two years by helping a man."

---

Then Alis Merrow opened the press, and the four hundred leaves came out onto two desks, and the room stopped being a room a stranger was standing in and became the thing it had been for a hundred and forty years: an office at work.

It is not a dramatic object. That is the first thing a man from outside is not prepared for, and Aren Kest was not prepared for it, and had not been prepared for it in twelve days in Bellweather, where the arch is four hundred feet of stone with a washing line over it and the guild hall has a stone in the floor and a bell in a founder's yard under a tarred cloth.

It is a book about money. It is a ruled book, five columns, the hand of every clerk for a hundred and forty years, and there are about nine hundred lines in it, and each line is a *tender*: a thing a person has offered in satisfaction of a debt, with the day, the amount, the offerer's name, and a second column called *returned* which is either the day it came back or a cross.

He had been reading a history. He had come into a counting house and found a ledger.

"Do not read it as a history," said Merrow, watching him. "You will read it as a history and you will be right about the shape of it and wrong about everything in it, and you will say a thing in this room that a hundred and forty years of men did not say. Read it as what it is. It is what was *offered*. In every one of those nine hundred lines somebody in this city was told they could settle a debt with that, and they took it or they did not, and it came back or it did not. That is all. That is a book with no heroes in it, and the book with no heroes in it is the one that gets kept, and the one with heroes in it gets burned in a yard by somebody in a hurry in the year after a fever."

And then Alis Merrow put his finger on a line, near the end of a leaf, and said: "Nineteenth of Mudmonth, YR 301. That one. Read it out."

And Aren Kest read it out.

---

He read it in the flat voice a man uses when he is reading a thing and not reciting it, and he read the whole of it that a creditor's clerk had written, and then he read the line above it, and the line below, because that is what a man does.

*From the office of the marshal, by the hand of a deputy-clerk, sealed and sent: a writ severing the crossing at Alder Vale and its dependent places, and the removal of the persons found within the sealed limit, and the handing of the arch to officers of that office, and the tolls of the arch released to that office for the term of the severance. Tendered on the fourteenth of Mudmonth, YR 301, and tendered against the arch. Returned on the twentieth. Withdrawn on the twenty-second.*

*Witnessed at the tender by a deputy-clerk whose name is entered. Witnessed at the return by the same hand, whose name is entered. Entered at the withdrawal by a third hand, whose name is entered, and it is not the first hand and it is not the second.*

And then, in the last two columns, after the day and after the amount, in the plain trade's hand of a counting house and not in a clerk's flourishment and not in a seal: a *press-mark*.

*Press 4. Die 11. Impression 2 of the season.*

Merrow put his thumb on the press-mark.

"That is the whole of what you came for and it is four characters long," he said. "Not a seal. A press-mark. Because a creditor does not keep the wax. The wax is rubbish in eleven years, the wax is *rubbish*. A creditor keeps which press cut the die, and which die in the press, and which impression of it that season, because that is how a man knows in twenty years whether the paper in front of him is the paper that was sent, and the wax will have gone and the paper will have gone and the press-mark is cut into the page and cannot go anywhere."

Then he went along the wall to a press that was not the one the register had come out of, and opened it, and took out a slim book with a limp cover, and put it on the desk, and it was a *seal-book*: a page for every die in that office's use, with the office's own impression of it in wax, kept because a man who takes a paper in payment is going to be handed paper one day by somebody who has taken it from somebody else, and the seal-book is how he tells.

He turned to a leaf in the middle and nobody was surprised, because the middle of a seal-book is the middle of a hundred and forty years of the Crown's traffic and there is nothing remarkable in the middle of anything.

And in the middle of that page, in wax, in the device of press four, die eleven, was a hand laid flat on a book.

Aren Kest did not say a word.

He had seen that device twice. It was on the grey wax of a sealed order on a post four days north on a bridge in a fen, where it had been rained on since the nineteenth of Mudmonth and had not run. It was on the wax of a storehouse door by a fish weir. It was on the wax of the instrument that Lysa Fenn had held in a salt-loft room in Merefen on the fourth of Greenmonth, YR 312, and had put into a public finding in her own name, in a county with no superior court inside a day's ride, along with the sentence that the office behind it had produced nothing and could not be named.

He had written the description of that device into a sworn account in his own hand and into a report of a bell, and the report was in a filing room over a salt merchant, filed as reported, with an hour and a direction and a tower sight-line, and it was worth nothing to any court for five years and could not be argued with.

It was in a book here, in wax, under a dead man's hand, in a shed on a wharf, and the device on it was the same.

"You have gone a colour," said Alis Merrow.

"You knew."

"I have known for eleven years, and I have had a clerk put a finger on that leaf four times in eleven years for a man with a reason, and the reason has always been that the press-mark on a line was the same press-mark as something else in his pocket." Merrow shut the seal-book. "And you are going to be told what that is worth and I am not going to be the one who tells you, because you already know and because I want to be able to say, in a year, that I did not put it in your head. Sit down."

Then he put his thumb on the page again, on a different column, and said: "And not that."

"That is the whole of what I came for."

"Which column."

"The—"

"*Not that column. Not in this room, and not today.*" Merrow did not raise his voice, and he had not taken his hand off the page, and it was the first time in two hours that anybody in that room had stopped the reading of a book. "You have read a date and four figures. You are now a man who knows where a thing is in a book, which is the most dangerous fact a stranger can hold in a counting house, and I have had one in this building before and it went badly for me and it went badly for two men who are not alive. So we are going to stop here. I will have that line read to you in full, all of it, on a day of its own, with the conditions set out first, and you will come back at the second hour and be given it in one piece, and I will sit in that chair while you read it and I will not say a word while you read it."

"And if I do not come back."

"Then in a year a clerk will open a book at a line and there will not be a man in the room, and I will have lost nothing and you will have lost everything, and I will not lose sleep about it, and you will." He took the key off the clerk's neck and put it on his own belt, which the three clerks noticed and said nothing about, because it had not been done before. "The second hour. Tomorrow. Without a coat, so that the clerks can see you have nothing in your pockets, because they have been asked to look at pockets in this building for eleven years and they are extremely good at it."

---

He walked back up the wharf in the rain and got about forty yards and had to stop, and then he stood on the crown of the arch under a sky going the colour of a wet slate and did the arithmetic a man does when the arithmetic has stopped being a road and become a person.

*Nineteenth of Mudmonth, YR 301. A creditor's book, in a shed on this wharf, has a line for that date, and the line says a writ was tendered against this arch and came back on the twentieth and was withdrawn on the twenty-second. And in the same line, in wax, in this office's own seal-book, there is a hand laid flat on a book, and I have seen that hand in wax on two doors and one paper in a county four days off, and a woman put it in a court record in her own name and was told there was no office behind it.*

*He has offered me a name. He has not said so. He has said a great deal about the shape of a page and nothing whatever about a person, and that is the same as saying it, and I have not got it, and I could not carry it if I had, and he knows both of those, and he has made me wait a day in order to teach me the second one in a way I cannot argue with.*

*Two days. I have two days. And on the second day I am going to be in that room again, and the reason I am not being read the whole of it this afternoon is that a counting house cannot have a man walk out of it with a name in him at two o'clock on a Tuesday, and he said that to me as a piece of customer service, and it is the single most frightening thing anybody has said to me since I came up these steps.*

He went and worked. The fourth camp had nine hundred setts to dress by the fourth hour the next day, and there was a man at the far end of the rubbing floor who had begun, somewhere around the sixth day, to check his own work with a string and a plumb line, and had begun to check the *staging* with a plumb line, which nobody in this city did and nobody in this city had any reason to, because a staging is a thing a man stands on.

"You are checking the staging," Aren Kest said.

"I am checking the staging."

"It is not crooked."

"It is not crooked," said the man, "and it will not be crooked on Friday either, and that is the whole of why a man checks a thing while it is cheap to check." He took the plumb away and hung it on the nail. "I am forty-one and I have watched three good men go into a river on this crossing in my working life, and none of them were in a hurry, and two of them were the best stone-setters in the city, and one of them was checking his own work, and one of them was not."
