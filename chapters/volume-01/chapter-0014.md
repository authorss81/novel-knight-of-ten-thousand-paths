# Chapter 0014

The fight on the afternoon of the second of Greenmonth was about eleven days and four shillings, and by four o'clock Mara Vey had come to the end of every position she had except the one that was true, which was that the Bellweather Claims Archive was poor and the thing they were arguing over was the price of opening a book.

They were in the outer room because the inner room held six people and the office held eleven, and because the office had spent two years being careful about who was in a room when a Crown officer was in it. Halven had come down with the county's clerk and was sitting in a good coat on a bench that was not built for sitting in, with a locked wooden chest on the floor between his boots, and the chest was the reason for the visit and everybody in the room knew it inside a quarter of an hour.

A registrar travels with the roll he is working on. Mara Vey had learned that in her second year and had thought it a curiosity and had since thought of it several times in the small hours, because a man who keeps a public book in a locked chest under his own bed cannot be asked where it is, and cannot say it has been borrowed, and cannot be asked to produce it and be unable to. There is no Crown office in Neyra that can be got at as easily as a man with a chest on the floor between his boots.

"Master Halven," she said. "Thank you for coming down. I have your schedule of the second and I have a great deal of time to put into it and no money at all, and I would rather say that to your face than send a letter about it."

"I have been sent a great many letters about money by people who had it," said Halven.

He was forty-one, and about half of that was the provincial town, and he had a way of arriving in a conversation that made the conversation's subject sound like somebody else's business. He had quoted Mara four shillings in Hearthmonth for one consultation, and had been paid, and had written that the roll was with the marshal's emergency office and could not be moved, and Mara had entered it in the day-book as an expense and as a fact, and had never yet decided which of the two entries mattered more.

"Then let us be brief and let us both be spared," said Halven. "You have asked for the Bellweather salvage and clearance entries. I have them. I have brought the volume, because a volume that leaves the registry travels with a man, and because you have not paid me the four shillings I quoted you in Hearthmonth and I would rather not be a man who sends a thing out of a room and cannot say what was in it when it came back."

"You quoted me four shillings for the discovery that a file exists and is elsewhere."

"I quoted you four shillings for a consultation on a subject on which I was not to be instructed, which is a different service, and if I have been guilty of overcharging you for it then I shall continue to be guilty of overcharging you for it, because it is the only part of the office that pays for itself."

"Name your price for the rest."

Halven took a folded paper out of his coat — not out of the chest, which Mara noted — and opened it on the bench beside him, and it was not the same paper as the schedule. The schedule was printed. This one was a copy in a fair hand, and it had a list on it, and the list had a heading, and the heading was the sort of heading a man writes for himself.

"*Anchors, schedules and dispositions, Alder Vale,*"

"Stop," said Mara.

"That is the head of a book in my registry," said Halven. "It is a schedule of the anchors of the Alder Vale crossing and of the material held in trust for it: the bells, the tie-stones, the ironwork, the lamps, the water-stones, and the disposal of each. It is Crown property and it has been Crown property since the year the crossing was made, because the Crown's road money on that valley was secured against the anchors and you cannot secure money against a thing you have not written down. It runs from the founding of the schedule to the year the valley was closed. It is the only book in that registry that names *objects*, as opposed to claims, people, and money."

"Which is on your roll."

"Which is on my roll as a book, dated and numbered and shelved, in the fourth press." Halven put a finger on the fair copy. "The book is not in the fourth press."

The room got very quiet, and Mara Vey became conscious of the river under the floor and of her own hands.

"When you say it is not there," she said, "say what is there instead, because a registrar has five sentences for a missing book and I would like the one that is true."

"Four of the five would be ordinary. One is not." Halven looked at his copy, which he did not need to look at. "The register of the volume says the book is in the fourth press. The fourth press has been opened eleven times in the last two years for productions and every one of those eleven is a slip I have personally signed, and I know what is on the second shelf of that press, because I put the volume away myself in Frostmonth, YR 309, and the book that should be next to it in YR 302 is not next to it, and there is a gap on the shelf the width of about two inches and a half and I have not entered the gap in anything, because if I enter the gap in anything I have to write *missing* in a Crown register, and a Crown register does not take that from a registrar without a schedule, and the schedule is the book."

"You have a gap on a shelf."

"I have a gap on a shelf and a line in a roll and a book that was there for eight years, and I have said three sentences about it to a magistrate's clerk in the provincial town in the last year and each time it was taken as a jest, and it is not a jest, and I have not raised it in writing because a registrar who raises a missing Crown schedule in writing is a registrar who has told the Crown he cannot account for the Crown."

Mara Vey put both hands flat on the table.

"Master Halven. I want to be very careful with you, because you have just said something that could be read three ways and I have a professional obligation to be clear about which of them I have heard."

"Then be clear."

"You have a gap on a shelf. That is what you have told me. You have not told me that a book was removed by a person, and you have not told me that it was removed lawfully, and you have not told me that the gap is my business, and I am going to write in my day-book this evening exactly the sentence you said and not one word more, and if anybody in this room or any other asks you about the gap, I will repeat that sentence word for word and I will not improve on it." She took her hand off the table. "Do you want me to say that in front of a witness?"

"Yes," said Halven. "That is the reason I came down in person instead of sending the schedule by the week's cart. I have been a registrar for nineteen years and a clerk for nine before that, and I have never once in twenty-eight years removed a book from a press, and I have never once been asked to, and there is a difference between those two sentences that nobody in the provincial town has ever been able to hear me say."

"Then say it here, and I will write it, and it will be a document in a room above a salt merchant and not in a Crown register, and that is a great deal better for you and a great deal worse for me, and I would like you to say it out loud anyway."

"Off the roll," said Halven, "by my own hand, and not in any hand of the office, in the fourth press of the provincial registry, in the year last, on a date I have not entered, and no hand of mine has removed it."

Sarn Oyelaran wrote it down.

She was twenty-six and she had been copying in this office for two years, and she copied faster than anybody Mara had ever worked with and she did it with a look of open boredom that Mara had been carefully not insulting. She had a fair small hand, a very good ear for a date, and the habit — which Mara had corrected once and which had come straight back — of ruling her columns before she had read the whole page.

She said it in the copyist's flat voice: "Entered on the roll, the fourth, and not entered on the shelf, and a registrar did not do it, and I do not know what that is."

"Then it is a fact and not yet a thing," said Mara. "Set it aside, Sarn."

"May I ask what a fact and not yet a thing is?"

"You may not. Write the sentence and put it in the working file." She had not said the word *dispute* and she was aware of not saying it, because a disputed document is a document with a mark on it and a document with a mark on it is a document the Crown can carry to a magistrate and say *our own office does not accept it*. "Master Halven, I will now do the thing I would rather not do, which is to tell you exactly what I am able to pay and exactly when, and to tell you both before I leave rather than in a letter a week hence."

"Please."

"Your schedule of charges for the production of anything touching my entry. The registration fee of four shillings I can pay this afternoon and I will pay it, and I want you to know I am paying it out of money that is meant for paper and salt, and I want that on the sheet so that in a year somebody can see what it cost me and not what it was worth."

"Four shillings is not a fortune, Miss Vey."

"Four shillings is a fortnight's copying and I have not had a fortnight of copying since Harvestmonth." She turned the printed schedule round and put her finger on a line in the middle of it. "This is the one I cannot pay. The search of the closed press. A mark and a half and the clerk's day, and it is the only item on your schedule that can produce anything, because everything else on this list is a copy of a thing that exists and the closed press is where a book that does not exist would be."

"That is accurate."

"It is, and I have not got it, and I am not going to pretend to a registrar that I have." Mara Vey squared her hands on the table. "So I am going to do two things and I am going to do them both in front of you, because you have been honest in this room and the least I can do is be as exact. First: I pay the four shillings now, and it holds the date. Your office will keep my request open and dated from today, and I understand that a request with a date against it is a request that has to be answered in the ordinary way, and I understand that the ordinary way takes as long as it takes."

"And the second?"

"I am writing to the magistrate of this county today for an order to inspect a register without fee, and I am going to tell you the truth about that order, which is that from the day it is stamped it will take eleven days, and that it is the only weapon I have, and that when it comes it will be a paper from a woman who has already been paid once by you and will be paid again by a clerk at a different table, and that a man in your position will be able to refuse the order and still be right, and that the Crown will then be able to say that the Bellweather Claims Archive asked a registrar to break his own rule." She looked at him. "You will not lose your post over this. A keeper of a public register has no jurisdiction. That is the whole of your authority and the whole of your protection: you cannot decide what a register means, and so you cannot be blamed for what it says, and so you cannot refuse a fee without a magistrate, and so you cannot be got at by anybody who has not first got at a magistrate. I have had that figure explained to me twice in four years and I have only now understood that it is a thing a Crown builds on purpose, and that it has kept at least one honest man in his chair, and that it has cost the rest of us eleven days each time."

There was a pause.

"Miss Vey," said Halven, "you are the only person in this county who has put that in those words, and I have been quoting the phrase *no jurisdiction* out of the standing orders at people for nineteen years as though it were a defence, and you have just told me it is a wall that I live in the middle of, and I would like to sit down a moment longer than the business requires, and then I should like to go and find whether the provincial town can produce an order of its own, because if one can be produced here in nine days instead of eleven, you will have it in nine days, and I do not care to think that I have taken eleven days off a woman's file because I was enjoying the conversation."

"Nine days would be a kindness and I would take it," said Mara, "and I will not put it in my day-book as a kindness, I will put it in as a fee paid at a date, because the moment a registrar's kindness is in a file it becomes a debt, and I have enough of those."

Then she wrote the four shillings out on a slip, and the slip went into the chest, and Halven signed the slip, and the signing was the one moment in the whole two hours when anybody in that room believed anything at all.

At the door, with his hand on the stair, he stopped and did not turn round.

"Miss Vey. One thing, seriously, and then I will go and be unhelpful in a different direction." He shifted the chest. "There is a schedule in that book for the bells of the Alder Vale crossing. Three of them. I have never had cause to open it and I have never had cause not to. I am telling you that it exists, and I am telling you that I have thought about it for about two seconds, which is more than I usually think, and I am telling you that a thing which is in my head and not in my register is worth nothing to anybody, including me, and that if I ever think about it again I shall find that I have thought about it in a room with a person in it, and that person will be an officer of a court or a keeper of an office and will be a fool if they write it down in the wrong week."

He went down the stair with the chest.

---

Mara Vey stood in the outer room with a receipt for four shillings in her hand and eleven days started, and let out a breath she had been holding since the sixth hour, and then said:

"Sarn."

"Ma'am."

"Shut the door."

Sarn shut the door, and came to the table, and put her hand flat on a page of the copy she had been making for two hours while the three of them talked over her head.

"You heard a registrar of nineteen years tell a clerk that he did not do a thing and ask her to write it down," said Mara. "That is now a document in this office. It is a better document than the fee schedule, because the fee schedule is a fact about money and that is a fact about a man. I want your name on the wrapper as the copyist, with the hour, and I want it in your own hand and not in mine, because in four years there is going to be a man who says that a woman wrote down what a registrar said because she wanted a thing."

"I wrote it."

"I know you wrote it. That is not the point." Mara sat down, which she had not done for two hours. "The point is that if I am wrong about this, and if in a year a Crown office decides that this office is a nuisance and comes to take the room and the press and the day-books, then the person they will want is the one who copied it, and not the one who heard it. Do you understand what I am telling you?"

"That I am the one who can be got at."

"That you are the one who can be got at, and that I have thought about it and I cannot avoid it, and that the honest arrangement is not to protect you from it. It is to make sure that if it happens, it happens to you as the person who did the work, in your own hand, and not as a woman who had a clerk in the room." She squared the copy. "You are twenty-six. You have been copying in this building for two years and I have never once asked you whether you have read one."

"No," said Sarn. "You have not."

"I have not, and that has been my own convenience and not your fault, and I am going to stop it." Mara put the receipt away. "I want to say something to you that is not about money and is about a post, and I want you to hear the shape of it before I say the content of it. This office has a keeper and a clerk and two rooms and a press, and the keeper is a woman of twenty-nine with a salary that is a room and paper, and the clerk is you, and there is no third post and there never has been. In a year's time I intend to be able to give you a warrant, with your name in it, and not a letter saying that you have been useful. And when there is a third post, it will be a post that exists on paper, with a holder, and a person can point at the paper and say that the office has a third post and that the third post's holder is named on it. That is the whole of the ambition and it is not a large one."

Sarn Oyelaran looked at her own hand on the page, and then at the door, which was shut, and then at the window, which was giving up the light.

"If there is a warrant, there is a warrant to recall," she said. "I know that. I am not a child."

"You are not a child, and that is exactly why I am telling you now rather than promising it to you in six months." Mara turned the day-book round and put the pen down beside it, in Mara Vey's own hand, and not a fair copy. "So that you can have one object in this office that is not a copy of somebody else's. Write it out. It is four lines and it is not clever and it is the only thing I have ever been able to promise anybody in four years of doing this."

They wrote it out. It was four lines and it was not clever and it was dated the second of Greenmonth and signed by the keeper of the Bellweather Claims Archive, and it went into the day-book and not into a file, because a thing you intend to be able to prove in a year should not be in a file that can be lost.

The light went off the upper window. Sarn gathered the copies, and squared them, and did the thing Mara had corrected her about once — ruled the columns of the last sheet before reading it — and stopped, and went back, and took the sheet off the pile again and read the whole of the page.

"Ma'am."

"Mm."

"This one is out of place." She put her finger on it without touching the ink. "The Bellweather clearance. It is the year three hundred and two, and it sits in a bound order between two entries of three hundred and four, and the entries on either side of it are the fair copy of a roll that has been kept in order since the founding, and this one is in a different hand, and the hand is not older or younger, it is just a hand that has not seen the rest of the book."

Mara Vey came and stood behind her and read the page, all of it, at a speed that would have been a scandal in her inner room, and then she read it again.

"Out of place," she said.

"Out of place. Rolls get misbound. It happens."

"It happens," she agreed, and put her hand on the shelf-edge and left it there, and looked at nothing at all for the length of four breaths. "Sarn, I am going to tell you the truth about the state of my head this afternoon, because you are the only person in this building I can say it to and I would like to say it out loud once. I have been wrong in this building four times in four years, every one of them by being sure. A man's whole life can turn on a thing being got exactly right and I have got it exactly right four times and the fifth was a girl at Alder Cross who is dead."

"Yes, ma'am."

"Set that page aside. Not in the working file. In the press, on its own, with the date on the wrapper, and do not tell me about it again until I ask you, and I will ask you, and I do not yet know when." She straightened up. "Go home. You have been in this room since the eighth hour and I have not once asked you to stop, and that was me being lazy and not me being generous, and I want you to hear which of the two it was."

Sarn Oyelaran went home.

---

Mara Vey shut the press, and the bar on the clamp was out of true, and her hand found the twist and took the bar straight without a tool, and she was alone in the room and there was nobody at all to see her do it.

She had four shillings less than she had had that morning, an order written and not sent, a registrar's honest sentence in a working file, a page in a press that was out of place in a way she could not yet name, and eleven days.

She sat with the river going under the floor and the day-book shut, and worked out, because it was that kind of afternoon and because the arithmetic did not care what else she was doing, that the office would hold four marks and eleven pence to the end of Greenmonth, and that the order she was writing now would be stamped on the fourth, and that eleven days from the fourth was the fifteenth, and that on the fourth of the month there would be a hearing in the inner room with a magistrate and a watch captain and a garrison of a town's opinion, and that she would have to be in that room, and that the money for it did not exist, and that the fight she had just spent the afternoon having was about four shillings to hold a date.

She wrote the day out in full. And then, at the bottom of the page, in the ordinary ink, because it was a fact and not yet a thing:

*A registrar who keeps a book he cannot account for is a man in a building with a wall round it, and the wall is a rule he did not write and cannot refuse. He gave me a sentence in a shut room and asked me to write it down. Nobody in this county has ever given me anything at no charge, and I have made a habit of expecting payment, and I sat there waiting to be charged and he knew I was doing it.*

*Eleven days. A fee I can pay and a fee I cannot. A book with a gap on a shelf. A page out of place in a hand that is the same age as the hands around it.*

*And a warrant for a third post, in my own hand, in a day-book, dated today, which I intend to be able to produce in a year, and which is the first promise I have made anybody in this office in four years and the only one I can keep entirely on my own.*
