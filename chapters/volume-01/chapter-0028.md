# Chapter 0028

Aren Kest answered Captain Ruen's question on the crown of the arch, standing up, at the twelfth hour, with a lamp and a river and no witnesses, and he answered it in the wrong order, on purpose, because he had been in rooms for fourteen days where a man said the easy half first.

"The man building the fourth camp is a road warden of Merefen in a fen county and he is paid a shilling a day and he has a bond on a roll and eleven days of wages that a trust will not pay," he said. "That man is the same man. And he was on this deck on the nineteenth of Mudmonth, YR 301, in a Crown's cloak, at twenty-one years old, and he was on it at the second hour of the night, and that is the half that will not cost you anything and I am giving it to you first so that you know which half of me you are talking to."

"You were on the deck."

"I was at the north gate and then I was on the deck. I was in the valley nine days. I had a written order with a seal on it and it covered the Alder Vale crossing and eleven men and nothing else, and the Alder Vale crossing is a stone bridge four miles inside the valley and it is not this arch, and nobody in that office ever told me that one place in that valley was worth more than another place in it, and I did not ask, because I was twenty-one and I had been made a captain by brevet and I believed that a man given a charge had been *given the whole of it*."

The lamp was held down and the water went under the arch.

"Say the rest."

"My order did not cover this city. Not this crossing, not the north gate, not the inner city — I do not know what the inner city is called, I have never been in it, I got as far as the low quarter and a street of shuttered windows and the water steps, and I have never in eleven years found out what is at the end of that street. I had a written order for a bridge four miles in. On the evening of the seventeenth, at the sixth hour, I sent a rider up to the office that had sent the order, asking for a written extension covering the arch, because a Crown's officer had told me on the fourteenth at a table that the crossing at Bellweather was in the same term and that if it was severed the Alder Vale would be let go with it, and I believed him, and I asked in writing. It came back on the eighteenth at the second hour. It was one line. It refused the extension, and it was signed, and it is the reason I did what I did at the second hour on the deck, and I did not have it in front of me at the second hour — I had it at noon, when I was already in a cell, and the copy of it is the reason there is not a lie in this story instead of a failure."

"What did you do at the second hour?"

"I did not hold the deck." He said it flat, because a man who softens that sentence has written a speech. "There were two hundred people on it with the bar stock and I had eleven men and a written order for a different bridge, and the Crown's officer on the toll stone had a written duty and no instruction to use it and he was sat down, and I had no authority over him and no authority over two hundred people, and I did the correct thing according to every instruction I had ever been given, which was to hold what I had been given and not to take what I had not. And by noon there was a finding in it saying that a captain lost a bridge."

Captain Ruen was quiet for a while.

"I have been angry at you," he said. "For three days. I want you to understand that it was not at you, because I did not know there was a you. I have been angry at a story, and the story is that this city was taken by somebody who was bad at his job, and I have said that story at four tables in eleven years and I have been wrong every time and I could never find out who I was wrong about, and it turns out the man I was angry at was twenty-one and had been given half an order and had a rider come back the day before saying no."

"Do not do that," said Aren Kest. "I would rather you were angry. If you take it off me now then in nine years there will be a man in a market who was angry and I will be a man nobody was angry with, and that man will be able to say anything."

Ruen made a sound that was not quite a laugh.

"My daughter is going to be interested in you," he said, "in a way I have not got a sentence for, and I would like you to understand before it happens that I have not asked you this for the case. I asked you for her. And I am going to go home now and I am not going to make anything up, and I am going to tell her that there is a road and a man on it, and I am going to tell her both halves, including the one about the second hour, and she is fifteen and she will find out in about a year that I left half of it out, because she is her mother's daughter and she counts."

---

The last week was the last week and nobody in Bellweather-Living called it that.

Aren Kest worked. He was on the fourth camp at the fourth hour every day of it, and the fourth camp was a hundred and forty paces of the north bank with three drying floors and a rubbing floor and a pitching floor and a store, and it was cutting and laying setts on the gate bridge approach — the approach, not the span, because the span was four timber bays on soft ground at the town end and the third bay was a hole with a plank across it and the guild had no oak and would not have oak before Longlight.

The third bay came out on the sixteenth day, and it was worse than the sounding rod said, and the sounding rod was Aren's own account of the north and he had told Alis Merrow in a counting house that a man with a rod would have that bay open in a day, and the man with a rod was on the north side of the world and there was no rod on this side, and they got the answer by putting a chalk line on the deck at low water and watching it and a man in a boat with a line and a weight, and it took two days, and the answer was that a third of the third bay was not there.

The ferrywright put him on the fifth bay of a new section to the south and let him do the whole of it himself, and the second of the second pier came up to the staging and watched him do the first day's work and then went and did his own, and did not comment, which was the highest thing that trade has.

He wrote in the notebook on the seventeenth day, and it is the only line in the whole of nineteen days that is about himself:

*Seventeenth day. I have not said the true thing and I am going to have to say it in a room, and I have been putting it off for five days by cutting setts, and a man who cuts setts instead of saying a thing is a man doing what I did at the second hour on a deck in YR 301, and I have noticed it, and noticing it is not the same as stopping it.*

---

The bench sat on the eighteenth day, YR 312, at the ninth hour, with the shutters up and about sixty people at the back and the ferrywright's lamp on the table and the bench-clerk's pen already out.

There is no truth in that, said Aren Kest, when he had got to the front and the hall had gone the way a hall goes.

"I am going to say a thing out loud in a room and I am going to say it badly, and I would like the clerk to write it in the form I say it and not improve it, because I have been on the improving end of that trade for twenty-one years and it has cost this country a city.

"There is a document in the north. A sworn account, in my own hand, entered in a magistrate's court in a county four days off, on the thirtieth of Mudmonth, YR 312, six weeks ago, with a claim number and a chain of custody with three hands on it. It says four things and I will read the four things out and then I will say the fifth, and the fifth is not in the document, and that is why I am standing here.

"One. That a bell in the north is this city's bell. Two. That I was twenty-one years old at the time and was given part of a written order and did not reach the inner city. Three. That for three years afterwards I said out loud, in rooms, to men who wrote it down, that a fever had taken this valley. Four. That for those same three years I kept my own field notes in the breast pocket of a coat and did not hand them to the inquiry that was sitting to hear me.

"That is in the document. It has been in a public file for six weeks, and I put it there by swearing it, and I did not write a word of the finding that is on the top of it, and I am the man who is in it, and I know what I am about to do with myself tonight and I have done it to other people by accident and it took about six years."

Nobody in the hall moved.

"The fifth thing is this. I was on the deck of this arch on the night of the nineteenth of Mudmonth, YR 301, in a Crown's cloak, at the second hour, with two hundred people on it who had not been ordered there. I have never said that in any room in the world, including to the woman who put the document in the file, including to my brother. And it is the fifth thing because a man can be in two places in one night and be a fool in one of them and be given a finding in the other, and the finding in the other is the one that killed a valley and left this one alive, and I have let that be true for eleven years without ever once standing in a room and saying the first half out loud."

He put his hand into the breast of the coat.

"I have brought this in with me twice," he said. "Both times. It has not been out of that pocket in eleven years and it has been into this city twice and it has not been read by anybody in this world and I am going to take it out and put it on this table, and I want the clerk to write down the hour, and I want him to write down that I have not read a word of it in this room and do not intend to, and I want him to write that it goes into the hall's book and not into anybody's keeping, and I would like the bench to decide what to do with it, and I would like the bench to decide it after I have said the other two things, because I would like the other two things to be said in a room and not in a document."

He put a small oilcloth bundle on the table, and it was the size of a man's hand, and it had been round a field book since the year of the cull and the corners were soft as cloth, and the bench-clerk looked at it and did not touch it until the woman at the end of the table said, "Now," and then he put his hand flat on it and wrote the hour and the words and nothing else.

"The other two things," said Aren Kest.

"The first is that I did not know what I was told. I have spent every year since that morning trying to find out what was in the order, and I have got a piece of it — a one-line refusal of an extension, signed and dated, in a copy — and I have had it since YR 301, in the morning, in a cell, and it is the reason there is no lie in this story, and it is also the reason I cannot tell you what I was told, and I have made my defence out of that for eleven years and I would like it noticed that a man who has been defending himself with an absence for eleven years has stopped defending and has started asking.

"The second is that a Crown office built the case on a boy, and it built it correctly, and it built it because I was twenty-one and had no following and my own advancement made it easy to pin a thing on me, and I have said for eleven years that I was too young to be believed and I have never once said the other half, which is that I was exactly the right age to be believed, and that nobody in that room was lied to about my age, and that the only lie in the whole of it was the fever and the fever was true."

The ferrywright put her lamp down on the table.

"So the bench has a man in it who has said out loud that a Crown ordered the severance of this crossing, and that a name was in a creditor's book, and that the man with the name was a boy, and that the boy was on this deck. That is four things, and the fourth one is a man telling a room that he was in a place at an hour, and it is eleven years late, and it is worth nothing to this bench as evidence of anything at all, and the bench knows that."

The man with the mallet turned the mallet over on the table, twice, which was the only fidget he had.

"It is worth one thing to this bench," he said, "and the thing is that we have spent five days with a name in our heads and no idea what to do with it, and now we know the man who says it is a man who was standing in the room when it happened, and that changes what it is for. Not what it proves. What it is for."

"It is for a war," said Alis Merrow, from the near side, and everybody turned, and he had not been announced and he had been in the room for a quarter of an hour.

"It is for a war," he agreed, "and I have been in a counting house for twenty-two years and I have a clerk who wrote the third hand in a business book in YR 301, and I told you in that room five days ago what happens to a name in that book, and it happens. So I am going to say the only thing I have ever said in a hall in twenty-two years, and then I am going to go home.

"I will not be the man who opens a war with a book of debts in it. I have four hundred leaves behind me and thirty years of a city that has nothing to be proud of except that it kept standing, and if the next thing that comes out of this hall is a Crown with a fleet, then I will be a man who spent five days getting a name into a room, and my grandfather's grandfather did not build anything and I would like to be a person who did not hand this city a Crown to hide behind."

"So the name stays in the counting house," said the man with the mallet. "Agreed. And the name is a fact in a foreign country that a bench cannot use, and the man who has read it cannot carry it, and the page cannot leave. Then the bench is going to have to do the only thing a bench can do, which is to say out loud in a room that we have been told something and we are not going to do anything about it this year, and hold the arch, and go on marking the second pier twice a year."

"Yes," said Alis Merrow. "And I would like the bench to mark me down as a man who has said so in a hall, and I would like the date on it, because in nine years somebody is going to say the toll houses were the reason nothing was done."

"You are the reason nothing was done," said the man with the mallet. "It is written in the book. Go home."

---

Then the terms, and Aren Kest found that he had nothing to say about them, which had not happened to him in sixteen days.

"First," said the man with the mallet. "The consent with conditions, as read out on the seventeenth, and as written, and as signed by the woman it belongs to, and it is hers and not the bench's, and the bench will not stand in its way and will not argue with it. Second. A named person, and her name is on the book, and she keeps the road, and a road kept by a person can be seen. Third. A promise with a date on it. And the date is not the bench's date and it is not yours. It is the day the second pier is marked, in the middle of the year, at the lowest water, by two men and a clerk, in this hall, and the clerk will write down in the bench's book on what day of the year that falls, and the page will be cut for you now, in front of the bench, and the woman who keeps the road will hold it."

"And the fourth," said the ferrywright, from the side. "Which is mine and which I would like said in the same breath as the rest, because a man who is going to be four days off for a season, and who cannot be reached while he is there, ought to be told what is being kept in his place, and not only what is being asked of him."

"Go on."

"You will have no road out of your country except on that date, and nobody on this side will be able to reach you, and you will not be able to send a thing, and if the pier goes between now and then, or the river comes up, or a child in your town is ill, then the bench will be told by a traveller in a year or not at all, and there is nothing in this hall that can change that, and I am not going to pretend to you that there is." She had her hands flat on the table. "That is the whole of the fifth term and it is mine and not the bench's and you are agreeing to it in a room and not in a letter, and Captain Ruen is here and he is going to write his name under the same line, and the clerk is going to enter the hour, and that is two people and one record, and I have thought about that for four days and I have not been able to improve on it."

"And the price," said Aren Kest.

"The price is yours to name and not ours, and I am going to name it for you because I have watched you for sixteen days and you will not name it and it is the only thing in this hall that you have not been honest about, and that is not a compliment."

The hall was quiet.

"Three a season," said Aren Kest.

"I do not know what that means."

"It means the road crews in my country have a saying and the saying is three, and a man who has not three to give should not begin, and it is not a rule anybody can argue with because it is not a rule, it is four generations of men who have found out the hard way. I spent one in Mudmonth. I am in the second now. When I go out of this hall on this side I will have one left in the year, and there are two in me at this moment and the year turns in about a month." He said it flat, and did not look at the man with the mallet. "And your date is in the middle of the year. So I am telling you plainly: the third one is yours, and if the other two are gone by the time your water drops then I have nothing, and I will have to find out whether that is the same as being able to come. I do not know. Nobody in this room knows, and I have not been able to find out in nineteen days, and I would rather say that in a hall than work it out quietly in a bed and turn up in the spring or not turn up at all."

"That is not a price," said the man with the mallet. "That is a man telling us he does not know."

"There is a second half and it is worse," said Aren Kest. "The seat I go out of and come back through is a block of the fourth course with two pieces of Steppe glass in it, and it has been used twice. The man who dressed the glass told me, in a shed, in his own hand, on a page he gave me and would not charge for, that a scar takes two, and that three is not a number he would sell to a stranger and would not sell to a friend, and there is no fresh Steppe glass north of my town before Longlight, and I have no money, and I have no maker. So on your date, in the middle of the year, somebody has to have cut me a new seat and set it, and paid for it, and I do not know who, and I cannot promise you I will be there, and if I am not there you write *not here* and that is the end of the road for a season."

"That is the price," said the man with the mallet, after a moment. "Write both halves. The three, and the seat."

Nobody said anything for a moment.

"Then the bench records it," said the man with the mallet. "The bench records that the man has put his third of three into this room, on this date, at this hour, and that the bench will hold the arch and mark the pier, and that the bench is aware that it is holding a road for a man who cannot come, and that is the state of the world and the bench is not going to be consoled about it in a market."

"Read it back," said Aren Kest, "all of it, and let me hear it."

The bench-clerk read it back, and made him hear it twice, and the second reading was in a flat hand and it took four minutes and it said: the consent with its conditions, the named person, the visible road, the promise with a date, the two witnesses, the hour, the third of three, the mark on the pier, the field book in the hall's keeping with the hour written on the wrapper in the clerk's hand, and at the foot of it, in the clerk's own addition, which he read out last and which the bench had not known he was going to write:

*And entered in this hall, on the same page, at the same hour, in this hand, that the said Aren Kest has stated in this room that he was on the deck of this arch on the nineteenth of Mudmonth, YR 301, at the second hour of the night, and has stated it for the first time, eleven years after it happened, and that this bench has entered the statement as a statement of a man and not as a finding of this bench, and that the said Kest is a witness against himself in this matter and against the account his own office has kept since the thirtieth of Mudmonth, and that the bench has heard him say so and has not been asked to believe him and has not been asked to forgive him, and that it is recorded here because the bench does not have a column for the other things and has refused to invent one.*

Nobody in the hall said anything at all for the length of a slow count of ten, and then the woman on the step by the door, who had not taken her hood off at any point in three hours, said:

"That will do."

---

He did not sleep. He sat on the crown of the arch until the second hour with the ferrywright's lamp on the stones beside him, and she came out at the first and stood at the other end and did not come closer than a woman needs to, which was a thing a man had done to him on a bridge in a fen ten weeks ago and he had not known at the time that it was the same gesture.

"You will be back on the date," she said, not as a question.

"On the date or not at all."

"Then I will be here, and two men and a clerk will be here, and there will be a line on the pier, and if you are not here I will write *not here* in the book and you will find it in a year, and that is the arrangement and it is not a promise, because a promise is a thing the north keeps and a date is a thing this side keeps."

"And if the pier is worse?"

"Then it is worse, and I will have a page about it, and you will have a page about it, and neither of us will have done anything about it, and the bench will have held it, and in forty years a man will dig it up." She picked the lamp up. "Go to bed. You have to be on the fourth camp at the fourth hour and I have told them so and they will be there, and if they are not I will have somebody's ears, and it will not be mine."

---

He got his bed after the bench on the eighteenth day in a room over a boat-yard on the north bank that the ferrywright had taken for him out of a bench order and had entered in a book, and he lay on his back for two hours and did the arithmetic he had been not doing for four days, out loud, because a man who does it in his head has already decided what it comes to.

"Nineteen days here," he said, to a ceiling. "Eighteen days of work and one night on a bridge. And the ninth of Greenmonth to the sixteenth in the north, six days, and I do not know what that is and the saying in my country allows three each way and last time I was six days inside and it took nine out of the calendar and I could not make that add up then either, and I have written it in a notebook and it does not add up, and I am going to have to say that out loud in a room in a fen and be told I am wrong by a woman with a press, and she will be right, because she is always right about that office and I am never right about it.

"And a third fever when I come back, because it is three days and it was three days the last time and it is not going to be fewer for being a worse man.

"And one verse of my mother's river song that I still cannot land on its note, and I have got ten days to try it on the way back and I have had nine weeks and it is not coming.

"And a market on the ninth with no road warden in it, and a clerk in a provincial town putting *absent* in a book, and six marks on the guild roll at a shilling a day, and a trust in abeyance for a year so the crossing has no owner and the yard has no work on it, and my brother's four men on a stone contract a factor may or may not keep.

"And a bench of nine in a guild hall four days off that has entered *not here* as a possible line in a book, and a woman at a ferry who will write it."

He turned over.

"And the only seat I have is a block of the fourth course with two pieces of Steppe glass in it, and the man who set it wrote me a page saying how it will fail and would not charge me for the page, and I have read it twice a day for nineteen days, and what the page says at the fourth line is that a seat in cut stone will carry a certain number of times and then it will not, and he would not tell me the number, and I have been carrying the number about this city for nine days and I have not told anybody, and I am telling a bench of nine now because I have run out of ways of not telling people and I am tired of being the only one in a room with a number in him."

Then he slept for three hours, and got up, and was on the fourth camp at the fourth hour, because they were there, and four men had set out eight hundred setts by the time the light came and the ferrywright was at the end of the sixth bay with a line and did not look up, and the crossing was up the river for four hundred yards, and the market under the second arch was doing the same trade it had done every morning for eleven years, and there was nothing in the air at all.
