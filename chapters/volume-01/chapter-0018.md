# Chapter 0018

Five days after the renewal, on the evening of the seventh of Greenmonth, a boy handed Aren Kest a piece of paper with an hour on it and a direction on it, and the paper was the reason he went up the Old Toll road at the ninth hour instead of going to bed.

It was folded in four and soft at the creases, and it had been written on the back of a cooper's bill, and it said:

*Bell, like the old one. Seventh, between the eleventh hour and midday, the dead calm day. Off the far bank, off the bridge itself, not off the water. Three seconds, one note, bad, cracked. Nobody on the road. Two on the Sedge deck (the week's cartage man, and the other one on the hoppers). Nobody out by the storehouse. Wind: none. Cloud: none. Sun: on the water.* — and then, on the next line, in the same hand, because a boy of thirteen has been told to write down the hour and has done so and has then been unable to resist a witness: *Went to the top of the tower after. Looked down the old road. Nothing. Could see the mill chimney and the weir and the storehouse door and nobody.*

The tower had a light on a pivot and a line down the old raised road as far as the weir, which was the boy's grandfather's arrangement before it was the boy's and his father's, and the tower was the only building in Merefen from which a man could see the Old Toll crossing and the storehouse by the fish weir in the same look, and both in a frame.

That was in the boy's favour. It was also, as far as Aren Kest could see standing in the road with the paper in his hand, the only thing in the report that made it worth walking up to look at.

A bell had been heard in Merefen in daylight on the seventeenth of Mudmonth, and that report was a much better-founded one than this. Four ferrymen had rung the old crack off the slip for a carter's man who went under off the reed beds, eleven people heard it from the slab, and nobody had any doubt whatever about what it was. The difference was that on the seventeenth the bell was eleven feet above the moss on a bridge in the open air with four men standing at the wheel, and the report of the seventh said a bell had been heard where the bell had been, at a time of day when a man crossing the road would be visible for a quarter mile, and the bell itself was four hundred yards off in a storehouse by a weir, under a seal with a hand laid flat on a book, in a building whose door had been shut since the nineteenth of Mudmonth and which was, so far as anybody in Merefen knew, unopened.

He walked the mile up the road because walking it was the errand and the errand was the point.

---

The light was going off the fen by the time he got there, and it did that in Greenmonth in a quarter of an hour, from a long grey to a blue to a black you could read a chalk line by, and he had it before he got to the crossing.

The seal on the post had been rained on for a fortnight and had not run. It was the size of a crown piece and it was the same press as the one on the stub in a folder in a room over a salt merchant, and he had been within a yard of it on the first and on the thirtieth and he was not going to be the man who put his thumb on it on the seventh of Greenmonth. He looked at it instead. Wax that has been rained on for a fortnight has a bloom on it, a very fine grey mist, and there was a bloom on this one, and it was even, and there was a chip in the bottom edge where the seal had been pressed crooked, and the chip had a black line in it where a fly had got into the wax before it set.

Two flies. He wrote that down because he was going to write down everything, and a man who records a fly on a seal has told you what his standard of evidence is.

Then he got down onto the sill.

The cut stone, laid by a man who knew the trade. The third and fourth lengths from the abutment repacked in the autumn and again in the week before the storm, three courses of setts, a lime bed, his own mark chalked in the bed of it where the flood would keep the record. The cross in the middle of it, two lines crossing, the size of two hands, pale and dry in a bridge that was streaming.

He got his hand flat on it and waited, and this time he did it properly, which meant he did not simply wait. He did it the way he would do it to a span, which is: name the place, name the thing, name the terms, and then pay attention to what comes back.

He named Bellweather-Living. He named the arch: five arches, the middle one the great one, a hundred and forty feet between the piers, a washing line over the crown every morning since Thawmonth. He named the bench on the floor with the stone under the water. He named the third bell, and the boy of seventeen, and the full minute he had been given. And he waited, with his hand on a cut stone sill in a fen town, to be told what that place was doing.

Nothing came.

Not a warmth, which he had got on the twenty-seventh. Not a fold in the air, which he had got in a storm. Not a word. There was a cross of dry stone under his palm and a hand in the road and a black road in the hand and the river going past underneath at the speed it went, and a wind coming up the old causeway from the south-west that smelled of the fen and of somebody's fire, and after a while his hand got cold, which was all that happened.

He stayed on the stone for the length of twenty breaths, which was longer than he had stayed the first time and much longer than he had stayed on the first of Greenmonth, and then he took his hand off, and the mark in his palm had not changed, and there was nothing in it that had been there before and nothing in it that had gone out.

He tried the cradle iron.

The cradle was iron, welded to the stone, rusted to a colour that was nearly the stone's. He put two fingers on it and held them there, and the iron was cold in the ordinary way that iron is cold, and the iron was the thing the bell had hung on and the iron was under the same words that the bell was, in a Crown paper, held in charge of a keeper of the Bellweather hinge, and there was no more in it than that. If a thing is named on a piece of paper, the paper does not become the thing. He had known that since the fourth of Greenmonth, when a magistrate had said it, and it had taken him three days to be able to use the knowledge.

So he went and found something he could work with.

There was a scaffold against the downstream side of the crossing, put up in Mudmonth by the guild and never taken down, holding a plank and a ladder and a windlass nobody had used since the flood, and one of its uprights had opened a shake at the ground and was standing on the foot of a chisel somebody had used for something else. He put his hand on the upright and made a promise the width of the shake and the length of the timber, which is the smallest working there is and one he could have done at twelve, and he did it into the wood, and the wood stopped being heard, which is the only thing a Bind ever buys. Then he let go of it and put his thumb against the shoulder of the timber and pushed, the way he would push any bad upright he had been given, and the upright stood, and he went along the other three and pushed them and two of them walked out of true by more than an inch, which was exactly what they would have done and was why the scaffold was up.

It worked. It was a real working and it did what it had always done, and it had cost him nothing at all to find out, which was always the part that frightened him more than the other part. It was the third of the four parts, learned in eleven years the way a man learns a knot in the dark, and it had been available to him on a wet evening on a village bridge, at any hour, without a paper, without a witness, and without anybody's permission — which was, he thought, sitting back down on the sill with his hand going cold again, the whole of the difference between the two things. There was no arguing with it and there was no way to improve on it by wanting harder.

Then he took his hand off the upright and put it back on the sill.

Nothing. The same nothing, at the same rate, in the same weather, with the same hand.

He stood in the middle of an old bridge in a fen town and worked out, with the plainness a man gets when a thing has stopped being a mystery and become a measurement, that both of those were the same hand and that the difference between them was not in him.

Oathcraft was a promise made to an anchor. That was all it was; he had known it since he was thirteen in a worse form and had learned it properly at thirty-one on a ferry slip four hundred feet over a river. The anchor is the thing the promise is made to, and the promise has to be about that thing, and the thing has to be there. He could promise a post to stand up because the post was standing up and he wanted it to keep standing up, and the wood was in the room and it heard him. He could not promise a crossing to a stone sill in a fen, because the thing on the other side of the promise was not the sill and had never been the sill; the sill was a door, and the door had been used once, and the thing behind it was four hundred miles away and nine hundred feet below, and a man in Merefen had no standing in a room he was not in, and there was nothing he could say on a wet stone in the dark that would be heard in a hall over an arch.

The road was open. That was the phrase, and it was true, and it had been true for eleven days and would be true on the ninth of Greenmonth and on every night after that, and it was a phrase about a *hand* and not about a *journey*, and he had not known the difference until the seventh of Greenmonth with his hand on a dry cross in a stone.

A road is not a door. A road is a thing that can be walked, and a man can be in the road and in a room four hundred miles away, and the distance between those two facts was the whole of his position and it was going to be the whole of his position for a very long time.

He got his notebook out and wrote it, sitting on the parapet with his boots hanging over the drop into the shadow under the third arch.

*Greenmonth, the seventh, at the ninth hour, the Old Toll sill. Nothing, and I waited twenty breaths and I am recording that I waited twenty breaths, because a man who records the length of his own waiting is a man who will not be able to say afterwards that he did not try.*

*The fourth part is a person, or two, or a record that cannot be denied, and a man standing on a sill in Merefen at the ninth hour with a bell in a sealed storehouse four hundred yards off and a Crown's wax on the post has not got one. On the eighteenth I had Wenna's crew and a bar and three arches of noise and a man with a bar is not a witness, and the bench asked me for terms and I had none to give and I gave them my own word instead, which is a thing I have thought about every day since and which is the same mistake twice.*

*Note: the working still works. I put a promise into a scaffold upright this evening and the timber took it and it cost me nothing, and I stood on the same bridge and put the same hand on the same sill and the stone gave me nothing at all, and both of those are the truth and they are the same truth.*

*Note: the boy has the hour. If there is anything to be made of the sound it is the hour and the sight-line and the fact that there is no bell on this reach. There has not been a bell on this reach since the seventeenth of Mudmonth and nobody has rung anything since and the two men on the deck and the tower boy are the only witnesses in this county and one of them is thirteen.*

*Note: I cannot send a word. I have not been able to send a word for eleven days. There is no door in this direction and a path does not write letters, and I have a promise outstanding to a bench of nine men who will not ask me how or when, and they are holding an arch on the strength of a word from a stranger, and the word I gave them is the only thing I have ever put in a letter that I did not also pay for.*

He read it back. The last line was not a tradesman's line and he let it stand, because a man who edits his own notebook to be tidier has started writing it for somebody else.

---

Then he got up off the parapet and did the other thing, which was the thing he had not done since the sixth of Mudmonth.

He sat down on the near abutment, on the cold stone, with his back to the parapet and his boots in the wet, and he counted load points.

It is not a thing anybody can watch a man do. There is no guild rule about it, no charter, no examiner; it is a habit that a man who has been under bridges for twenty years has in his body, and it is the habit of a man who intends to go under one. You do not decide to do it. You sit down on a bridge in the dark and your hands begin to walk the structure without you, and you count.

The Old Toll crossing is three arches, and it is two generations out of cart use, and it is kept for a bell and then kept for nothing. Nine points on a three-arch deck. He went round them in the dark without moving from the stone, because he has the whole thing in his body and could have done it with a light and would not have wanted a light.

The crown of the near ring, where the cart used to sit over the highest stone and the worst bending is, and it is a right place on a three-arch deck for a crack to open from above. The haunch at the near springing, where the fill sits on the ring and where a wall that has been moved has taken a debt out of the ring. The first pier, river side and land side, at the waterline, and the same pier at the coping, because a pier's two interesting heights are the two ends of the thing that fails. The far springing, the far crown, the far abutment, and the corner of the Old Toll approach that everybody in this town has walked past for a hundred years and where the ground has been going out since the spring.

He went round the nine of them and stopped at each one and asked the same question, which is not *is it sound*, and never has been, because a man cannot hear soundness in the dark. It is: *where does the weight of this go, and what is this point being asked to do that it might not want to do.* And on the sixth of Mudmonth, a month ago, he had done the same nine and got the same nine answers, and on the thirtieth of Mudmonth the third pier of the Sedge deck had gone the same argument with him in the rain.

Two bridges. Two sets of nine. Two months of his working life in a fen town that has never once been counted, and about a pound of chalk between them, and a boy on a deck at the ninth hour and a man on an abutment in the dark.

The town had not asked him to do that. That was the difference between the two men his age whom he had watched on that bridge, and one of them had died in Hearthmonth of a fever, and the man had been a good one.

He got up. The light had gone entirely. The whole of the fen was one sound, the sound of the water going over the Sable Reach four bays upstream, and the Old Toll crossing under him was a shape with a bell-shaped hole in it that nothing was using.

---

There were two places a thing like that could go and he had spent the walk down deciding, which is that it was not a thing he could sit on.

The watch would take it in a morning. That was not speculation. A Crown seal on a storehouse door in this county, and a bell heard where the bell is not, and a road warden who had been away nine days and had said so in front of four hundred people — Captain Rell had a paper and a duty and a bell in a storehouse, and Rell believed the bell carried the fever, and a sound off the Old Toll bridge in a dead calm was exactly the kind of thing a frightened officer with a lawful custody and a belief would want to be told about. It would become a reason. There is no other word for what a sound in daylight is, to a man who has been holding a piece of metal for a fortnight because he thinks it is a disease, and it would be a reason with a witness in it, and the witness would be two men on a deck and a boy of thirteen, and Rell would not need to be a villain to use it. He would need only to be right about the fever and wrong about the bell, which is a combination that is very easy to get, because one half of it is true.

The Archive would make it a document. The keeper of the Bellweather Claims Archive could not publish a sound; that was not in the office's gift and Mara Vey would say so in the first minute. What she could do was take an hour, and a direction, and a sight-line from a tower, and enter it as a reported observation with the two witnesses' names on it and the fact that the bell is under seal in a storehouse four hundred yards off written in beside it, and the document would be worth nothing whatever to any court in this kingdom for the next five years, and it would be unarguable, and there is no way on this earth to argue a document. She would file it, and the filing would be dated, and the date would be a thing that existed whether he ever came back or not.

And that was the whole of the choice, and it was not a hard one, and he made it at the bottom of Weir Street with his feet wet.

The second one was better by every measure he could put a name to, and the second one cost him the only thing he had, which was the certainty that the sound was a boy's ears and a dead calm and a chimney in the wind. If he filed it, then in a year somebody could put the hour, and the sight-line, and the absence of a bell, and the boy, side by side, and make something of it. And the reason it was worth filing was the reason Mara Vey had made him sign an undated promise in a fair hand in the first week of Mudmonth: because a thing that is written down is a thing that has stopped being the only copy of itself. There is one of him. There is one of that boy's morning, and if the boy is asked again in a year he will remember it differently, because a boy of thirteen remembers things the way the ground remembers a flood, in shapes.

He got the notebook out at the bottom of Weir Street, in the dark, in the wet, and stood there a while.

Then he wrote the address on the wrapper, which was the upper rooms over the salt merchant on the west side, a stair with a window on the half-landing, and the smell of it got into his coat and stayed for days.

A year ago he would have gone to the watch. A year ago — and it was a month ago, and that was the part that was hard — a year ago he had been a man who solved things and paid for them himself, and this month he had learned that a thing paid for by one man is a thing that stops when he stops, and that the only thing standing between a report about a bell in a fen town and the inside of a magistrate's finding was a woman with a day-book and a press with a bar on it, and that she would take it, and that she would not thank him for it, and that she was owed a decision rather than a favour.

He walked up Weir Street to his own door and did not go in, and stood in the dark in the street for a while, thinking about a bench of nine men in a hall with a stone in the floor, and about a promise he had made in a room with a witness in it that was not a person, and about the distance between a hand and a journey, and about the two letters the provincial office had sent that said *held in the keeper's charge* to a keeper who did not exist, and about how a Crown can put a bell anywhere in Neyra and have no office in Neyra be able to say who told it to go.

Tomorrow, in a room over a salt merchant, there was going to be a document with an hour in it.

In two days he would be ninety feet over a river four hundred miles from here, standing on the crown of an arch in a city that had been running its own business before he ever came back and would be running it on the morning of the ninth whatever he did under it — if he had a door, and a glass sill, and four marks, and a witness.

He did not have any of the four.

He went up the stairs and slept with his back to the wall, and the moulding was damp and there was a draught that could carry a candle flame sideways, and he was two shillings a month behind and eleven days in arrears, and the river went over four bays in the dark, and nothing at all in Merefen answered him, and that was the correct condition of things and it was the most expensive thing he had ever been taught.
