# Chapter 0009

Tovan Kest had been in the river for four hours by the time the market opened, and his feet were still on the third pier, and the scour hole under the nose of it was the size of a washtub and had been the size of a bread loaf on the sixth of Mudmonth.

Two oak cribs, six feet, scribed to the batter, and he had them both in and packed with a spoon, and the spoon work was the part nobody in Merefen could have done for him, because the spoon is a mason's job and his brother was the mason's mind in the family and he was only the man with the hands.

So he did the mason's mind part too, slowly, alone, and got it wrong twice.

The wrong was the second crib. He set it plumb and packed it hard, and hard packing on a pier that is being scoured is how you put a wedge under a stone you meant to lift, and at the fourth hour of the morning the river found the joint he had made proud and put six inches of water through it in a space of time that Tovan would think about for the rest of his life. He got the second crib out and re-cut it and set it loose, with a corbel under it and a splayed set packing, and it held, and the six hours of that were the six most expensive hours of his working life, and he could not bill any of them.

That was the arithmetic of the twenty-seventh, and there was more of it.

Nine days. Nine days of a scour repair on a bridge that wanted to be a fortnight's work, done by the brother who had left on the eighteenth with a bar and no word, and Tovan had done it in three and a half days on a bridge that was shut, with a crew of four because two of the four were journeymen he could only pay on Fridays, and the two Fridays that fell in those nine days were Fridays he did not have.

He had a tally chalked on the back of his left hand, which is what a bridge carpenter does when he has run out of room, and it read: oak, one mark four; lime, one shilling six; stone and cartage, two marks; nails, eight pence; and under that, in smaller figures, the days the crew had worked and the days he had not been paid for, which was eleven.

He could carry four marks. He had four marks. The guild's bond was six, and the clerk had been twice, and on the second visit he had said the thing that a clerk says when he has been told to say it: *Mr Kest, the matter is against your brother's surety, and your brother's surety is a chest, and the chest is worth three and a half, and I have written three and a half, and a man who signs six and has three and a half is a man who has signed on faith, and faith is not a currency we take.*

"You'll get the six," Tovan had said.

"You'll get the six or the chest, and the chest will be sold to make the six, and the tools in it are worth more than the chest that holds them, so think about that before you think about the six."

That was on the twenty-eighth. After that the clerk stopped coming, and Tovan did not know why, and did not ask, because asking would have meant knowing, and knowing would have meant going into the room at the top of the stairs of the cooper's house to find out, and Tovan had a great many things to do before he could afford to do that.

His clients came at the seventh hour, all three of them at once, because men who are owed an answer will organise.

The toll-holder's man. The miller's man, wet to the thigh again, exactly as he had been on the sixth, which meant the state of the world had not improved for either of them. And a factor for a stone merchant from the western channel who had eleven tons of paving setts standing on a bank two miles up because a bridge was in the way, and who was the only one of the three who was not angry and was therefore the most dangerous.

The toll-holder's man wanted a date for carts.

"The deck's open for light loads this afternoon," Tovan said. "Thursday for a cart with glass in it. The ninth for anything."

"You said the ninth on the sixth."

"I said the ninth on the sixth and I was wrong on the sixth, and the ninth is right today, and if you want a date you can have the ninth and if you want a promise you can go and ask a priest."

The miller's man said his pass was still not worth half.

"Buy a boat, then. I've been in the water four hours. I'm not running the ferry."

The factor said, pleasantly, that the factor's eleven tons had already lost a fortnight, and that a fortnight lost on the western channel in Mudmonth cost him a quarter of his year's margin, and that he would like a date, and that he would take it from a man who had signed something.

"I have signed things," Tovan said.

"You have," said the factor. "That is what worries me. Kest, I am not a hard man, I am a man with stone in a field. Who is doing this work?"

"Me."

"You have been doing it for three and a half days and there are four men in your yard and one of them is under sixty and one of them has a bad shoulder. Who else is doing it?"

Tovan looked at him.

"Get your stone off the bank by Friday," he said, "and if you ask me a fourth time in front of my crew I'll put your eleven tons where the river can have them."

That was the shortest available version of the truth and it was not the truth. The truth was that his brother had gone off a bridge on the night of the storm with a bar over his shoulder and had not come back, and that the town had worked that out by the twenty-first and had decided, in the way towns do, what it meant.

What it meant, as far as Tovan had been able to establish, was that the Kest brothers are the sort who are not told. Not the sort who are in debt. Not the sort who are a disgrace. The sort who are not told. The miller's man had said it to a carter on the wharf road and the carter had said it to a woman selling oatcakes and by the twenty-fourth there were people in Merefen who said *you'd have to ask Tovan Kest* and meant it kindly, and Tovan had stood in the middle of Weir Street and heard it three times in a day and had not been able to make himself answer any of them.

Which was the thing his brother had done to him, and had done at seventeen, in Greenmonth, eleven years ago, without meaning to.

He went up at the ninth hour.

The room was two shillings a month above a cooper and it had a draught in it that came under the door and went out at the window, and his brother was in it in a bed that was not a bed, on a board under a blanket, with the cooper's wife sitting on the stool with a bowl of water and a cloth and the air of a woman who has been sitting with sick people for forty years and does not intend to be impressed by it.

Tovan had not been in the room in four days. He had been told, twice a day, by a boy, that the fever was on him and not in him, which is a thing people say to make themselves feel better and which is also, in this case, exactly right.

He put his hand on his brother's ankle.

"You look like something the river gave back," he said.

"You should see the other one."

"I've seen the other one. I've been standing in the water next to him for nine days." He moved the stool out of the way with his knee so he could sit, and did not sit, and then sat. "Say the thing. I know you've been saying it to the cooper's wife, and she's a decent woman and she'll keep it, but she isn't going to write it in a book, and if the watch has a book — and the watch has a book, because I was in town this morning and there were four of them on the Old Toll and a sergeant with a ledger and the Sergeant's face was the face of a man who has written something he cannot stop writing — then the book is going to want the second version."

"Eleven fingers," Aren said.

"I heard that off the fish-weir men. Eleven fingers and a book on a post that said seven and a quarter. That is the entire of your revelation, is it. A boatman was right about a river."

"He was right about a river and a surveyor was right about a book and they were both right and they were shouting at each other, and I have been a man in Merefen for eleven years telling that boatman he could not read water, and the man was in the right, and it cost him nothing, and it will cost somebody something." His eyes were open and not entirely in the room. "Tovan. The arch is four hundred feet and I stood on it. There is a stone trough in a floor with a pledge cut into it eleven years old, and the pledge is that no order closes that crossing while hands remain on it, and the city kept it under water for eleven years so that nobody would read it in a hurry, and it was read at a renewal in Longlight by men who had never met the writ, because it is a promise and a promise is renewed by somebody standing on it."

"That'll be the fever."

"It's not the fever. It's not fever, it's not a bridge, it's a bench of men and a writ and a third bell and a boy of seventeen with a bar in his hand who was told he had a full minute." He stopped to breathe, and it took a while, and the cloth came off his face and his forehead was grey. "The fever was real. There was a fever in the low quarter, in Mudmonth, and the woman on the gallery said two hundred and eleven, and they have a ledger for it themselves, and I have spent eleven years being the man who lost a city to a fever, and there was a fever, and it did not take the city. It was a real thing. The crown took a real thing and used it for a false one, and that is worse, brother, that is a great deal worse, because it means the fever was not a lie anybody invented to cover a murder. It was a murder with a true coat on."

Tovan sat with his hands on his knees.

"Then what do you want," he said. "Say it plainly, because I am going to the yard in an hour and I would rather hear it once."

"I want the other half of the writ."

"You've got the stub."

"The stub's a seal and a date. Every office in this kingdom has a seal and a date. The other half has a name on it, and the register with the name on it is in the hands of the toll houses in a city four hundred feet over a river, and the toll houses would give it to me for a trade road in about a year, and I have told them so, and I cannot go back and get it because there is no road."

"Then there's your problem," Tovan said. "You can have it. It's yours, it's got your hand on it, and it's nine days' fever and a roadmark and a song you can't sing, and I've got a scour hole and four men and eleven days' wages, and if you want me to hold any of it I'll not, and I want that said in this room while you're able to hear it, because you'll ask me again in a week when you've got some colour back and you'll have a better reason and the reason will be worse."

He got up. The chair went over. It was the only thing in the room loud enough to matter.

"You were the one they asked to hold it," Tovan said. "I want to say the rest of it, and then I want to do the pier, and then I do not want to talk about it again until one of us asks.

"I was ten. You went in Greenmonth with the Guard wagon and a new coat and I was ten and I did not have a trade yet, and I wrote to you in Harvestmonth, on the paper the cooper gave me for doing up his front door. That's the letter about the roof of the cooper's and whether the apple tree would come. I put it in with a man going east.

"I was eleven, and I got no word back, not one, for a year. I was twelve, and I wrote again, and I got no word back from you for two years, and then I stopped, and then I was your brother in a yard with a dead man's tools and a living man's debts. I did the roof myself over three summers when I was fourteen. It is still the best roof on Weir Street and I have never once said that to you, and I am saying it now because I am the one holding this yard and you are the one in the bed, and there is nobody else in this room."

He was not shouting. He had stopped talking, which is worse, and the room got very quiet.

"And you did not write because you could not. I worked that out years ago. You don't have to have it explained, and if you try I will put you back in the bed yourself. It isn't that. It is that you decided, somewhere between the roof and the Guard, that the way to be somebody was to be the one holding it, and everybody who would have held something with you got instead a reason why not to be in the room.

"Me. The ferryman on the Sable whose sign you carried for a loaf and a halfpenny. The watch at the north gate. The old man who reads the psalter at the fish weir chapel. Anybody."

He picked the chair up and set it down again, square, the way you set a tool down.

"So here's what I'll do, and you don't get to argue with a fever.

"I'll mend your bridge. That's my trade and I don't want a medal for it. Mudmonth, Greenmonth, I'll do it well, and I'll take my own pay for it and not yours.

"And I will not hold your road. I don't know what it is. I don't want to. I am not going to stand in it with you and hold the span while you work, and when it goes — brother, it is going to go somewhere, on a bridge, sooner or later, that is what bridges do — I am not going to be the man who is standing on it when it does. And that's not about the eighteenth of Mudmonth, so don't you dare turn it into that, sir, and don't you look at me like that either, because you can be very tired and very sick in the same chair and you still get to be told a thing straight.

"And I will not keep it. If a watch captain's got a ledger with the second version in it, and if a woman from the salt loft has been down asking to read a watch ledger — and she has, and she did it on the twenty-ninth, and the sergeant sent her away, and she came back on the thirtieth with a magistrate's paper, which I did not know until the cooper told me this morning, and the cooper told me because the cooper is a man who cannot keep anything including a thing you would want him to keep — then in about a week there is going to be a room with three people in it and a piece of paper in the middle, and the paper is going to be in a filing system in Merefen, and it is going to be read by men with titles, and it is going to be the beginning of a thing that will take eleven years."

He stopped. His jaw was working.

"You are not that," he said. "You are a road warden in a fen town with a fever. That's all you get to be in my yard. And if I hear that name in my yard again, out of your mouth, when you are well, I will take you by the coat and put you over Weir Street, and I will do it in front of people."

He sat down on the stool with his hands hanging between his knees and stayed there, and then he got up again, and put the stool back where the cooper's wife keeps it, because he had put it in the wrong place and she would notice.

Aren Kest lay on a board under a blanket for a while and did not say anything at all, and Tovan Kest, who had said the truest thing he had said in eleven years to the only man who had ever heard him say one, did not get an answer, and there was no more of the day left to get one in.

The cooper's woman had come to the door with the bowl.

"He's taken a physician's fee already," she said. "I sent for the old man from the fish weir at the second hour and he has charged a shilling and eight pence and he will want most of it now, because he is that sort, and I have told him the man hasn't got it and he said send it anyway, so I have taken it out of the float and you can pay me on Friday."

"A shilling and eight pence."

"Against a chest worth three and a half, in a town, on Weir Street." She put the bowl down. "He'll be able to have the room, and water, and I will sit up the first night, and there is nobody else to sit up the second, and I am not going to, so you will."

"Second night is the second night."

"He'll be on his feet by the second night."

"He'll be on his feet by the thirtieth," said Tovan, "if he's any use, and if he isn't, then the fever decides and not me, and I have a scour hole." He put his hand on his brother's shoulder, once, hard, and left.

At the Old Toll crossing, on his way to the pier, he came in off the road to look at the cradle, and the cradle was empty, and there was a clean rectangle in the moss, and there was a paper nailed to the post with a grey wax seal on it that he did not touch and did not read, and a girl of about fifteen was sitting on the bottom step with a basket between her boots, watching anybody who came along, and she asked him, before he had got two words out, whether he was here about the bell.

"No," said Tovan.

"Everybody's here about the bell," said the girl.

He went down to the water.

That night he paid the two journeymen what he had, in coin, on the rail, and told the other two to come back on the first and to tell anybody who asked that the work was going on and the man holding it was Tovan Kest and would be there in the morning and for as long as there was a bridge to hold, which is a thing that is true, and which he had decided, on the deck of a boat on the third of the month, would be the whole of his life.

He did not write to anybody. He did not go up the stairs again. And when his brother woke at the second hour of the twenty-ninth and said, to a dark room, the words *it is not a city the crown put there, brother, it is a city that refused* — and then said, clearly, *will you say it if I say it* — Tovan Kest said no, and meant the whole of it, and lay in the dark in his own house and listened to his brother breathe, and was glad in a way he would not have admitted to anybody that the answer had come to him at four in the morning in a room where nobody could hear it and that it had not cost him anything yet.
