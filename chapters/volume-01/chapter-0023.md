# Chapter 0023

There is a thing that happens to a man in the fourth hour of his sixth morning of work in a strange city, and it is not homesickness and it is not exhaustion, and it took Aren Kest eleven days in Bellweather to work out that it had happened to him and that he had spent eleven years arranging his whole life so that it could not.

It is relief.

The fourth hour of the sixth morning was cold and blue and he was on a staging over the second pier of the great arch of Bellweather with a bar in one hand, and a man called the second of the second pier standing in the boat below with his hands in his pockets and his head back, and the two of them had been arguing for an hour about mortar, and the man had won, and Aren Kest was standing on a staging feeling better than he had felt since the twenty-seventh of Mudmonth in his own country, which was three weeks and two days ago and had cost a bell and a market and a brother, and it was because he was being paid, and wrong, in public, on a job, by people who were better than him at it.

---

The bench had put him on the pier because the bench was short of hands, and the bench is not a body of heroes, it is a body of tired men, and it had lost the north trade and had nobody to send north and had a stranger standing in the hall with a note about a bell. It was a stonework repair and it was not interesting, and that was the point of it, and by the fourth day he understood the whole of what the bench had actually done to him: it had given a man with a case a job.

The outer ring of the second pier from the north was eight inches of open joint, river side, low down, at about the height of a man's chest on a good day. It opened in the cold and shut in the warmth. It had been measured twice a year since the year the city was written out of the world, by two men and a clerk, and the measurements were in a book in the guild hall in a hand that had not changed in eleven years, and Aren read the whole eleven years of them in one sitting on the third morning, and could have told anybody in two countries what the pier was doing.

The ring stone was sound. That was the thing nobody could get past. It was a single block, and it was not cracked, and it was not spalled, and it was the finest piece of masonry in four hundred feet of the crossing, and the trade that cut voussoirs for that arch had cut this one and had signed for them by number, out of a quarry nine days' barge north, and the setting was so good that a mason would sign it.

The stone was not the fault. Behind it the mortar had been raked out from the inside — not washed out from the river side, which would have been honest, but raked from within, by water coming up under the arch and out through the spandrel, and he found that on the second day by putting his arm into the joint as far as it would go and finding a pocket four inches deep with a lip on the far side of it, which is what water does when it has been at work for eleven years on a joint it is not allowed to get at.

"Every winter it opens and every summer it shuts," said the man in the hall. "Every Longlight, in the middle of the year, when the water is lowest, and the clerk writes down what two men with a line and a chalk have found, and they have found the same thing for eleven years."

"Yes."

"And in the warm it shuts."

"It shuts because the stone takes up the heat," said Aren Kest. "Stone does. A foot of it across nine inches of joint will move the width of a hair between a January morning and a July one and it has done that every year since before you were born, and that is most of what you are looking at. But it is not all. There is a hole under this pier that I have not seen the bottom of and that four hundred feet of city is standing on."

"So there are two things," said the man with the mallet.

"There are two things and the second one is under the first one, and the honest repair of the second one begins with the arch out of the water for a season." He put his thumb on the chalk of the eight inches. "A new cutwater on the river side of the second pier. Nine months of this bench's whole capacity, stone and iron and wages, and the tolls on that arch are pledged to the ninth of your grandson, and the man who pledged them knew what he was doing. So you do nothing, and you measure it twice a year, and I am going to say the other half of that sentence because somebody has to, and I am the only man on this crossing who is going to say it in a room, and I am saying it once. *A bench that cannot keep a promise is a worse thing than a bench that never made one.* You have a stone in your floor with a pledge cut in it and it is renewed every Longlight and read under water in the middle of the year so that nobody reads it in a hurry, and that is not a small thing to have got this far with. But the arch is not a promise. The arch is a hole in a river, and a pledge is not a repair, and the day the two are confused is the day somebody in this city finds out what it cost."

The room was quiet.

"Write that down," said the man with the mallet, "and do not soften it, and put it in the book with the measurements, and I will read it at the next sitting and I will not argue with it, and you and I both know what that means, which is that I have moved it off myself and onto a book, and the book is a thing I have to answer for."

---

The fourth camp was the north bank, and it was the first thing he had built in Bellweather-Living and the only thing that was his.

The gate bridge eight hundred feet up the river was four timber bays on four timber bays, exactly like a deck in his own country, and it was sinking on the town side, and nobody could mend it, and the reason nobody could mend it was the reason nothing could be mended: there was no oak on the trestles and there was no money in the city, and there were forty-one toll-houses, and the guild was a bench of nine men who could not spend the guild's money on a gate bridge four hundred yards from the arch without a vote and a fortnight.

So they made setts instead.

A sett is a dressed block of stone for a road surface, and a gate bridge approach wants about three thousand of them to a mile, and a city of four thousand people with a bench of nine and a great many unemployed hands can cut four thousand setts in a season, and what it cannot do is lay them, because laying is a trade with a rule about the camber and about what the water does at the low corner, and the guild had laid its own roads for four generations and had got better at it than he was.

So he built a camp. That was the word they used. It was a hundred and forty paces of the north bank above the slip, out of the wind, with three drying floors, a rubbing floor, a pitching floor, and a store, and it was run by two men of the guild and a woman who had cut setts since she was nine, and it was a camp for making a road surface and not a camp for anything else, and the bench clerk entered it in the hall's book as a works of the guild with a man in it from outside.

"You will be here four months," said the man with the mallet. "Not that you will. I am not asking you for four months. I am telling you that in four months the fourth camp will either be a road or it will not, and that I have put your name against it in a book because you will not be here in four months, and because a thing that is going to stop when a man stops ought not to be entered under his name at all."

"Then it is in the wrong column," said Aren Kest.

"It is in the right column and it is wrong, and I have entered it wrong on purpose, because the alternative is that nobody's name is on it, and a works with nobody's name on it is a bench order and a bench order is the thing that has been running this city for eleven years and it is why there is a hole in the second pier." He looked at the third pier from the staging. "Put the camp under the guild. When you go, the camp goes on with the guild's name on it and two of our men in it, and your four months is four months we would have spent on the fourth camp anyhow, and you have not saved this city a stone and you have not cost it one, and the difference is that a book in the hall says so in a hand that a clerk copied out."

Which meant, and Aren Kest understood it about an hour later, that the bench had just bought itself the argument that it was building a road and not waiting for a foreigner, and had put his name in the wrong column so that a stranger did not have to be paid for it.

---

The correction came on the sixth morning and it came from the second of the second pier, who had not given his name in five days, because in a yard nobody asks a man what he is called when he has been on the same staging since the second day.

The joint at the outer ring was raked open four inches deep and there was a wet weather coming, and Aren's whole instinct said grout. Lime and sharp sand, worked stiff, packed into the pocket from the river side, and a stone that tight, and a joint that would be the tightest thing on the crossing, and a man could put a thumb in it in a January and be sure of it.

"Grout's for a wall," said the second of the second pier. "This is a ring."

"It is a compression joint in a ring of voussoirs, which is exactly—"

"It's a ring," said the man, "and the ring's moved four lines in eleven years and you can go and look at the chalk marks the clerk takes, and the last four were the same four lines, so it isn't going to stop." He spat over the side. "You grout that tight you're going to make a ring of stone with a joint in it that's harder than the joint was. And a joint that's harder than the joint is a crack somewhere else, and the somewhere else is going to be in the spandrel, and you can't get at the spandrel, and a man who can't get at it doesn't know what's happened in it for nine years."

"Then what do you do with it."

"You put it in soft. Clay and a little lime, worked to the back of a spoon, and you leave it a whole inch and a half and you let the stone do what the stone does." He got out of the boat and up onto the staging, which was not easy, and stood over the joint for a while. "It's the wrong trade. I know it's the wrong trade. My master worked that joint in 'twas a different man, because it was a different water and a different stone and a different river, and it stood nine years and then a flood came up under the pier and took it out from behind in one night. So I know what your trade does and I know what mine does and I know which of us is going to be sitting on this pier when somebody wants to know."

"You have never seen a masonry ring move four lines in a decade and still be sound."

"No," said the man. "I have seen forty feet of it move nine inches and be sound, and I have seen four feet of it not move and be dead, and I have never once known which was which until afterwards, and that is my whole education and it took me thirty years and it cost me a stone of somebody else's I could not replace."

Aren Kest looked at the open joint for a long time, with a bar in his hand and a bar he was not using.

"Say it again," he said. "All of it. Slowly, and I will write it down, and I will write it exactly and I will not put my name in a wrong column about it."

"You want the fact of it or the reason."

"Both. And then I am going to do what you tell me, and I am going to do it your way, and I am going to be honest with you about the fact that I do not agree with it, and I am going to be honest with you about why, and you are going to tell me if I am right to say the reasons, and if I am not going to write them down at all."

The man laughed at that — one syllable, without malice — and then, for the first time in six days, told him his name, and refused to say it back to him twice.

They argued about it for two hours. The man was wrong about the spandrel, because water in a spandrel is not a crack and cannot be and a mason knows the difference; Aren was wrong about the grout, and knew he was wrong about it before the second hour was out, and could not say so for another hour because his hands did not know it yet.

Then they packed the joint in clay and a little lime to the width of a thumb, and left it an inch and three-quarters, and Aren's own hand did the last four feet of it, badly, and then better.

That was the sixth morning, and it was the best day he had had in eleven years, and he understood it that evening.

Not because of the joint. Because it was the only hour in eleven years in which he had stood in a room with a man who was better than he was at a thing he had come in to be told about, and the man had been better at it, and neither of them had once pretended otherwise, and both of them had got up on the staging in the cold at the fourth hour for it, and one of them had a hole in his pier and the other one had a bridge four days north that was not his and a bell in a storehouse he could not touch and eleven years of a story he could not get out from under, and they were talking about mortar, and it was the first honest hour he had had since he was twenty-one years old and a man at a table had told him, without any particular cruelty, that a bridge was a thing you could hand over once and pick up again in a year.

He had never once been on a job in his life with anybody who had been better at it than him. Every room he had stood in since the Sundering had been a room in which he was the most competent man present, and he had been twenty-one and then thirty-two, and the shape of the sentence had never changed and he had never once looked inside it, because looking inside it is a thing you do alone and he had not been alone since he came back.

---

Hushwater Reach is a mile and a half downriver on a channel that goes under at the spring tides, and the bench sent him there on the ninth morning with four men and a load of setts, because the ferry's approach at the low end had washed out in the winter and the ferry cooperative could not pay for it and the guild could not lose four men and a load of stone for a fortnight.

The crossing there is a ferry, and it is a working crossing, and the cooperative runs it on nine hands, and it is not on the arch and it is not the guild's and the guild has no authority over it and never has.

What he was shown, because he asked, and because a man who has been corrected by a pier's second man can be trusted to ask for a book, was the second page.

Hushwater Reach. The crossings. And the hearths on each side of the water.

There were eleven crossings listed on that page against nine names, because the tenth name was the woman keeping it and the eleventh was her mother, who does not cross on a working day any more, and the clerk had written a note about that in the ordinary way a clerk writes a note and it was the most useful sentence on the page.

And what was not on it, again, was any number of souls. Ferry hands and hearths, on each side of the water, because a cooperative is a set of boats and a set of chimneys and not a parish. Three assessors and a man from the abbey had put the best figure anybody could put to the hamlet at near sixty some years before, and every one of them had said it as though apologising for the imprecision, and the woman keeping the book had been there at that argument and had said the thing that has been said in this hall: *the imprecision is in the counting and not in the hamlet.*

She was about fifty, and she had a thumb wet with ink, and she held the book in her apron while she talked and did not once offer it.

"I know what you want," she said.

"I want it copied."

"That is not what I want and you know it is not." She shut it, which was a thing a person does to a book. "I will tell you why, and I will tell you the true reason and not the one that sounds noble, and you can write the true one down if you have a book like mine, which you do not appear to have.

"Eleven years ago a man came up this channel with a paper and a clerk and told us the crossing was to be severed, and read it out, and there was a woman on the boat who was not a guild woman and had been told to read it and to say nothing else, and the same woman wrote the names down afterwards while our old keeper was doing the other thing, which is arguing with the men on the deck. And that list is not in this book, because this book is a working book, and a working book goes in a channel boat and gets wet and gets left in the rain, and the man who came up said he would send a clerk and take a copy and send it back within a month." She turned the book over in her apron. "He did not send a clerk. Nobody has ever sent a clerk. And that is not the reason."

"Then what is."

"Copy," said the woman. "A copy is a second thing that somebody can lose, and the two of them do not come apart again, and after eleven years one of them is going to be in a book in a court in a country that has a finding in it saying this channel was never there, and the other one is going to be in my apron, and there is going to be an argument between them, and the argument will be about my hamlet and not about him." She said *him* without any weight at all. "So no. I will show you the page. I will read you the names. And I will not let a second man have a copy of it, and if you ask me why once more I shall say it again in a worse way."

He did not ask again.

What he did instead, on the boat going back up, with a load of setts and four men and a low river, was to write down everything she had said in the order she had said it, without improving one word of it, in the flat hand a man uses when he is copying a statement into a record.

And then, in the notebook, four lines for himself.

*She did not refuse me because the book is a secret. She refused because a copy is a second thing that somebody can lose.*

*In my country a woman keeps a press with a bar on it and a rule on the wall, and the rule is that this office keeps one set of records outside the Crown's custody, and she fought a man about it and won, and I have never once in eleven years asked a single question about why she does it.*

*I have been asking the wrong question since the fourth of Mudmonth. I have been asking a city to give me a thing. I have not once asked what it would cost her to have given it to me.*

*And the answer has been sitting in a boat on a channel for eleven years, and it is the same answer.*

---

He came back up the channel on the tenth morning with the setts laid and the low corner packed right and four men who had stopped calling him master by the seventh, and he was tired in a way that had nothing wrong with it.

The counting house is a hundred and forty yards down the wharf and it was shut when he passed it on the tenth, and it would be open on the eleventh, and he went past it twice in one afternoon without going in, because Alis Merrow had said the second hour of a day that had not been named, and because a man who goes into a counting house on the wrong morning does not get a second morning.

He learned the rule in the end from the woman who kept the ferry book, on the way back up on the tenth morning, and she had got it from a cousin in the north houses, and she told it to him flatly over a pile of setts with a thumb wet with ink, as a thing that is simply the way the counting house is.

"You do not go into a counting house because you want something from it," she said. "You go in because the counting house owes you, and it has been entered in the book that it does, and it has not been discharged. That is the only door. It is not a merit. A man who was never owed anything may stand in it and be answered civilly, and if he is answered civilly twice he is a nuisance, and a nuisance on a wharf in this city is a thing that gets a clerk to shut a shutter on you and the shutter is not the same as a door being shut because the debt is paid."

"So there is no way in for a stranger."

"There is a way in for a creditor," said the woman, "and there is no way in for a man who is owed nothing, and the difference is written down, and the writing down is the whole of the difference between this city and yours."

And then she said, because she had a way of finishing a thing that was to the point and no further:

"You have been here nine days and you have not asked me once whether the north has come back, and every man who comes up this channel asks me that inside a minute, and the ones who do not are either very strange or very busy, and I have decided that you are busy." She shut the book in her apron. "And if you go down there, go at the second hour. And do not knock."

---

Aren Kest stood on the wharf that evening with his hands in his pockets and worked out, for the first time in four days, what he had come for.

There was a bell in a founder's yard on the north bank under a tarred cloth on two trundles, and it had a founder's mark on its shoulder that he had read with his own hands in a scrap yard in Merefen on a wet morning, in a line of men who were laughing, before he ever came up these steps. He had come for it and it was four hundred yards from where he was standing and he had not gone to look at it, in nine days, and he was not going to, because the bench had work and the work was the only thing in this city that was his.

The other half of the writ was a hundred and forty yards down the same wharf, behind a lock whose key was on a string round a clerk's neck, in a book of four hundred leaves kept in order for a hundred and forty years by families who were owed by it.

And what he had that the city did not have was a sheet of good paper in a press over a salt merchant, four days' hard ride north, in a country that had never sent a clerk.

The counting house had been shut when he walked past it and it would be open in the morning. It did not open for a man who was owed nothing, and he was owed nothing, and there was not one thing in nine days he had done that anybody in this city was going to owe him for.
