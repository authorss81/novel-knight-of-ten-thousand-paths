# Chapter 0033

The twenty-eighth of Greenmonth was a Wednesday and the stone was nearly done, and on a Wednesday a yard does the arithmetic of what it is going to be on a Friday, and Tovan Kest did it at the sixth hour with the master's tally on the back of his left hand and four marks and a quarter against a contract and a man's hands.

Nine tons of setts for a factor and two tons on a field. The field was delivered on the twentieth and paid on the same day. The factor's stone was the ninth of the eleven tons and the other two were his own margin, and the cutting was on the last of the nine, and the last of the nine had to be dressed on the trestles on the twenty-eighth and carted on the twenty-ninth and be at the yard at the fourth hour of the thirtieth, and the factor's money was due at the mill on the first Friday after delivery, which was the seventh of Longlight, and the wages came out of the cutting and not out of anything else.

"If it goes on the cart on the twenty-ninth and it is at the mill on the thirtieth," he said, to nobody, "then I am square with him on the seventh of Longlight. And on the seventh of Longlight the wages on this load are a fortnight and four days for four men at the yard rate and a boy at half, which is two marks, thirteen shillings and sixpence, and the factor's money on this load is four marks, and I clear by two and six, and I clear on this load and I have four more loads of him before the contract is done and every one of them is the same."

He looked at the tally and at the fourth mark and the quarter and did the other half, which was the half he did every morning and had done for a month.

"Which means that the second load, if it comes, is a fortnight's wages for four men, and a factor's margin in a bad month, and the difference between those two things is my margin, and I have not got a margin, and I have got a yard with four men in it and a rate and a Friday."

The gate was open. The trestles were up. The iron went on the stone every four seconds and had gone on every four seconds since the sixth of Mudmonth, except for four days, and the four days were a bridge that was holding, and neither brother had said the word *holding* in a yard in ten weeks.

---

The market came first, because in a fen town the market comes first and the yard is what it comes to.

The board had been wiped and chalked every morning for a month, and the clerk's rule was over his own half of it, and the other half was not wiped, and there were four lines on that half and there had been three on the ninth of Greenmonth.

The first three were the standing ones. *Ask Kest, he was gone.* *Road warden in the cart on Friday. Asked nobody anything.* And under the first, a smaller one in a different hand: *Ask him what the three and a half is for.*

The fourth was new and it had no rule and it was in the clerk's own good hand, which meant it was the clerk, and the clerk had done it, and the clerk had not been asked.

*Road shut since the 19th of Mudmonth. Ask the road warden why.*

Tovan Kest stood and read it, and a man setting down a barrow of fish beside him read it too, because a man with a barrow reads a board.

"Mind," said the man.

"Mind yourself," said Tovan Kest, and went to work.

---

What the market said, on the twenty-eighth, was this, and he had it from four separate mouths by the fourth hour without asking a single one of the four, which is the only way a market works and the reason it is worth more than a court.

The law was on its side. That was the first half and it had been the first half since the fourth of Greenmonth and it was true and it was in a magistrate's own hand in a county file two hundred miles off, and the whole of this town had been built out of that one sentence for a month and had a crown clerk's compliment nailed up in the watch house to prove it.

The second half was that a road warden had not answered a summons to the provincial town on the ninth of Greenmonth. That was also true. He had been four days off under an arch and there was a word against the ninth in a ledger in a provincial office, and a word in a ledger is not an argument, it is a date, and nobody in this market had ever been able to look a date up and did not need to, because a market is not a court and a court would have said *and what was he doing on the ninth*, and the market had joined the two halves itself.

"Here's the thing about the road," said a carrier, to a miller's man, at the third hour, loud enough for four people. "The road's shut. Why's the road shut?"

"Because the crown took the bell off the bridge."

"The crown took a bell. Says nothing about a road."

"Road's shut and the road warden's not sat the ninth."

"Which is a different thing."

"Which is the same thing," said the carrier, "because a road warden's got to be sat in a room like anybody, and there's a word in a book says he wasn't, and the law's on its side, and so the road's shut, and here we are."

And eleven people heard that, and by the fourth hour it was on a cart going to Four Elms, and by the sixth hour it had a second version, and the second version had the road warden in it *as a cause* and not merely as an absence, and by the twenty-ninth it would be the ordinary weather of this town and there would be no point at which anybody could remember who had said it first.

Tovan Kest went and found the carrier afterwards, because a yard is a place where a man comes to be told the rate.

"You said it."

"I said what I said."

"You said the road warden wasn't sat the ninth, and that is true, and you said the law's on its side, and that is true, and you put them together in a public place and made one of them cause the other, and it is not, and you know it is not, because you have been on the wharf road with a cart for eleven years and you know a shut wharf road is a bridge with a trust on it and not a man in a room."

"Doesn't matter what I know."

"It matters to the four men in my yard," said Tovan Kest, "who will be asked, and it matters to a man on this road in a fortnight who is going to be told the road is shut because a road warden did not go to the provincial town, and that is going to be in a fourth man's mouth by the end of Longlight and there is no un-saying it in a market. Ever. In eleven years I have watched this town say the law was on its side and build a whole month on it, and I am telling you the shape of it so that you know what you built."

The carrier looked at him.

"You're the one with the yard."

"I am."

"Then you tell them."

"I have told four men in my own yard at the seventh hour tonight and there are more men coming up Weir Street every week than there are men in this bench of this guild," said Tovan Kest, "and if I stand in a market and say *the road is shut because of a Crown's letter in a drawer and a bench that cannot answer for its own stone*, I have said a thing in a market about a Crown, and this town has had one person say a thing in a market about a Crown in eleven years and it was the man who got four hundred people to hear him on the twenty-seventh of Mudmonth, and I would like the record to show that the two of them were the same man and I am not him."

That was the whole of it, and the carrier took his barrow off up the street, and Tovan Kest went back and found that the word *because* had already come off the board in somebody's head and was not coming off again, and that the correct thing to do about a sentence in a market is nothing at all, and he had known that for eleven years and had never had to use it.

---

What a shut wharf road cost, in one morning, in three trades, was this.

The miller at the Four Elms cut went up to Merefen twice a day with a cart and forty bushels and a boy on the front of it, and the wharf road was the last quarter of a mile, and with the wharf road shut he went up the Old Toll road instead, which is narrower and has a rise on the north side and no kerb, and it added four miles and about twenty minutes to each journey, and the miller did it for nine days and then found an ox-cart at his own price and put a man in front of it with a stick, which is what a man does when a road is shut in a country with no hills in it. Twenty minutes twice a day is a man and a half a week in a trade where the margin is a shilling on a load. The miller put it down as the cost of a bridge.

Wenna Koll had the wharf.

That was the other half of it and it was worse, because the wharf is the only thing in a fen town that can be given to somebody for nothing, and the trade had gone to her, and she had spent the nineteenth of Mudmonth to the twenty-eighth of Greenmonth discovering that a ferry operator's profit is a volume and that a volume arrives because a market is a market and not because a boat is a good boat. Her lighter fouled on the weir sill twice a week. Her own punt fouled on it once, and she had to pay a man with a barrow to lift her out, and she had done that on the twenty-first of Greenmonth and had asked a road warden to look at a stone and had been told the truth and had been given four hours of work for nothing, and she had told nobody about the four hours and would not.

"I do not want the wharf," she said, to Tovan Kest, on the twenty-eighth, at the second hour, in the lane, because she was a woman who said things in lanes. "I have said that to four people this month. I want the wharf when the road is open and the wharfage is worth fourpence a load, and I want the weir sill dressed, and I want the market on the fourth. I have got three years of the wharf and I have had a worse three years of the wharf than I have ever had of nothing, and there is a man in this town who is going to be able to say that a road was shut for a month, and I am the one who is going to have to say what it was worth, and I have had it three times already and the answer gets worse every time, and in Harvestmonth it will be a number in a book and then I will be a woman who profited from a disaster and there is no correcting that in a market. **Ever.** I have watched this town correct nothing."

"What do you want me to do with that?"

"Nothing," said Wenna Koll. "I want you to know it so that if you are ever in a room where somebody says the road should stay shut, you are the only man in it who knows what I just told you."

---

The Old Toll crossing at the ninth hour, with a bar and a line and no crew, was where he got the thing that the whole chapter had been walking toward without knowing it.

The abutment was sound. That was the first thing, and it was the thing nobody in this town would believe, and he put a line across the parapet at the third string course and got it true to within an eighth, and the coping stones were bedded in lime and the lime was good, and the bell-shaped hole in the parapet where the cradle had been was going green in the sun and had moss in the bed of it and no iron in it now at all, because the cradle had gone with the bell on the nineteenth of Mudmonth and had been rusted into the stone and had been prised out in three pieces by a man with a chisel.

"No iron," said Tovan Kest, to the river.

That was all it was, and it was the whole chapter, and it took him forty minutes to say it out loud to himself while he walked about on a bridge nobody was paying him to be on.

"For eleven weeks this crossing has had an owner who could not be found and a crew that could not be hired, and everybody in this town has been saying the two are the same thing, and they are not, and I have known that since I was seventeen and I have never once had to say it in a room.

"A crew is not an owner. A crew is three men and a fortnight and a wage out of a box, twice a year, at the two low waters, to point the parapet and look at the sill and dress what is loose, and a crew can be hired by anybody with a box and can go home at noon. And that is *all* the crew ever was, and the crew never once decided anything about this bridge, and the crew never once signed anything about this bridge, and if this crossing had been left to its crew it would be a footway with a rope on it.

"The owner is the thing that answers. And the answer is the expensive part. When a stone goes at four in the morning on a Tuesday in Fallowmonth, the owner is the man who has to be told there is a stone to replace, and the owner is the man who has to find four hundred marks, and the owner is the man who has to stand in a room and say *I have known for a year and I did not do it.* And nobody in this town has ever had to make that distinction, because for two generations the owner was a bench and the bench was up the road and the bench had a book, and a bench has a book and therefore has an answer, and nobody in Merefen had ever had to think about what happens to a bridge when the answer is a date and not a man.

"And the answer to this one is a date. The bench wrote *in abeyance* in its own minute on the fourth of Greenmonth and it is in a roll in a chest in the guild house and it is a good clear hand and it is the truest sentence that anybody in this town has written about anything in four years, and it means that this crossing has no owner until this bench sits in Frostmonth, and until it sits it has nobody to say to, and *nobody to say to* is not the same as nobody looking after it, and I have found out this month which of those two things this town is actually short of.

"It is not short of looking after it. There is a road warden who comes out here on his own time and dresses a weir sill and puts a rail on a stair. It is short of one thing. It is short of a man who can be asked."

He stood on the Old Toll deck with the line in his hand and did the other thing, which was the thing he had come out there for and which he had told himself on the way up the road he would not do.

He counted the crew a crossing in abeyance would need, and what it would cost, out loud, to the river.

"Three men, nine days, a fortnight's wages, lime, and a barrow of stone. Three marks and about six the campaign, and two campaigns a year, which is seven marks and no farthing, and I have said four marks and six in every conversation I have had about this in a month, because four is the figure the bench will enter and the bench's four is the figure that goes in a book. I have been telling a rounded number for four weeks because a man who says a round number gets believed sooner, and I caught myself doing it and I have stopped.

And that seven a year has come out of a wharfage of fourpence a load for two generations, and it has been paid every year, and this year it is not going to be paid, and the year after it will be against nothing, because the wharfage is suspended by a Crown's letter from the first of Longlight and there is nothing behind the wharfage but the trust, and the trust is in abeyance.

"So the honest figure is: this crossing loses about four marks and six a year of maintenance and about eight marks and ten shillings in a winter half of tolls, and nobody in this town can pay either, and neither of those numbers is the reason anything has gone wrong and I want that written down somewhere by somebody who is not me.

"The reason anything will go wrong is that in about a year there is going to be a hole in the guild's roll exactly the shape of this year, and in about six years after that there is going to be a hole in the roll the shape of six years, and the man who reads it in Fallowmonth in ten years is going to be perfectly correct to say that nobody kept this crossing, and there is going to be no page anywhere in this county that says anybody tried, and the thing that would be on the page is a handrail."

He got the line back off the parapet and put it over his shoulder.

"That is what I have to write down tonight," he said. "Not the four marks. The handrail."

---

The yard at the seventh hour, and four men, and the iron every four seconds, and he said it before anybody asked, which is a thing he had done once in a month and had learned the cost of and the value of and was going to do again.

"Right. Once, and then we do the last of the nine and we go home.

"This yard is a yard. That is all it is and that is all it has ever been, and I have said the other version of that out loud in this gateway once and I am not going to say it twice, so here is the new one and it is the part I have not said.

"A yard is paid for a contract. A contract has a rate, and a term, and a day of the week, and a name at the top of the paper, and a third party's name in the second column, and a clerk's hand on a copy that is not this one. And every wage that has ever come out of this box has come out of it that way, and that is the only defence this gateway has ever had, and I have never needed it before and I am going to need it more than I ever did, because the road is shut and a shut road is a thing people say, and a yard is a thing people check.

"So three things this yard is not, and I am saying them tonight while the stone is on the trestles and not on a Friday.

"It is not a road. Nobody in this yard has ever said one word to any of you about a road and nobody ever will, and if a man comes up this street with a story about a road and wants to leave it here, he can leave it at the gate. There is a woman two flights above a salt merchant in this town whose whole trade is a man's paper and who has been at me for five weeks to make this yard into a second press, and she is right about the reasons and she is wrong about the building, and I will not be the second copy of anything.

"It is not a court. I have sat in a room this month where nine men took a vote about a bond and I stood up and did not vote, and the reason I did not vote is that a man on both sides of a thing cannot weigh it, and I said that out loud in a public room and I meant it, and the rule is the rule and it applies to me and it applies to my brother and it applies to anybody.

"And it is not a hiding place. That is the third one and that is the one I have just invented and I want you to know that I invented it tonight in this yard because of a stair rail.

"**Nothing that belongs to a man goes in this yard's book. Only a thing set.** A thing set has a date and an hour and a description and it can be looked at. A man's paper is his, and if it is in this yard and anybody comes up this street with a Crown's hand, then it is in my building and I am a man who keeps another man's Crown paper in a yard on a street, and there is no answer to that in any court in this county. So if a man brings me a document, I will tell him to take it to the salt loft, and I will say so in front of you and not in a corridor, and I will not be angry about it, and I will not keep it. Not for a day. Not overnight. Not if he is my brother."

The four men looked at the tallies and the stone and each other.

"Master Kest," said the fourth man, "who is going to say the thing that's been sitting there all month?"

"I am," said Tovan Kest. "Go and turn the ninth ton over."

---

And then he did the other thing, and it took him nine minutes, and it was the second entry in six weeks on a page of the yard's own book that was not the week's wages and was not a contract and was not a tally.

The first was from the eighth of Greenmonth, and he had written it out on the morning after, in his own hand, at the fourth hour, in the yard's own book, and had told nobody, including the man it was about: a seat cut square in a block of the fourth course of the Old Toll sill, two discs of Steppe glass set edge to edge across a mark in the stone, a pin, an oak collar, three blows in the head of it, the ninth of Greenmonth, half past the ninth hour, and the maker's mark under the near disc. And no sentence about a road.

The guild had a copy. The press over the salt merchant had a copy. Tovan Kest's *week-book* had the guild clerk's copy at the ninth page with the clerk's name and the date and the hour. And the yard's own book, which was a day book with tallies in it and a column for the day's weather and nothing else at all in the whole of it that was not a day's work, had the fact itself, in the hand of the man who watched it happen, with no sentence about a road in it, and it was not a copy of anything, and it could not be refused, and it could not be produced, and there was no office in this county that had any right to ask for it.

The second entry was for the twenty-eighth, at the seventh hour, in the same hand, on the same page, and it went above the first because Tovan Kest wrote the last thing first and had been doing that in this book since he was fourteen.

*A handrail, six feet, oak, two draw-bored pegs, new tenon dressed in a cooper's bin stave, set in the post at the head of a nine-tread stair on Weir Street outside the cooper's own door. Set by Aren Kest. The timber was the cooper's and had been thrown out in Rainmonth. Not paid for and not asked for and the cooper does not know. The stair is outside the room the setter sleeps in. No charge, no contract, no name against it, and I am entering it here because a thing set ought to be in a book and there is no book for it and this is the book.*

He ruled a line above it, and the line was there because he had learned that off a market board in Greenmonth and had been angry about it for a month and had put a rule over his own line without saying anything to anybody, and the anger had gone somewhere he had not been able to follow it.

Then he sat and looked at the page, and worked out the thing he had actually done.

He had put his brother's unpaid work in a book. Not in a court, not in a claim, not in a minute and not in a roll. In a creditor's-adjacent day book in a yard on a street where a man comes down it every day, with a date and an hour and a description and no opinion.

And there is no column in that book for it.

And he had spent a month telling four men in a gateway that this yard's only defence is that it is paid for a contract, and here was the first line in the book in six weeks that was not a contract, and it was not a favour either, because a favour is a thing somebody can stop, and nobody can stop a rail on a stair.

He did not tell his brother. He had had the entry in the book for nine minutes and he was aware, in the ordinary way a man is aware of a thing he is going to have to say at some point, that he was not going to tell his brother, and that the reason he was not going to was that the moment he told him it would become a thing between them, and he had spent a month and a half refusing to have things between them, and a stair rail is not a thing between them. A stair rail is a rail.

---

The man from the factor's counting house came up Weir Street on the twenty-eighth at the ninth hour, on foot, on his own boots, and stood at the gate of the yard with a factor's coat over his arm, and he had been in this yard three times in a fortnight and had not yet been given a straight answer about anything, and Tovan Kest had watched him on the last two of those three and had worked out that he was not a fool.

"Iven Sarrat," said Tovan Kest. "You have been in my yard three times and asked me nothing and looked at everything, and I have decided you are not a fool, so I am going to answer a question you have not asked yet, and you may have it in front of the four or take it in the gateway."

"I will take it in the gateway."

"Right. What you want to know is not about a road. You have asked my brother about a road and he gave you a true thing with no number in it, and you have not slept properly since, and you have come up this street three times since and you have not asked him again, which means you have worked out that he is not the man you want.

"You want to know who in this county is answerable for a crossing. And that is a real question, and it has a real answer, and the answer is going to cost you about four marks a year for the next eleven years and it is not going to be anybody, and I am going to tell you how I know that and I am going to tell you before you ask, because in this town a man who asks a question after the answer has been given is a man who was going to do it anyway."

He put the bar down.

"Eleven years ago this crossing had a bench, and the bench had a book, and the bench was the owner, and the owner was *somebody*, and somebody was the only word in the sentence that mattered and there was always one. This month it has a minute that says *in abeyance*, and *in abeyance* is not the same as *nobody* and it is a great deal worse than nobody, because nobody is a thing you can go and shout at and in abeyance is a thing that is in a chest. And the difference between a bridge with no owner and a bridge with a crew is about four marks and six a year, and the difference between a bridge with a crew and a bridge with an owner is the whole of the answer to your question, and I have never in my life seen anybody in this county put those two things side by side and be believed.

"So: no. There is nobody. There has been a bell, a bond, a finding, a Crown's letter in a drawer and a bench-minute in eleven weeks, and there has not once been an owner, and you cannot carry a load of paving stone on a road that has no owner, not because the stone is heavy but because in this county a man who has a claim on a road is a man who has a claim on nothing, and I am telling you that as a man with four marks and a quarter and a factor's contract and a brother on a guild roll and I am telling you that I have been the answerable man twice in ten weeks and it is the worst thing that has ever happened to me and I would do it again and it has cost me a year's wage and half my crew's peace and a hand I have not got back the use of in rain.

"The thing you should do, and I am saying it as a creditor's man to a creditor's man, and not as a favour and not as advice, is this. Come back on the seventh of Longlight, when my factor's money is due at the mill. If it is there, you and I are square, and you write to your house and say *the town of Merefen has a yard, a rate, a Friday, and a man who is answerable at the gate.* And you do not write one word about a road, and that letter will be worth more to you in Fallowmonth than anything this county is going to tell you in a room."

Sarrat stood in the gateway with the coat over his arm and did not say anything for a while, and Tovan Kest watched a man do arithmetic, which was the first decent thing he had seen a stranger do on that street in a month.

"You are telling me to write a letter that is true and is not about the thing I came about."

"I am telling you to write a letter that is true, which is the only kind there is, and to leave out the part that is not true yet." He took the bar back up. "And I will tell you the part I have left out, because I have had a month to work out which parts of this are mine.

"The yard has work in it. Eleven thousand setts' worth of it in a month, in a town where the road is shut and the crossing has no owner, and I did not get it by being a good carpenter, and I know exactly why I got it and so do you now, because you have been in this gateway three times and you have watched a man come down this street, look at a thing that is wrong, and say a figure out loud, and everybody behind him hears it.

"So my brother is the reason this gate has anything behind it, and I built this gate for eleven years to keep one thing out of it, and I got it right, and it does not work, and it is not going to. And I have said that out loud in this yard tonight to four men, and I will say it in a room in a year if there is a room, and I am not going to be a man who spent a month being proud of a gate."

"Will you take the second load?"

"I will take the second load," said Tovan Kest, "and I will tell you at the gate on the seventh of Longlight whether the yard can carry it, and if it cannot I will tell you that as well, in the same words, on the same day. And I have not said yes to a stone I have not been paid for, and I have not said no to a man I have not looked at.

"Go home. It is a long road and you will be at the wharf before dark and the tide is making."

---

He shut the yard at the ninth hour and hung the tally on its nail with the mark's name on it and the hour beside it, and then he did a thing he had never done in nineteen years of keeping this book, which was to go back to the eighth page and read two entries in his own hand in the lamplight, one of them six weeks old and one of them two hours old, with a rule over both.

The ninth ton was dressed and carted on the twenty-ninth and was at the mill on the thirtieth, and the factor's four marks were due at the fourth hour of the seventh of Longlight, and the wages came out of the cutting, and the second load would be a fortnight's wages for four men, and the second load might or might not come, and the man who would decide it was going to sleep in a house two days south of here with a coat over his arm and a letter to write that would not mention a road.

Tovan Kest shut the book and put it on its shelf, and where the seat and the rail were there was a column for the day's weather and a column for the day's hours and a space at the foot of the page that he had ruled, without knowing he was doing it, at about the fourth hour of a Thursday, for things that were neither of them.

It was the smallest space he had ever ruled in a book, and it was the only one in the county that had anything in it.
