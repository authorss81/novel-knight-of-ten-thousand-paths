# Chapter 0034

The second of Longlight was a Sunday, and it was the first Sunday in four years that a bridge in this county had more work than it could do, and Aren Kest was on the Sedge Reach deck at the fifth hour with a sheet of Crown paper in a leather tube and the river four fingers below where it had stood in Mudmonth.

He had come out at the fourth hour for the ordinary reason and had stopped at the gate with the tube in his hand, because the gate of the Sedge Reach has a post on it and the post has a board nailed to it, and the board is the county's.

**SCHEDULE OF THE SEASON'S INSPECTIONS.** The county of the Sable. By the hand of the county's surveyor. Returnable at the fourth hour of the sixth of Longlight, at the survey office in the county town, and not elsewhere. **A penny a mile and a shilling a day and the cart at the county's.**

Forty-one structures on it. Eleven bridges. Four culverts. Two fords. Nine causeways. Fifteen pieces of raised road the county claims to keep and does not. And a column on the right-hand side of every line headed, in the surveyor's good hand, in one word:

*Keeper.*

He read it four times on the second of Longlight standing at a gate in the sun with the tube in his hand, and then he went and did the work, and he came back to it at the eleventh hour at the end of the day, and it was still the same one word.

---

The four jobs were not four jobs. They were three Crown jobs and one man's own, and they had come at him in the order they came, which was: a schedule on a Sunday, a courier on the Tuesday, a copying post on the Thursday, and a farm at Four Elms on the Friday, and he had said yes to two of them by the seventh hour of the second of Longlight and no to two, and he had not told anybody in Weir Street and had not written it down anywhere, which is how he found himself standing at a gate at the fifth hour on the second of Longlight with a Crown's tube and a Sunday's work in him.

The copying post came from the county's survey office and it was three shillings a week and it was the best money offered to anybody in Merefen that month. It was three shillings a week. His own trade was a shilling a day, which was five shillings a week and six on a market day, and the difference between the two numbers was the difference between a man who can be asked whether he is free in Fallowmonth and a man who cannot, and there was not a single person in this county who would have told him to refuse three shillings a week.

The courier's was a Crown sealed packet twice a month from the county town to the district office and back, four days each way, at the county's expense, with a horse and a boy and a change of horses at the halfway house, and the packet was sealed and was not to be opened and was not to be left out of the rider's hand.

The farm at Four Elms was a culvert. A man with forty acres and a bad ditch and a year's crop in it wanted a roadwarden's eye on a structure nobody had looked at since the year it was built, and would pay a shilling a day and materials, and the road warden's eye on a culvert is the cheapest thing in this county and is the only thing in this county that a man buys with his own money.

And the schedule was a sheet of Crown paper with forty-one lines on it and a date against each and a signature against each, and a penny a mile and a shilling a day and the cart at the county's, and it was the only one of the three Crown jobs that did not make him anybody's man.

He took the culvert and he took the schedule and he refused the courier and he refused the copying post, and he had the whole shape of it in his head by the seventh hour and the shape was this:

**A schedule is a record. A post is an office.**

That was the whole of it and it took him four days to get to, and he wrote it out in the notebook on the eighth of Longlight at the ninth hour, sitting on a culvert head at Four Elms, and the notebook is the only place it has ever been and it is in a coat.

*A schedule asks me what I saw. A post tells me what to do next. I will sign what I saw. I will not be told what to do by a man two hundred miles off who cannot be got at in a room, and I will not have my hand initialled in a Crown book, because an initialled return is a return nobody in this county will look at again for five years, and the initial is the whole of its weight, and the initial is the part of it that says a man was here.*

*And the money. I am refused three shillings a week and I have two marks and three quarters on my brother's cutting contract, cleared at eight pence a day off the yard rate, and I am a man who is behind on a room on Weir Street, and I want it entered somewhere, including here, including in this book, that one of the two reasons I refused a copying post in this county at three shillings a week is that I am the man the Crown would pay, and a man who is paid by an office is a man the office can use, and being useful at a Crown's expense is the exact shape of the thing I came back to get out of.*

*And the fourth thing, which is the reason and not the reason. The old road warden's office in this county could be stretched to cover anything at all, if a man is willing to be the man who signs. It is not an office. It is a guild post, elected in this room in Frostmonth. And a county can put a form in front of it and the form does not ask what the office is, it asks whether a man will put his hand on a line, and if he does, then the office has become whatever the last form wanted it to be, and it will have become that by six separate small uses, and no single one of them will have cost anything, and in four years the Crown's county book will contain a paragraph about a road-keeper of Merefen and the paragraph will be true of every line in it except the first.*

Then he did the work, because that is what he had decided and the deciding had taken four days.

---

He started at the fourth hour of the Sunday at the north end of the county and worked south, and he is a man who counts load points under a structure before he enters it, and he counted them at every one of the forty-one, the way he had counted them at every one of the forty-one since he was seventeen.

Of the nine: four were sound, and he wrote *sound* and the date and the hour. Two were the Sable's own crossings and had been in his yard's memory since Mudmonth. One was a plank bridge on a mill leat with a rotted string and a sound abutment, and the sound abutment and the rotted string is the whole of what a culvert or a leat is: somebody in the middle of the last three generations cut a corner. The other two he had not reached the near side of and did not guess at.

The Sedge Reach was the ninth, and the ninth was the worst of the nine, and it was not on the list as a bad one.

He was on the deck at the ninth hour with the tube open and the sheet on the low parapet weighted against the wind, and the deck was true to the chalk marks he had put there on the twenty-first of Greenmonth, and the third bay's pack joints were what he had said they would be, and the fourth bay's string course had a crack in it that was not structural and was going to be, and he measured it twice and wrote it down and wrote the word *watch* beside it, which is a word that means nothing in law and everything in the trade.

And then he got the instrument out and put it on the deck boards beside the brass stud, and took two readings, and they were the same two readings he had taken on the twenty-first of Greenmonth, to the eighth of an inch.

The guild's man with the levels was on the deck. He was not there by arrangement. He was there because the Sedge Reach is on the county's schedule and the mark is the guild's and the guild sends a man when the guild's mark is on a Crown's paper, and he had been on the deck since the seventh hour and had watched the whole of the first ten lines and had not said anything, which was the most he had said in a month.

"A quarter of an inch," said Aren Kest.

"I have not said a word," said the guild's man.

"You have not."

"No. I have been standing here for two hours watching a man do forty-one structures and I have not said a word and I would like it noticed that this is the longest I have ever gone without saying a word to anybody about a level, and I am going to say something in about four minutes, and I would like you to have the sheet ready."

The remark column on the county's schedule was a ruled space two inches deep at the right-hand end of every line, and it was ruled on the sheet itself, and the sheet was the county's, and the sheet was going into a book in a building in the county town at the fourth hour of the sixth of Longlight and then a copy of that book was going to a provincial office, because that is what a county book is.

Aren Kest wrote:

*Sedge Reach, four timber bays. Deck true to 2 1/2 eighths over 22 feet against the marks of the 21st Greenmonth, and the marks of the 21st are still good. Third bay pack joints to be cut out and re-packed in Fallowmonth at low water, as recorded in Mudmonth. Fourth bay string course, crack 2 1/4 inches by a two-inch gauge, not structural at present, to be watched and re-measured at each marking. Sound.*

And then, underneath, in the same hand and the same ink, because a thing written underneath a thing is read underneath it:

*DATUM. The bench mark at the low end of this deck was re-cut by hand on the sixth of Mudmonth, YR 312, by three men of this guild, the instrument being three days away, the minute being in the bench's book. By the same means in Mudmonth of YR 297, and in the twenty-eighth year, the minutes being in the bench's book. I have taken two readings off this mark. It reads a quarter of an inch below the height given in the bench's own figure of the twenty-eighth year. I cannot give the intermediate figure, which is not recorded, and the bench has never compared the three, and I have asked and been told that the bench has no wish to be compared. Every level in this county taken since the twenty-eighth year is therefore out by not less than a quarter of an inch, in the direction that makes a deck appear sounder and a crown appear higher than it is. Entered here on the county's paper, with the day and the hour, by Aren Kest, road warden of Merefen, in the presence of a man of the Merefen bridge guild. The bench has been asked for this figure for eleven days and has declined. This is entered on Crown paper because it is Crown paper and not because the bench is wrong, which I have not alleged and do not know.*

He put the instrument away and the guild's man took his cap off.

"Now," said the guild's man.

"You were going to say something."

"I have said it, in the sheet, in your hand, and I have not said one word of it." He put his cap back on. "I am going to say the rest of it, and I am going to say it to you and not to the county, and then I am going to go home.

"You asked me on the twenty-first to put a quarter of an inch in this bench's roll and I would not, and I have not, and I am not going to, and I have thought about it for eleven days and I am still not going to and I want to tell you exactly why, because I have never in thirty-one years told anybody why I do the things I do in this building and I have started twice.

"A figure in my roll is a figure the bench owns. And if that figure is wrong — and I have not said it is wrong, I have said I cannot check it and I am sixty-one and the instrument is three days away — then it is wrong in a book in the guild house with my hand on the entry, and in ten years a man comes into that guild house and reads a quarter of an inch and says *in the twenty-eighth year, by the guild's own hand*, and he will be right, and it will be the third thing that guild has got wrong in a hundred years and the third one is the one that kills the reputation of the other two.

"But that is not mine. You have written it on the county's paper. And the county's paper goes into a book in the county town and a copy of the book goes to a provincial office, and if the figure is wrong then the error is the county's, and the county has a surveyor and a county clerk and a court, and all three of them can be got at it, and one of them will be, in about four years, by a man who is not you and not me.

"That is the whole of my trade and I have never been able to say it out loud in a yard. **A thing that is ours is a thing we are answerable for and a thing we cannot be corrected in. So keep the figures you can be got at.** I have been keeping this guild's wrong ones *ours* since I was thirty, because I did not know how to make them somebody else's, and you have made one somebody else's in eleven days by putting it on a sheet in a leather tube, and I want to tell you that it is the cleverest thing I have seen a road warden do in thirty-one years and I do not think you did it on purpose, and I am going to be irritated about it for a week and then I am going to be glad, and I am telling you both now so that you can be glad sooner."

"A quarter of an inch," said Aren Kest, "every level off that mark after the first re-cut, and every man since who took a figure off a book instead of off the stone. Copy a level once and it is a quarter out. Copy it twice and it is half. **There is no bench in this county that can tell me which of the three marks a level came off, and by the time there are four generations of that, it is nine inches.**"

"And if you ask me for the exact figure I will tell you that I have never once had it and neither has the bench, and a man who gives you an exact figure he has not measured is a man who is about to be asked for it in ten years by somebody with a rule." He picked the tube up and gave it back. "Call it nine. Call it a hand's width. And understand what I am telling you, which is that the stone is out a nail's width and it has been out a nail's width for thirty-five years, and **not one man alive in this county will ever find a nail's width on a mark a bench has dressed by hand.** The nine inches is not in the stone. The nine inches is in the heads. A man takes a level off that mark and writes it in his own book and does not write *this came off a mark that is a nail's width out*, because nobody ever told him, because telling would have to be in the book and the book says *re-cut in hand*. So he rounds it, because a round number is what a book takes. And the next man takes it off him and rounds it again, because he cannot check it either, and the third cannot, and by the fourth generation the figure in a young man's head is a hand's width out and the stone under his feet has never moved at all. **Four generations of men, and every one of them rounded it in his own favour.** And it is the heads that go on building for a hundred years. Sign it. Do not improve it. And then go and do the other thirty."

---

The market was at the eleventh hour because the second of Longlight was a Sunday, and a Sunday market in Merefen is a small thing on the wharf road, and it was doing no business at all on the wharf road, because the wharf road was shut, and so a Sunday market in Merefen in Longlight is about forty of the forty-one stalls on four hundred yards of the Old Toll road and the Sedge Reach approach, which is a market that has been moved to a bridge it was not built for.

He went through it at the eleventh hour on his way back to the gate with an empty tube and four days' work in him, and the woman who sold the good cheese from under the second awning said, to two other women, in the voice of a woman making a joke that is also a courtesy:

"Are you the keeper of the crossing, then?"

"I am not, ma'am. There is no such office in this county and there has not been one since the nineteenth of Mudmonth, when a magistrate sat in a room and read out a list."

"You are the man that keeps it," said the woman, "which is not the same thing and is better."

"Thank you," said Aren Kest.

And he walked on down the approach with his hand in the pocket that had the notebook in it, and the thing that happened to his face in the next four seconds was the thing that Mara Vey would have recognised from a filing room two hundred miles off and would have described in one line.

There were two words, and a market only had one pair of hands' worth of them, and one of the pair meant *the woman who counts the thing* and the other one meant *the man who does the work*, and he could stand in a market in Longlight and be called the keeper of a crossing every day for the rest of his life and mean it, and he could not open his mouth about the other one, because the other one was a document in a drawer in a toll-house with three *noes* in chalk on the outside of it and a column headed *keeper* on a county sheet, and a man in a market who begins to explain a column heading on a county sheet is a man who has begun to explain something in a market, and this town had a name in a market once already and it had cost a man four hundred people on a market morning and eleven years of his own life.

So he said nothing, and he said it perfectly, and he walked down an approach in the sun in a market that had been moved onto a bridge it was not built for, being called the keeper of a crossing by a woman selling cheese.

And then a man at the last stall, who was not a stranger and was not from the trade and had a week's wages in a bag, said the same six letters, meaning them, and a road warden corrected him, because that one was his business.

"No. I am the road warden. I am elected in this room in Frostmonth, I am paid a shilling a day when there is money in the trust and there has not been any since the fourth of Greenmonth, and the man who keeps that bridge is nine men in a bench who wrote *in abeyance* in their own book and cannot be asked for anything until they sit again, and the word you want for me is *keeper*, and the word you want for them is *bench*, and they are not the same and the difference is four marks and six a year and a crossing four hundred yards long."

---

The work was done at the fourth hour of the sixth of Longlight and the schedule came back into a leather tube on the seventh with a stamp on the wrapper, and he read it in the room above the cooper with the candle at the wrong end of the table.

Every line had a date and a signature on it. Every one of the forty-one remarks was in his own hand and had not been improved on by anybody, which was a thing he had been quietly afraid of and had not written down, and which turned out to be the entire mechanism of the Crown's survey system: nobody improves a remark, because improving one costs a clerk an hour, and a clerk has a book.

And at the foot of the sheet, in a space the surveyor had left, he had written at the second hour of the second of Longlight, in his own hand:

*Taken by Aren Kest, road warden of Merefen, on the second of Longlight, YR 312, at the shilling a day and the penny a mile, on the county's cart.*

*My reason for taking it, and it is entered here because I am not able to enter it anywhere else that is worth being entered in: because a schedule is a statement of what is there and when it was seen, and this man's hand on a schedule is worth more than this man's hand on a finding, and I will not sign a finding in this county at any rate, and a Crown's paper is the only paper in this county that will carry a figure into a building two hundred miles off without anybody in this town being asked one single question about it. The bench of this guild has declined to carry the quarter of an inch on the datum and I have put it on this sheet, and it is the county's figure now and not the guild's, and I have not alleged anything and I am owed nothing.*

*And I am taking it for two reasons and one of them is the money, and I have written that down on Crown paper in a Crown's book, on the fourth day of the first week I have done this, because a man who takes Crown money for a month and cannot say in his own hand why is a man who has already agreed to something and does not know it.*

He read it back twice and put it in the tube, and then he sat in the room with the candle going and did the other arithmetic, which was the arithmetic he had been doing every night since the twentieth of Greenmonth and which had not changed in a fortnight.

*A shilling a day for forty-one structures at a penny a mile and the county's cart, over five days of riding, is about eight shillings.*

*The culvert at Four Elms is a shilling a day and the materials are the farmer's, and it is nine days, and it is nine shillings, and the two of them come to the same place, which is that I have earned about seventeen shillings in a fortnight in a town where I had nothing in the twentieth of Greenmonth.*

*Less three weeks of eating. Eleven shillings and fourpence in a jar on a shelf, and the two shillings for the room comes out of the cutting contract and not out of the jar.*

*And the room is two shillings a month and it is due on the first of Longlight, which is tomorrow, and I have the money for it, and I have written that sentence out because I have written out the other four five times this month and a man who only writes down the figures that are wrong is doing a kind of arithmetic that gets him into a court.*

*And the glass is four marks the pair, money before cutting, and four marks is sixteen shillings, and there is no fresh Steppe glass north of Merefen before the Steppe road comes down in Frostmonth, and it is the second of Longlight, and I have eleven shillings and fourpence, and I am four shillings and eightpence short of a pair of discs, and there is no man in this county who will cut them without the money, and there is no money in this county that is mine.*

---

Sarn Oyelaran came up the second flight of the salt-loft house on the third of Longlight at the fourth hour with a slip in her hand, which was not unusual, and stood in the doorway without coming in, which was.

"Mara sent me down with a line for you and would not give it to me to read, which is a thing she has done four times in four years and each of the four was because the line was about somebody who was not in the room."

"Go on."

"It says: *The county clerk has had a request. Not a person. An office, with a form, at the fourth hour yesterday, from the provincial court, asking the county to state the purpose of the county file on the matter of the crossing of Alder Vale.* And it says: *the county clerk has answered it in one line and the one line is that the file is the file of a court and its purpose is the business of the court, and he has entered the request with the day and the hour, and he has told me that the form came with a printed space for the answer to be given, and that a printed space is the most frightening object in this county, because a printed space can be left blank and a blank is read as an answer.* And it says: *do not write to me about this. There is nothing you can do about it and I would rather you spent the ninth of Longlight on whatever you are doing to the bench of that guild.*"

He read it twice.

"She has not asked me for anything in four days and she has sent me a thing with a sentence in it telling me not to write," he said. "That is the fourth time. It is going to be the fifth time about a thing that matters and the fifth time is going to cost one of us a year, and I am going to be able to say in a year that I knew, and she is going to be able to say in a year that she wrote it down and dated it, and both of those are true and neither of them is a resolution."

"Master Kest, she said to say the other part."

"There is no other part."

"She said, exactly: *and tell the road warden that the date on the form is the second of Longlight, which is yesterday, and that the provincial court in a building two hundred miles off asked a question about this county's file on the day he was on the Sedge Reach writing a quarter of an inch on a Crown's sheet, and that I do not believe that is a coincidence, and that I am not going to tell him what I believe because he would act on it.*"

Sarn Oyelaran went back up the stair and Aren Kest stood in the middle of a room over a cooper on Weir Street with a schedule in a tube and two marks and three quarters carried on a cutting contract and eleven shillings and fourpence in a jar on the shelf, and he worked out that the whole of his month had gone on a piece of Crown's paper in a leather tube, and that on the day the Crown asked what a file was for he had put a true figure into a Crown's hand, and that the two facts had nothing to do with each other and were the same fact, and that the woman in the salt-loft house had been right not to tell him what she believed, and that she had told him, in writing, that she had been right not to tell him.

The river was down and the season had started and the county had more work in it than it had had in four years, and there was not one single thing on forty-one structures in this county that could not be done perfectly well by the other men in it.

Then he got the notebook out of the coat and turned forward past the seat, past the sill, past the two levels, past the four days' reasons, to a fresh page, and wrote the hour and the day and the four lines he had promised himself in a guild hall four days off, four hundred miles and nineteen days away, in a hall he had been in twice and could not be got back to.

*Middle of the year. The lowest water. Two men and a clerk. I will be on the crossing or I will not and there is nothing in this world that will tell me which until I am there or am not there. I have not got a seat. I have not got four marks. I have got a schedule and a culvert and a cutting contract and a man in a provincial filing room who was careful because I spent fourpence, and I have got a woman two flights above a salt merchant who has sent me a line telling me not to write to her, and if that is not a road held open by other people then I do not know what is.*

*And under it, because a man who writes a date in this book and stops there has written a wish, and a wish is a thing that can be got to change: a clerk in a provincial town, at the fourth hour of the second of Longlight, sent a form to a county asking what the county's file is for. The form had a printed space on it for the answer. A printed space can be left blank, and a blank gets ruled through and dated, and a ruled line is read as an answer by anybody who opens the file in twenty years, and the person who has to answer for it is the person who signed the return and not the person who left it empty.*

*So a form went up this road on the third, with a printed space on it, out of a building two hundred miles off, about a bridge in this county into which a man yesterday put a quarter of an inch in a Crown's own hand. The two are in the same fortnight. I do not believe they are the same thing and I am not going to write down that I do not believe it, and there is a woman two flights above a salt merchant who has already written it down and dated it and has told me in writing not to write to her about it, and that is the third document in four months about this matter that has been written by somebody else without asking me.*
