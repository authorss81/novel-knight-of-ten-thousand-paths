The steps were the north steps, and he had said in a yard on the eighth of Greenmonth that he did not know how the road worked, and he had learned on the twenty-seventh of Mudmonth that he did not know that either, and there was nobody on the steps he could ask which of the two was the true one.

That was the first thing. The second was that the copy of a dated undertaking was folded into the inside pocket of his coat where he had put it, with a carpenter's signature and a glassmaker's mark on it and a line at the foot that said a page cannot be asked to turn round, and it had come up with him, and it had not been the thing that brought him, and there was nobody on the steps to hand it to, which told him everything there was to know about a record and about a road in about four seconds.

There was a cold river below and a smell of hot glass and tar and wet wool coming up off the stone, and a girl of about fourteen sitting on the third step with a basket of onions between her boots.

She looked at him for a while.

"You're back," she said. "You said you'd come back and everybody said you'd not."

"I did come back."

"You said you'd come back *at the seventh hour*," she said, with the enormous fairness of a girl of fourteen who has been told a thing is a promise and has decided to hold people to it, "and Mam says a road is not a thing you can be late to, and there was a lot of arguing about it in our house for about a week, and I want you to know that it was a whole week of arguing and it wasn't your fault that I liked it, because I didn't like it, I just had to listen to it."

Aren Kest stood on the bottom step of the north bank of Bellweather with a bar over his shoulder and understood that he had cost a household a week of arguing, and that the reason he had cost it a week of arguing was that he had promised a date to nine men instead of a season.

Then the toll man came along the landing stage at the foot of the steps with his ledger on a board and a string of wooden tokens on his belt, and he was the same man, and he looked at Aren Kest, and he did not write anything.

"Farthing," he said.

"I haven't got a farthing."

"You haven't got anything." The toll man came up the step and looked at the seat of Aren's coat, where the copy of a dated undertaking was folded in. "That's a paper," he said.

"It is."

"Paper's no good here." The toll man took the string of tokens off his belt and put it back, which was a piece of theatre and a decent piece of it. "No, hold on. Paper's good for a lot of things. Paper's how the bench remembers what you said and how the counting house remembers what it gave you and how I remember what I took. Paper's the best thing in the world." He tapped the ledger against his hip. "Paper's just not a ticket. A ticket is a thing that says you came through. A paper says what somebody meant to do about it afterwards. And a man who has come out of a road with a paper in his coat has come out of a road somebody told him about, and the somebody is not me, and the fare is a farthing, and I am not going to be the man who waives a farthing for a piece of paper, because the day I do that is the day this slip is a library."

He took the farthing out of Aren Kest's own pocket, which was the last one, and put it in the box, and wrote a farthing against a cross, and a half mark against a ticket, because that is how this book has kept the two apart for a hundred and forty years and a clerk who puts them in one line is a clerk whose book cannot be read in a court, and he gave the ticket back, which was a strip of whiting with a hole in it.

"For the gate," he said. "And I'll tell you the same thing I told you last time and it has not got any better in a fortnight. There is no crossing behind you. The toll's a farthing on the north side for anything that crosses and a farthing for anything that comes up the steps, because a man who comes up the steps has crossed and is lying about it, and a man who hasn't crossed and comes up the steps is a fool, and I do not charge fools twice, but I do charge them once."

"I crossed," said Aren Kest.

"I did not see you cross," said the toll man.

The morning was cold and the city had been up since the fourth hour and nothing about it was the same as it had been, and that was the first thing he was sure of and could not have put in a document.

A market had opened somewhere down the wharf, in a hall with the doors up, and it was not the market of a place that had lost anything. It was the market of a place that had gone to work. There were loads of salt coming up under the second arch and a boat-load of dressed stone going down with three men on it, and a butcher's boy running the length of the deck with a string of sausages, and above the whole of it, going over the crown of the great arch on a line from a winch on a second-storey gallery, eleven sheets and a blanket and a man's coat, on a Friday, in the cold, because that is what this city did every morning and had gone on doing every morning for eleven years with nobody in it to stop it.

Aren Kest stood on the crown of it and looked for the scar and could not find it, and it took him four seconds to remember why, and then he found it: there was a second mark on the crown, on the north edge, where a thing had come through once, on a night in Mudmonth, in a season this city has no word for, and it was a place where the stone was a different colour, and it was nothing at all. There was no warmth in it and no voice in it and no way of telling a man standing on it that anything had ever come up through it.

He had a notebook in the same pocket as the paper, and he took it out on the crown of the arch, in the wind, with a market going on under him, and wrote four lines and put the book away, and the four lines were about the steps and the toll and the paper and the mark on the stone, and not one of them was about the road.

Then he went to the guild hall, and the hall was over the arch, and the arch ran under the floor of it, and there was a stone in the floor of the room that was the crown of the third arch, and the bench sat around that stone.

---

There were eleven people in the room, which was two more than there had been, and the two more were a man with a clerk behind him and a great deal of paper, sitting on the near side of the table, and a woman in a good coat with a bundle of papers done in string on the end side, who did not sit down.

The toll-house steward from the last time said it himself, before anybody else could, which was a thing a merchant does.

"You have come back with a paper," he said. "I have spent a day and a night arguing about that in three houses on a road that has been shut since before you were born, so let me be quick. My name is Alis Merrow. I keep the counting house of the tolls of this arch, and I am not the bench, and this is not the guild's room, and I have been at this table every sitting for eleven years and the bench knows exactly what I am and it is not a friend and it is not an enemy and it is a creditor."

"Alis Merrow," said the bench-clerk, writing.

"And that is the stranger," said Merrow, without turning round. "Master Kest. The man at the end of the north trade. I argued for a day and a night against three of my own houses that you were a story, and I lost inside it, and the reason I lost is that a man in a guild hall offered to be a witness to the guild's own account of the night, in a room with a clerk in it, and three houses in this city have not had a thing entered against their will since the founding. So here he is, and here is what the week bought, and you may have him."

The man in the chair — the same man, the mallet still on its nail behind him, and Aren noticed that it was a different hammer — said:

"Then let us not have him twice. Master Kest. The bench gave you three things in Mudmonth and you have been gone three weeks, and a bench that makes a man wait three weeks for the price of a promise has no business saying anything about keeping promises. So here is where we are. First. The bench will give you the whole of our night, in our hand, dated, and you will carry it to whoever sends the next order, and it is ours and not yours and you may not enlarge it, and you may not soften it, and if you are asked where a city of four thousand came from you will say that the number is the north's and the account is ours. Second. You will come back. Third — "

"Third has changed," said the bench-clerk, without looking up, "and I am going to read it before the bench says it, because the bench will say it wrong."

The room went quiet in a way Aren Kest had not been in a room in for eleven days.

"Third," said the bench-clerk, "is that the bench wants the promise witnessed, and it wants to know what a witness costs. That is the whole of the change and it is not a small one, and I will tell you why it has changed, and then you may say whether you understand it." He read from the page. "You are the only man in the world who can carry a thing out of this city into a court that has a seal on it. That was true on the fourteenth of Mudmonth and it is truer now, because a magistrate in your country has found that a Crown paper is lawful and the office behind it cannot be named, which is the single most useful sentence anybody has ever said out loud about us, and you said it in a room with a clerk in it and I do not know what it cost you and I am not going to ask.

"A document with one hand on it is a favour. A bench has been carrying a refusal for eleven years with nobody to give it to. That is the whole of our eleven years: a stone under a trough, a counterfoil in a press, a toll house holding the other half of a writ, and forty-one houses, and not one person in any of them who could put the refusal in a court in another country and be believed. You are not the first man who has said he would carry it. There is a list. It is in the second press and it has four names on it and three of them are dead of the fever of the low quarter and the fourth is a man who was carried up the north steps in a cart in the year before last and did not get past the second gate.

"So the bench is not asking for a promise any more. The bench is asking who holds the other end of it. And I will put the question in the form the bench can vote on, and I want it answered in the form a clerk can enter, and not in the form a man uses when he is being honest, because a man being honest is not a form."

He looked up.

"Who is standing at the far end of your road."

And Aren Kest, who had had a month, and a market, and eleven sheets of washing, and a toll of a farthing to think about it, found that the answer he had brought with him was not an answer at all.

"I do not know," he said. "And I am not going to pretend to a room that I have been finding out. There is a woman keeper of records in my country, in a town in a fen, in an office called the Bellweather Claims Archive, and she keeps records outside the Crown's custody, which is a thing she has fought a man about once and won, and she is the only person in the world I would call a witness. She is four days' hard ride from here by a road that does not exist to me and I have no way to tell her anything. I have told three people in Merefen that I was going and the date, and they have written it down, and it is in three books and a press, and not one of those three books is in this city."

"You have no person," said the bench-clerk.

"I have no person at this end either," said Aren Kest. "I have three hands in a yard and a maker's account in a book, and I have told that room in plain words that a page cannot be asked to turn round. I knew that when I made the promise. I did not think it was the interesting part."

"It is the whole part," said the bench-clerk, "and the bench knows it, and the bench is not going to pretend otherwise to a man who has come a long way and got it right before we asked. Write that down in your own hand and put it in your coat, because in about a year somebody with a seal is going to ask you whether you had a witness, and the answer is going to be either *yes, and here is the name* or *no, and here is the paper that says no*, and the second one is worth having, and there are very few men in the world honest enough to bring it."

Then Merrow, from the near side, without turning round:

"He is going to be asked one more thing and he is not going to be ready for it, and I would like the bench to hear it while I am here, because when I am not here I am not going to be able to help and the three northern houses will not."

"Master Merrow."

"Master Kest keeps a thing in a coat that he has not shown anybody." Merrow turned round, and he was not unkind about it, and that was worse. "He has had it since the first morning and he has carried it past a toll gate and up a stair and into this room and he has not once put it on the table, and I have watched a great many men carry things they intend to put down, and you are not one of them yet."

"I brought an account, not a thing," said Aren Kest.

"Then say the account out loud and let the clerk write it," said Merrow, "because a man who says an account out loud in a room with a clerk in it has entered it, and a man who says it in his head has not, and I did not spend a week of three houses' money arguing for a story that stops at the door."

---

So Aren Kest stood on the crown of the third arch in the floor of the guild hall of Bellweather and said out loud, to a bench of nine, a toll-house steward, a toll-house clerk, and a woman in a good coat with a bundle of string on the end of the table, what there was in the north.

He said it in the order a court would use, and he said it slowly, and he answered three questions the bench asked him in the middle of it, and he did it the way he would have read a load table off a bridge to a man who did not want it.

"On the thirtieth day of Mudmonth, YR 312, a sworn account in my own hand was entered in a magistrate's court in the county of Merefen in my country, and it was stamped, and a claim number was given, and the account is in that court's file and not in my keeping. I have no copy of it. I gave the only thing I brought back out of this city to that court on the thirtieth with no condition and I kept nothing, and I would do it again, and I am telling you that I have nothing in my coat except a copy of a sheet about a seat in a bridge, so that no man in this room builds anything on a document I am carrying.

"In that account I said that this city is standing, and that a bell in my country is this city's bell, and that I was twenty-one years old and was given part of an order and did not reach the inner city, and that for three years afterwards I said out loud, to men who wrote it down, that a fever had taken this valley. Those are facts about what I said. They are not facts about what happened. I have never been able to tell you what happened and I have stopped trying to say that I can.

"On the fourth of Greenmonth a magistrate of that court heard the watch captain of my town under oath about the removal of a bell from a bridge, and found the instrument lawful, and found that the office that sealed it produced nothing, and recorded that there is no office of keeper of the Bellweather hinge in that county's charter, in the county's list of appointments, or in the Crown's own schedule of provincial offices, and that two papers before her describe a person as holding it. She could not compel the office. She wrote the asking down and dated it, and that is the whole of what a magistrate can do with a thing like that."

He stopped there, because the next part was a sentence he had prepared on a road in a fen and had now discovered he could not say in this room without it sounding like a request.

"Go on," said the bench-clerk.

"That is the account," said Aren Kest. "I am not going to say what anybody should do with it. I have no standing to say and no warrant and I would be a fool to try. You may file it exactly as filed."

The bench-clerk wrote for some time. Then he read it back, all of it, and made Aren listen to it twice, and at the end of the second reading he put a line under it and wrote four words and read those out as well.

"Received, unverified, on the word of a stranger," he said. "Entered at the eleventh hour of the ninth of Greenmonth, YR 312, and in the counting-house copy of the same sitting, at the request of Alis Merrow, who wishes it noted that he asked for it."

"I did ask for it," said Merrow.

"Entered," said the bench-clerk, "and I am going to enter one more thing at Master Kest's request and not at mine, because it is the only sentence in this evening that is actually for us and not for him. Enter: that the north's account, whatever else it is, does not name this city. It names a bell and an office and a road warden. It does not say four thousand of anything. If this city is ever entered anywhere it will be entered by us, in our words, in a column, and not out of a man's mouth in a court he cannot attend."

"He was right the first time about that," said Aren Kest, "and it was not his rule."

"No," said the bench-clerk. "It was hers. But he gave it to us free, and this building has been free since the fourteenth of Mudmonth, YR 301, and I would like somebody to write down today that we are still free and that the reason is a woman with a press four days' hard ride away that he cannot reach."

Nobody in the room wrote it down. The bench-clerk wrote it down.

---

The woman in the good coat with the bundle of string said four sentences in the whole evening, and the first three were about the tariff, and the fourth was at the door, and it was the reason the night had gone the way it did.

"You have told us a thing we have wanted for eleven years," she said. "We have wanted it so much that I have not said it once, because a city that has waited eleven years for a paper to arrive is a city that has organised itself around the waiting, and if you turn up and say *here it is* in a guild hall, then in a year there will be nine men in this room who believe that the north is coming, and they will do everything else eleven years late waiting for it. You have cost us that. I hope you understand what it costs, because it is not the same as six marks and it is not written on a roll."

"I understand it," said Aren Kest. "My brother has a road warden's brother and that is what he says."

"Your brother has a brother who is a road warden," said the woman, "which is worse, and I know it, because I have one too, and she is a magistrate, and neither of them has ever once come home when it was inconvenient for them, and both of them think the other one has a life that is a great deal more dangerous than it is." She took up the bundle of string. "The tariff for a stranger's entry is a farthing and it is a farthing and the three northern houses will keep it, and if you go up that road in the spring I will stand in front of you and take it off you myself, and that is not affection and it is not a threat, it is the toll."

She went out.

Merrow stayed.

"I want to say the last of it and I want it said where the bench can hear it," said Alis Merrow, "because the bench will hear it in a year whether I say it or not and I would rather the record showed I said it in a room."

"Say it."

"You are not going to get the register page by being honest," said Merrow. "I have known that for eleven years and I have never once had the pleasure of saying it out loud to a man who is being honest. You will get it by being useful, and the use I have got is not your case and it is not the city and it is not the bell, and if I tell you what it is you will laugh, and I would rather you did not laugh in the room."

---

The bench broke up at the twelfth hour and the room emptied the way a room empties when nine tired men have finished, and Aren Kest stayed at the table with the man with the mallet, who poured water into three cups and pushed one of them across.

"You will want the pier," said the man. "I have said you would and Quen has said it louder. Sit down. I am going to give you the whole of it in a quarter of an hour because you have not got one and I am not going to have you waste six days finding it out."

The outer ring of the second pier from the north, river side, low down: eight inches, opened in the cold and shut in the warmth, opened and shut eleven years, not growing this month, marked twice a year by two men and a clerk and kept in this hall. The ring stone itself was sound — that was the thing a man could not get past — and behind it the mortar had been raked out from the inside by water coming up under the arch and out through the spandrel, and there was no repair of that which did not begin with the arch out of the water for a season. A new cutwater on the river side of the second pier. Nine months of this bench's whole capacity, stone and iron and wages, and the tolls on that arch pledged to the ninth of a grandson, and the man who pledged them knew what he was doing.

That was the arch.

And the arch was on top of the other thing, which the man said last, and which took eleven seconds, and which nobody in the room said out loud again for the rest of his stay.

"Three toll-houses on the north road," he said, "shut since the nineteenth of Mudmonth, YR 301. Not ruined. Shut. Shut with the books open and the shutter down and a man inside twice a year to keep the frost out of the ink. There is a paved causeway under that shutter with four feet of paving still on it and no traffic on it and no traffic on it going to stay on it while a city that cannot mend its own bridge asks a creditor's office for a favour. The northern trade is not shut because of a fever, Master Kest. It is shut because there is a crown's writ in a counting house with a name in it and nobody will move a cart north of the third arch until they know whose name it is."

He drank the water.

"You want to write a book about us," he said, "and I would rather you went and looked at a pier first."

---

Merrow was at the door with his coat on when Aren Kest got there, and the toll-house clerk was behind him with the bundle of paper, and the count of the evening had been entered in three books, one of which the bench did not know about.

"Master Kest," said Merrow. "The page you came for. I have not said no to it and I have not said yes, and I have been in a counting house for twenty-two years and I know what a page in an outgoing register is, and I will tell you what it is so that you can stop asking for it as a favour, because a favour is a thing I can refuse and a receipt is a thing I cannot."

"Tell me."

"A writ goes out of an office and the half that goes out is the whole of it, and the half that comes back is the counterfoil, and the counterfoil stays in the office that sent it, and *the register* is not the office's book. The register is ours. The register is the account of what was owed and what was tendered and what came back and what was withdrawn and who said so, in a column, in a hand, with a number against it, because if a city ever wants to argue in a century about whether an order was lawful, it will not be arguing with an office. It will be arguing with a creditor's book, and there is only one of ours, and it is four hundred leaves and it goes back a hundred and forty years, and your writ is one line in it among about nine hundred lines, and the man who wrote that line is dead, and the family that keeps the book is the family that is owed by it." He put his hand flat on the door frame. "You did not come here for the truth. Everybody comes here for the truth. You came here because you have a bell in a storehouse in a town called Merefen that is under a paper, and a page in my book would let a court ask the office that sealed it why the paper exists. And I cannot give it you for nothing, and I cannot give it you for your case, and I am not going to insult you by pretending that a man in a wet coat with a road in his coat and a piece of paper in a country four days off is a currency."

"I know," said Aren Kest.

"Do you," said Alis Merrow. "Because you have not once, in an evening, said a single word about what my houses are owed, and I have been sitting eight feet from you for two hours. Say it now, and say it as a trade and not as a plea, and I will tell you whether the arithmetic is any use, and if it is not I will tell you that as well, and we will both go home."

---

Aren Kest stood in a doorway in a city that had not asked to be in anybody's book and did the thing he was for, which was the load path.

"Three toll-houses shut on the north road," he said. "Paved causeway, four feet of paving, no traffic. A city that cannot mend its own bridge asking a creditor's office for a favour. And a writ in your register with a name in it, and nobody north of the third arch moving until they know whose name it is." He stopped, because he had been about to say the next part and the next part was a speech, and a man who can name a motive is a man who has already written a speech.

"The north has a road," he said instead. "A raised causeway, reed channels either side, four timber bays, a fen with nothing on it for four days' riding in any direction, and a town called Merefen that is fed off four hundred yards of bridge. And the market on the ninth is worth more than the fourth. I know that because I have stood in it two ways."

"That," said Alis Merrow, "is a promise. And a promise is worth nothing to me, and it is worth less than nothing to a clerk, because a promise is not a fact and a fact is the only thing a register will hold."

"No," said Aren Kest. "It is not a promise. A promise is what the bench asked me for. That is a road. There is a raised causeway with four timber bays on it, and I have been over it at flood stage and at low water, and I have put a level on it, and it is in my notebook, and it is a fact about a bridge and not a wish about a river. It is there. It is four days off. There is nothing I can do about whether anybody in Merefen ever uses it, and that is exactly why it is a fact and not a promise, and I have heard myself say the word promise eleven times this month and I would rather give you a bridge than a word."

Alis Merrow looked at him for a long moment.

"Four timber bays," he said. "On four, and how far apart, and what is under the outer end, and is the third bay sound or is it a thing that will be a hole in Fallowmonth."

"I do not know the third bay and I will not guess at it in a room," said Aren Kest. "I know the deck, the camber, the string course, the way the load goes into the piers on the last of the four, and I know the outer end is on soft ground, and I know a man with a sounding rod would have that bay open in a day and you should have that bay opened before anybody in this city plans a cart road on my word."

"That," said the toll-house steward, "is the first true thing anybody from the north has said to me in eleven years, and it is a thing about a bad bay, and it is worth four times what your speech was worth." He took the bundle of string off the clerk. "You have not got the page. You have got a fortnight, and in that fortnight a clerk is going to open a book and read a line and read the line above and the line below, and the line above and below are the tolls on a bridge, and I am the man who decides whether a stranger walks into my counting room while my clerks are at that book. Do not thank me. Do not come early. Come at the second hour, and come without a coat, so that the clerks can see you have nothing in your pockets, because they have been asked to look at pockets for eleven years and they are extremely good at it."

"Are you giving me the page?"

"I am not giving you anything," said Alis Merrow. "I have told a man with a road in his coat where a book is kept and what time it is opened. What happens in the fortnight is between you and four hundred leaves and a clerk's judgement about whether you are a creditor, and I have a clerk who is not a fool and who has a grandmother at the third arch."

He went out into a cold evening with his coat buttoned to the throat, and the clerk went with him, and the four hundred leaves went with him in a leather box that had a lock on it and a lock's key on a string round a clerk's neck, and the counting house was a hundred and forty yards down the wharf and it was shut.

Aren Kest stood in a doorway in a city of about four thousand people who had not asked to be in anybody's book, with three cups on a table behind him and an hour to think in, and he had come back across a road with a case.

What he had found, in a room with a stone in the floor, was a bench that wanted a witness, and a toll house that wanted a road, and a city that had been carrying a refusal for eleven years with nobody to give it to.

He had come back to be believed. He had found that the last useful thing he could possibly be in Bellweather was a man who could be checked.
