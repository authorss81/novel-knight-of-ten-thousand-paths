Alis Merrow had a condition, and he delivered it on the thirteenth day, at the second hour, standing at the head of his own table with the four hundred leaves out on the two desks and a clerk at each end of them with their sleeves rolled, and the seal-book shut at the wrong leaf and left in the open where a stranger could see it, which Aren Kest understood at once to be a piece of theatre and then understood, four seconds later, to be a piece of honesty, and to be both at once, which is how this city is run.

"You read a date and four figures yesterday afternoon and I sent you out of this room with them," he said, "and I have had an office of two desks and three clerks ringing all night about whether that was correct, and I have concluded that it was correct and that the only thing wrong with it was the company it happened in, and that is what the conditions are for. So they are first, all four of them, before the book is opened, and the clerk will enter them, and I will not open the press until he has."

"First," said Merrow. "You will not copy it. Not a word, not a figure, not the press-mark, not the impression. There is no paper in this room and there is nothing in your country that is worth writing on, and a man who cannot copy a thing in an hour cannot copy it in a year.

"Second. A clerk will copy the line in a fair hand tomorrow and the copy will go into this office's own press, and it will be a copy of a copy of a thing in a book in this city, and it will not be signed, and it will not say who read it or when. That is deliberate. A copy with a name on it is a document, and a document is a thing that gets produced.

"Third. The fact leaves. The line does not. The fact is the one I put on the back of your own entry in this room on the fourteenth, and I have had it copied in this office's hand as well, so that there are two places with the same fact in it and neither of them is this building.

"Fourth, and this is the one that will matter in nine years and not tomorrow. *You will not say a motive.* Not in this room, not in the north, not in the middle. If you say why, you have written a speech, and a speech is a thing a man can be got to change, and a man who can be got to change a speech is a man who has already been defeated by somebody with better information and a quieter room. A line in a book is a fact. A motive is an opinion, and an opinion can be argued with, and a fact can only be looked at, and a thing that can be argued with goes out of the record the first time somebody in a court says *with respect*.

"And the name," he said, "is in the line. I did not write the line and I cannot unwrite it, and I have had it in a book in this room since the year it was written and I have never read it aloud, and I am going to read it aloud today at this table in front of three clerks, and then I am going to read it again on Thursday in a guild hall because the bench-clerk will make me, and I shall be doing that every month for a year, and I have decided that I would rather be a man who says a name in rooms than a man who has one in a drawer."

The clerk wrote all four conditions down, and read them back, and Aren Kest listened to them, and did not sign them, because there was nothing to sign.

"Now," said Merrow, and nodded at the old clerk, who turned to a leaf and put his finger on it. "The fourteenth of Mudmonth, in the three hundred and first year. Read it out. The whole of it, and the line above it, and the line below it, and the day's column, and then stop."

"Read it out," said Merrow. "The whole of the line, and the line above it, and the line below, and the day's column, and then stop. And you will stop, and you will not go on to the next thing, however much you want to, because what is in the next thing is a man who barged paving stone in Frostmonth of the ninth year and it is not relevant and it is in the same book and that is the whole trouble with a book."

---

Aren Kest read it.

He read it in the flat voice a man uses when he is reading a thing and not reciting it, which is the flat voice a man uses when he is reading a thing and not reciting it, and which he had last used in a filing room over a salt merchant, and the voice got him as far as the press-mark and no further, because the press-mark is a number and the name is not.

It went:

*From the office of the marshal, by the hand of a deputy-clerk, sealed and sent: a writ severing the crossing at Alder Vale and its dependent places, and the removal of the persons found within the sealed limit, and the handing of the arch to officers of that office, and the tolls of the arch released to that office for the term of the severance. Tendered on the fourteenth of Mudmonth, YR 301, and tendered against the arch. Returned on the twentieth. Withdrawn on the twenty-second.*

*Witnessed at the tender by a deputy-clerk whose name is entered. Witnessed at the return by the same hand, whose name is entered. Entered at the withdrawal by a third hand, whose name is entered, and it is not the first hand and it is not the second.*

*Press 4. Die 11. Impression 2 of the season.*

*Day's column, the twentieth: two hundred on the deck, four days stood, crews both banks at nothing, no store of ours touched and one load of flour carried off the north quay and entered as ours because the man who took it was in a Crown cloak and there was nobody on the quay who could say otherwise, and it is entered.*

And then the name, at the head of the first hand, in the column headed *tendered by*, where a business book puts a man's name because a business book has to be able to say who it dealt with.

*Edric Hunn, marshal.*

---

The room did not make a sound, and Aren Kest, who had spent twelve days in a city learning to read rooms, understood afterwards that this was the most alarming thing that had happened to him in twelve days.

A counting house at the second hour on a cold afternoon, four hundred leaves out on two desks, three clerks, and the name of the man who put this valley out of the world, spoken once, in the flattest voice a man has, and nothing happened at all.

Then the old clerk with the spectacles on his forehead took his hand off the page, very deliberately, as though the page had been wet.

"Nineteen years old," he said. "I was in a cradle. My grandmother wrote the third hand and she is eight years dead and she told it wrong and I have always known she told it wrong and I never once said so in this room, because it was not mine to say."

"She told it wrong how," said Merrow.

"She said a Crown's boat came up the river in armour," said the old clerk, "and there was fighting on the deck, and she said the Crown's men cut a man down on the crown of the arch in front of two hundred people, and she said it in a market, for eleven years, and it is the only thing anybody remembers about her, and she was not on the quay, because she was the second clerk here and she was told to be in this room and she was in this room, and she wrote a line in a book and went home and told her sister the version that made it a story." He put his hand back on the page. "I have had eleven years to say that and I have not said it, and I have said it in front of a stranger from the north, which is the worst place in the world to say it, and I would like the clerk to enter that I said it."

"Enter it," said the clerk at the third desk.

"And entered," said the old man, "and I would like the entry to say that the line was tendered on the fourteenth of Mudmonth in the three hundred and first year, and that the man who read it aloud in the guild hall on the night of the fourteenth was a Crown's man and not one of ours, and that this office has had a copy of a hand for seventy years and has never once had to use it on a person, and that the third hand is the only hand in this book that was ever frightened."

"How do you know that."

"Because it is the only hand in this book that has ever been a fair copy," said the old clerk, "and everything else in four hundred leaves is a hurried hand, and a frightened clerk does not write badly, a frightened clerk writes beautifully."

---

The young clerk at the third desk had not looked up.

"The flour," he said.

"The flour is entered as ours," said Alis Merrow, "and it is entered as ours because it is entered as ours, and the day I have entered a line in that column saying otherwise is the day this office is a party, and I am not going to be a party in a shed on a wharf with a lock on it. One load of flour."

"One load of flour in YR 301," said the clerk, "in the twelfth month, at a shilling and a half a load, and there are people in this city who were hungry in the twelfth month of that year, and I have heard my grandmother say so, and I have known since I was nine years old that it is in a book two desks from where I sit, and I have never once asked, because I am the third clerk and I was not going to be the man who found things out."

"You could have asked."

"And I would have found it," said the clerk, "and I would have been right, and it would have been in a book with a name in it, and the name would have gone into a market in about a year." He shut the book in front of him, which no clerk in that room did. "That is what a name does, sir. That is the whole of it. That is why it does not leave. I have had a name in a room for eleven minutes and I can already feel it in my teeth."

---

Aren Kest had not moved.

He had been in the room for a quarter of an hour and he had read a line out of a creditor's book in a city that had been taken out of the world by it, and the name on the line was a man he had served under at seventeen and had defended in debriefs for eleven years and had never once thought about, in the sense that people use the word think about, in the whole of thirty-two years of his life.

He had argued that man into a corner once, in a room, in Mudmonth, four days after he came back, on a bridge, to a brother.

He had looked for eleven years for a name, and the name had been in a book a hundred and forty yards down a wharf, and a man who had been given it for free in Mudmonth by the old man with the mallet, in a hall, in words, had told him on the ninth of Mudmonth in plain English that the other half of the writ was in toll-house hands and that the toll houses were not the guild and were not his friends and were the only people in this city who had offered to give him anything at all.

And the guild's man had read it aloud in a hall on the ninth, and had not read the name, and had said he would not get the name out of him that month, and had been right, and the reason he was right is that the name was in a book he did not control, in a building he could not enter, on the other side of a hundred and forty yards of a wharf from his own bench.

"Do not," said Alis Merrow.

"Do not what."

"Do not do the thing your face is doing." Merrow had not raised his voice and he did not raise it now. "I have watched you get it. I have watched it arrive, and it is the most useful expression a man can have in a counting house and it is the single most dangerous one, and I am going to tell you what it is and then you are going to decide for yourself what to do with it.

"It is *it was him*. It is a Crown and a marshal and a fever and a boy who lost a bridge, all of it in one bundle, and every single part of it has just been taken out of a book and put in front of you, and every part of it is now a fact, and a fact is a great deal easier to carry than a suspicion. A suspicion weighs something. A fact weighs nothing and it goes anywhere. That is what facts are *for*. I have been selling them for twenty-two years."

"I am not going to say a motive," said Aren Kest.

"You are going to have to say something and I want it on the record that I asked you a question about it in a room and you were correct, and that you were correct on the first morning, in the guild hall, before you had read a line, which means it is not a thing I taught you and I am going to be able to use you in about four years." He nodded at the clerk. "Go on, then. The arithmetic, because that is what is in the book and the arithmetic is what you are actually here for, and I would like it said out loud in this room by the man who has read it and not written by me."

---

He did the arithmetic, because it was the trade's part and the trade's part was the only part he had a right to.

"The writ is dated the fourteenth," he said, "and it was read aloud in a guild hall on the evening of the fourteenth, and it gave five days, counted from the day before it was read, which put the end of its own term on the nineteenth. So the paper's term expired on the night the city refused it. That is either a coincidence or it is a paper written to expire on a night somebody had already chosen.

"It was tendered on the fourteenth, which is the day it was read. It was not carried north by a courier with a warning. It was carried north as a tender, in a column headed *tendered against the arch*, and it was put in front of a bench, and a bench is nine men and a room, and a paper that is going to be read in a room on the day it was written is a paper that had already been answered before it arrived.

"And the tender came back on the twentieth with two hundred on the deck and it stood there four days, from the eighteenth to the twenty-second, and the recall came up on the twenty-second with a name on the outside of it. A recall is written by an office that has decided a thing is over. This office decided on the twenty-second, at a table, four days after a Crown captain sat down on a toll stone in the rain with a written duty and no instruction to use it. To decide that on the twenty-second, somebody in that office had to have known, before the tender went back down the river, that it was not going to be used.

"You do not send a recall after four days. You send it in advance, and you send it with a boat, and you leave it in the cabin with a name on the outside of it, because you cannot be in two places. *The recall was in that cabin before the tender came up. Which means somebody at that office knew on the fourteenth, when the writ went out, that it was not going to be enforced, and the two boats were in the same flotilla.*

He stopped there, in the middle of his own sentence, in a counting house, and the old clerk put his pen down.

"That was a motive," said the old clerk. "That last clause. You were four words into a motive and you have stopped."

"Yes," said Aren Kest.

"Noted," said the old clerk, and wrote it, and did not look up. "I have entered the day and the hour and the words. I have entered the arrest, which is a thing this office has never entered before, and I have entered it against you and not against him, because it was your mouth and not his and because you are the one who is going to be asked."

"That is in the book," said Alis Merrow. "Not one word of the rest is in the book. Every one of those other things is a habit of a trade, and I am telling you the habit because you have a building with a lock on it and I would like you to be able to open it, and because a habit is a thing a man can check and a motive is a thing a man can only repeat. The book says what was tendered and when and by whom and what came back and when. I have told you what that shape usually means. If your hand is wrong the Crown will say so in a court in four years and your hand will be in a file in this building by then, and that is exactly how I would like it."

Merrow looked at him for a long moment.

"That," he said, "is the best thing anybody has said in this room since I took this post, and I have heard a Crown's lawyer in here twice, and one of them did not come back, and the other one is upstairs at this hour and does not know what has happened downstairs, which I have decided is a good sign about the other one."

---

Then he gave back the arithmetic that was not his to give.

"Now the other half of it," he said, "and I am going to give it because if I do not, you will go and look for it, and you will look for it in the wrong direction, and the wrong direction is a Crown, and a Crown you can be frightened of, and a frightened man makes a bad case.

"There is a line in the guild's own book that will say he was on the deck at the second hour. There is a man in this city who was on the deck and has a written refusal of an extension in a copy, in a cell, in a Crown office four days off, and a sworn account, and a woman in a claims office, and a brother, and a town. And the same line says that a writ severing that crossing was tendered on the fourteenth and returned on the twentieth and withdrawn on the twenty-second, by an office that had a recall ready."

"So the line exonerates you and convicts you in the same four lines," said Aren Kest.

"Yes," said Alis Merrow. "And I have been a creditor for twenty-two years and I have never seen a page do both, and I would like you to understand, because you are about to go and stand in a hall and say this, and you will get one half of it across and you will not get the other half, and the half you will not get is the one that costs you the case.

"The name on that line takes a valley out of the world and it is eleven years old, and the marshal is alive, and he has an office four days from here, and he has a road and a clerk and a man who can write, and every day that passes is a day that the paper gets older and the man gets stronger. I am sixty-one years old. I am a creditor in a shed on a wharf in a city that was never built, and I have a lock on a book, and the man on that line is the sort of man who does not stop."

The old clerk put his spectacles down on his nose and said: "And there is a great deal in the argument about the flour, and I would like somebody to have the answering of it in a fair hand before Longlight, and I do not care who has the answering of it."

"It will be copied," said Merrow. "Tomorrow, in a fair hand, and it will go into the press, and it will not say who read it or when."

"Not that," said Aren Kest.

---

"Not that."

"Write the line," he said. "Write the whole of it, the day and the hour and the hand and the name, and put it in the press. And then write one line on the wrapper, and I would like it in your clerk's hand and not mine, and I would like it to say: *Copied from the fourth press of the counting house of the tolls, at the second hour of the fifteenth of Mudmonth, YR 312, on the word of Alis Merrow, and not produced in court, and held in this city.* No opinion. No comment. And that is the end of it."

"You have just given away the only thing you came for."

"I have just arranged for it to be in two places instead of one, in one of which I am not," said Aren Kest. "And I have just kept my word, and I said it in a hall on the ninth of Mudmonth before I had read a line, and Merrow, you were in that hall, and I would like it entered in your book that you heard me say it before I knew what I was asking for."

Merrow looked at the clerk, and the clerk wrote it, and the clerk wrote it in a fair hand, and neither of them said anything for a moment.

"You are not going to write it down," said Merrow.

"Not the name. Not anywhere. The notebook goes back into the country at the end of the day and it is the only thing I own that does, and I have had one file in a coat in my life and it cost this city a bench-clerk's afternoon and a woman two nights' sleep, and I am not going to sit in a counting house and make a second one." He shut the notebook, or did not shut it, it was in his coat. "I have read the line out of your book in a room with three clerks in it, in a city that cannot be found in my country by anybody, on the thirteenth day of my being here, and I have said the name aloud once, in this room, and the clerk has entered the day and the hour, and I am going to say it again in a hall tonight because your counting-house man will not be able to stop you and I would like it said where a bench of nine has to answer for it. Twice in this city, in two rooms, with the date on it. That is all the name gets. And what goes south of this wharf is a fact, in your hand and in mine, on a date, saying that a creditor's register in Bellweather has a line for the nineteenth of Mudmonth, YR 301, and that the line is sealed with the press-mark of press four, die eleven, and that the line says the writ was tendered on the fourteenth and returned on the twentieth and withdrawn on the twenty-second."

"That is a very small thing to have gone a very long way for," said the old clerk.

"Yes," said Aren Kest. "That is exactly how much of it is mine."

---

The bench sat at the ninth hour and Merrow read the line out, himself, in a business hand, to nine men.

He read the tender. He read the return. He read the withdrawal, and the four days on the deck, and the flour that was entered as theirs. He read the press-mark, and made the clerk read it twice, because a press-mark read once in a room is a number and a press-mark read twice is a device.

He did not read the name.

He read the name because the bench-clerk put his pen down flat on the minute, and squared it, and said, "If there is a name in it, it is read in this hall. This is the hall the bench of this guild has met in for four generations, and a name in this city is read in here or it is read in a market, and a market is not a room, and I will not minute a name I have not heard in a room."

And it was read. In a hall, by a toll-house steward, in a business hand, in front of nine men, four of whom had been born after that night.

There was a silence in the guild hall of Bellweather after a name was read out in it, and it was about the length of a slow count of four, and it was not a silence of horror. It was a silence of nine men finding out at the same time what a name does to a thing, and the man with the mallet put his hand flat on the stone in the floor of the room, on the crown of the third arch, and said:

"Then the tolls in that line are released. The writ releases the tolls of the arch to that office for the term of the severance. The severance was refused and the writ was withdrawn, and a writ that is withdrawn releases what it tendered, and if it releases the tolls, then the pledge to the ninth of my grandson is made against a thing that was let go eleven years ago, and I have had eleven years of decisions resting on a line in a book I have never seen."

"Yes," said Alis Merrow.

"Then I would like somebody to tell me whether I have to be frightened or whether I am allowed to be pleased."

"Neither," said Merrow, "and if you are frightened, then you have understood it, and if you are pleased then you have understood it worse, and I would rather you were frightened in a room than pleased in a market."

The man with the mallet said: "Then what is done."

"Nothing is done," said Alis Merrow, "and I want that in the minute in these words, and I want the minute to say who said it, which will not be me if the minute is fair, and I would like the bench to hold the arch and mark the second pier twice a year and go on being a bench and not a court, and I would like a man in this hall to understand that the toll houses have just been handed a fact that would double the northern roll in a year, and that I have not taken it, and that the reason I have not taken it is that I have been in this city twenty-two years and I would like to be a person who did not spend a name in a market, and I would like somebody to check in eleven years whether I managed it."

The minute was taken. It took a quarter of an hour and the clerk read it back and the bench-clerk read it back and the bench read it back, and by the fourth reading there was a sentence in it that nobody had put there, which was in the old clerk's hand, and which said that the office of the tolls had been informed on the thirteenth day, and had not acted, and had entered the fact, and the reason would be in the guild's own hand on the following page.

They wrote the following page. It took the rest of the night.

---

The bench-clerk put his pen in the pocket and stood up and looked round the room, and one person was still at the table, because a man who has read a line out of a creditor's book and then stood in a hall for two hours does not go home at a fixed hour.

"Master Kest."

"Yes."

"I have been the clerk of this bench for nineteen years," said the bench-clerk, "and I have written down a great many things in it, and tonight I have written down a name and a date and a press-mark and two loads of nothing at all, and I have a question and I am going to ask it before I go, because if I do not ask it tonight I will not ask it this year, and the whole of the trouble with the thing we are all in the middle of is that nobody asks it in the first week.

"Twelve days ago you stood in this room on the first day of your being here and told us the north has a document. Tonight we have read a line that names a man. I have been sitting at this table for two hours listening to a bench decide what to do about it, and the toll houses have decided, and the guild has decided, and the ferry has decided, and I have heard every one of those decisions, and every single one of them is a decision about what to do with the name.

"Nobody has asked the other question. Not once. Not in two hours, and not in twelve days, and not on the first day of it, and not in a hall in the north, and I have been in this room for all of it."

"What question," said Aren Kest.

"Whether the crossing wants to be found."

The room had nine men in it and a toll-house steward and a clerk and a ferrywright at the door and a man who had been on a deck eleven years ago in a different life, and the arch went under the floor of the room, and the stone in the middle of the floor was the crown of the third arch, and the sheets had gone over it at the sixth hour and the winch was up and the market under the second arch was shutting its stalls.

"We have spent eleven years not knowing what the north is," said the bench-clerk. "We have not known. We have had a stranger come out of a road twice and we have believed him about everything except the one thing that matters, and we have not asked him what the north wants with us, because asking would mean we had an opinion, and a city with an opinion is a city that can be quoted.

"And now there is a name in a book, and the name is a man, and a man can be written to. And a name in a book is the first thing that has come into this city in the whole of my working life that was not ours, and every bench and every counting house in this room is going to spend the next year deciding what to do about it, and I would like it minuted — and it will not be minuted, I am the clerk and I will refuse to minute it, I am telling him that — I would like it said out loud in this hall once tonight, by the one man in this room who has a door in him, and then I am going to go home.

"So. *Does the crossing want to be found?*"
