# Chapter 0031

The room above the cooper on Weir Street had one chair, one table, one shelf, a bed, and a candle that a man could read a page by and not a page and a half, and on the night of the twentieth of Greenmonth, YR 312, Aren Kest sat down at the table with the candle at the wrong end of it and did the arithmetic he had been putting off since the eighth.

He wrote it out because writing it is the only way of being certain that the writing has not improved anything.

*Eleven days of crew wages owed by the trust. The trust has nothing in it until the frost. None of it will ever be paid out of the trust.*

*Two marks and three quarters, being a wage advance against a cutting contract, cleared at eight pence a day off the yard rate. Entered as a wage and not a favour, on the eighth of Greenmonth, in my brother's book at the fourth page, with my mark and a quarter in the same line as paid and not as credited. Carried on if the cutting fails. Sixteen days and a half of my own labour at the yard rate, and I have not yet been on that contract for a day.*

*Six marks on the guild roll against a tool-chest at three and a half, at a shilling a day on the wages, entered as a debt of mine and not of the guild's, proposed by me and withdrawn by me before the vote, and carried on the ninth of Greenmonth.*

*Room, two shillings the month, due on the first, and the first of Longlight is nine days off and the first of Greenmonth was the last one I had.*

*In hand: one shilling and fourpence, being what was left of a day's work at the third bay on the nineteenth.*

He put the candle on the other end of the table and looked at it.

The arithmetic had a shape he had known since the eighth of Greenmonth and had been declining to look at directly, which is a thing a man can do for a week and no longer. The shape was this: there was no version of the month of Longlight in which he was owed anything by anybody, and there was a version in which he was hungry by the ninth, and the only money in this town that was moving was in his brother's yard chest, and the yard chest was a factor's money for paving stone.

He had been eleven years without a household and eleven years without anybody who was owed an account by him. He had thought of that as independence. It turned out that the great deal of the independence was a man in a yard saying *not from your own*, four times, in a row.

The other shape was worse and he got to it last, because he had learned in the last ten weeks that the worst thing is always the one you put at the end of a piece of paper.

There was no work coming off the Old Toll crossing.

The trust had been in abeyance since the fourth of Greenmonth and would be in abeyance for a year, and a trust in abeyance is a crossing with no owner, and a crossing with no owner is a crossing with no crew, and a crew is not an owner. He had been saying those words at himself for ten days as though they were a piece of trade learning, and they were, and they were also this: for a year, nobody in Merefen is paid to look at the Old Toll sill, the parapet, the iron cradle going green in the bed of the stone, or the wharf kerb, or the north approach to the fish steps, and the men who would have done it are on a cutting contract for a factor in the western channel, and the factor may or may not send a second load.

He shut the book and blew the candle out and lay down on his back with his clothes on, which he had stopped doing about eleven years ago and had started doing again in Mudmonth, and lay there and did the other arithmetic, the one he had no figures for at all.

The Sedge Reach was holding.

It was the only thing in this town that was holding. Four timber bays on soft ground, the third bay packed in soft clay to the width of a thumb and the fourth bay's string course sound and the camber true to a quarter of an inch in eleven feet, and the gauge board at the low end reading what it had read every day since the river came down, and the Reed Line standing along the old raised road with the cut ends of the last flood still white on it. In four months it would want the third bay lifted and the pack joints cut out and the shear pins looked at, and in four months it would be Fallowmonth and the water would be low enough to put a staging under it, and in four months he would be the only man in this county who could do that, and he would be doing it for a trust with no money in it, or for the town, or for nobody.

He had been the man the town came for at the fifth hour for eleven years. He had never once, in eleven years, had to work out what he was for.

On the twenty-first of Greenmonth he was on the Sedge Reach deck at the fifth hour with a rule and a line and a lump of chalk, and the river had come down another finger in the night, which was the first time in a month it had come down at all.

The third bay's string course took the line without a fight. He ran it along the underside of the camber for twenty-two feet and came back with the chalk marks two and a half eighths apart, which is a bridge answering a question it has been asked before, and he wrote the figure in the notebook because a figure you do not write down is a figure you have not taken.

Then he went down onto the third bay, because he could not help it, and lay down on the deck boards with his cheek against the planking and his hand flat on the underside of the top string, and the deck was warm already with the first sun coming over the fen, and the timber was doing exactly what he had spent a fortnight telling a bench's nine men it would do.

He came up off it and sat on the parapet and looked at the market going on below without him.

*Ask Kest, he was gone.*

Nobody was at the Sedge Reach that morning but him and a ferryman poling a lighter and a woman with a barrow of peat, and the deck took a cart at half past six and would take another at seven, and none of it needed a road warden in the way that a thing needs a man when it is going to fail. The town had a bridge and it was holding and the man who knew how to keep it holding was standing on it in the sun with nothing to do, and there was no work in that for anybody, and the whole of the difficulty of the twenty-first of Greenmonth was contained in the fact that he had come anyway, at the fifth hour, because he had come at the fifth hour for eleven years, and a man who has done a thing every morning for eleven years does not ask himself on the eleventh year whether it is wanted.

---

The guild's man with the levels came out to the bench mark at the seventh hour, because that was what he did, and not because Aren Kest had asked him, and the distinction was one both of them were careful about.

The mark was a brass stud in a block of the fourth course at the low end of the deck, and it was the datum from which every level in this county was taken, and Aren Kest put his instrument on the deck boards beside it and took two readings and wrote both of them down and did not say anything about them for about a minute and a half.

"A quarter of an inch," he said.

"It is a quarter of an inch," said the guild's man, "and it has been since the sixth of Mudmonth, and you took it on the first of Greenmonth and told a bench-clerk and he told a woman with a press, and I have heard about it, and I am not going to pretend to you that I have not."

"You re-cut it by hand."

"We re-cut it by hand on the sixth of Mudmonth because the instrument was three days away getting the level of the fish weir cut and there were eleven men on the reach and a flood coming, and three men of this guild took a cold chisel and a rubbing block and did it in an afternoon, and they were better at it than the instrument would have been, and the bench signed for it and the minute says the bench signed for it." He took the instrument off Aren Kest without asking to, which he was entitled to do, and set it on the block, and squared the foot. "So the datum is correct to the hand, and the hand is a guild's hand, and a bench does not take a road warden's paper against its own mark. That is the whole of it and I have said it three times this month."

"Who re-cut it before that?"

The guild's man stopped, and Aren Kest watched him decide whether the question was a trap or a real question, and watched him decide it was a real question, which is the only reason anything gets said in these conversations.

"Mudmonth of two hundred and ninety-seven," he said. "The winter before. The instrument was in the capital being mended and the reach wanted setting out and there was no way to get a level out of the county, so the same three men did it, or their fathers, and there is a minute. And before that, the twenty-eighth year, same thing, the instrument was away. And I have not got the figures and neither have you, because nobody has ever taken them off that mark and written them down anywhere except in their own heads and in a bench minute, and a bench minute says *re-cut in hand* and not by how much."

"Three times in thirty-five years, all the same way, all by hand, all in a month when the instrument was away." Aren Kest took the instrument back. "Do you know which way it goes?"

"I know which way the hand goes and it is not a figure. A man cold-chiselling a stud in a block is grinding a little off the top of it every time he dresses it, and he is grinding off whatever is proud, and on a bridge the proud thing is always the high place, and the high place is the place that took the last flood." He took his cap off and looked at the mark and not at the man. "So it comes down. It has always come down. And every time it comes down every level in this county taken after it is a quarter of an inch, or a bit less than a quarter of an inch, out, in one direction."

"Which direction."

"That is the question, and you know which direction, and I have known which direction for eleven years and I have said it out loud to two men and both of them asked me how much." He put his cap back on. "A low datum makes a deck look better than it is. That is the direction. When a man takes a level off this mark and finds a deck two inches under the line he was given, and the mark is a quarter low, then the deck is two and a quarter under and the crown is higher and the freeboard is smaller than the sheet says. And in nine inches of that, a man on a deck in Floodmonth in a westerly has a sheet of water on him who has been told by a written table that he is clear."

"Nine inches is four generations."

"Nine inches is four generations," said the guild's man, "and there is a bridge in this county that was built to the twenty-eighth year's figure and there is a reach that was set out to the two hundred and ninety-seventh, and I have known since I was twenty-four that if anybody ever compared the three of them the whole of this town would have to be levelled again, and I have never once wanted that to happen and neither have you."

He took the instrument and put it in his case and buckled it, and then he stopped, and looked at the notebook, because Aren Kest had it open on the parapet with the page where the two readings were.

"You are going to write them in that."

"Yes."

"That is a page in a coat," said the guild's man. "I will tell you what that is, and I say it as a man who has kept this guild's numbers for thirty-one years and not as a man who is against you. A page in a coat can be lost, and it can burn, and it can be in a coat in a river, and then the three figures are gone and this county goes on being wrong in the same direction for another generation. A figure in this bench's roll cannot be lost. It can be argued with. A man in a room can come here and say, the roll says a quarter, and your page says a quarter and an eighth, which of the two is the truth. And the answer to that is that the bench's mark is the mark, because the bench is the mark's owner and you are not." He shut his case. "And I have had this conversation with a man who was under an arch in Mudmonth and I had it again with him on the eighth, and I have now had it with him three times and he is still writing in the coat."

"That is four times," said Aren Kest.

"It is," said the guild's man, and went, and did not say it was the last time, and the reason he did not say it was the thing that came in the next eleven days and cost a man in a yard a conversation he had been avoiding for a month.

---

The work that was not on anybody's roll came to him in four pieces over two days and every piece of it was the same work, which was a thing nobody had written down.

The first was a footing. Wenna Koll's punt had fouled on the sill of the fish weir because the wharf road was shut and she was bringing her lighter round by the upper cut, and the weir's sill had a worn hollow in the third stone along from the west end that had been worn there since before the flood, and a man going over it with a barrow of salt had put his knee in it in Mudmonth and had not said anything to anybody. It took her a day and a half of work to get her lighter round and she was losing a half-day a week on it, and she asked him at the fish weir at the eleventh hour on the twenty-first whether he would look at the stone.

He looked at the stone for a quarter of an hour and told her the truth, which is that the hollow was not going to get worse and could not be got better without taking the sill up, and the sill could not be taken up because the weir belonged to the town and the town's money was in the trust. Then he dressed the two arrises either side of the hollow down to the fall of the stone, by hand, with a point and a lump of sandstone, and made it a thing a man could go over without thinking about it, and it took him four hours, and she offered him fourpence, and he said no, and she said it twice and he said no twice, and she then said the thing that made it a gift instead of an insult, which was: "Then I will have a boat of yours mended when the wharf opens and there is no wharf to have it mended at, and neither of us will write it down."

He wrote none of it down. That was the whole of the decision and he made it in about four seconds on a weir sill in the sun, and the reasoning was a trade reasoning and it was sound: an hour of a man's time that is entered in no roll is a thing that can be denied by the person who did it, and a thing that can be denied is worth more than a thing that is owed. He had four hours a day that nobody could pay him for, because of a letter in a drawer in a toll-house with three noes chalked on the outside of it, and he was going to spend them on things nobody owns, and he was not going to leave a record, and if the town ever wanted to know what he did with a month in which nothing came, the answer would be that he did not do anything anybody paid for, and that would be true.

The second was a handrail. The stair from the street to the room he slept in was outside the cooper's own door and it was nine treads of oak with an oak post at the top and nothing at all at the bottom, and it had had nothing at the bottom since the cooper bought the building, and a man carrying a lamp up it in the dark would put his hand on air and find a wall nine feet down.

He took the post at the top, which was sound, and a length out of the cooper's own bin of staves that the cooper had thrown out in Rainmonth and would have let anyone have, and he dressed a new tenon in it by hand on his own bench on the evening of the twenty-first, and set it in the top of the post with two draw-bored pegs of oak, and made a rail six feet long that a man could take his weight on with both hands.

He did not tell the cooper. He told nobody. He went down and up it eleven times in the dark with a lamp in one hand, and the twelfth time he went up it the way a man goes up a thing he has made, which is badly and without the lamp, and the stair was there.

The third piece of work was the north approach to the fish steps, and he did not do it, because it was the toll-house's and it was in a creditor's book and the yard had it on a wall, and he went and looked at it for a quarter of an hour and left it, because a man who takes work off another man's page without asking has told the other man's page that the other man's page is not worth anything.

The fourth was not work. It was a question, and it was asked in a yard on the twenty-first at the fourth hour, in front of four men, by a man who had come up the channel with a factor's coat and a factor's boots and who had been in Toban's yard three times in a fortnight and had not yet been given a straight answer about anything.

---

"Iven Sarrat," said the man. "I am told you are the man to ask about a road, and I have been told that by four people in this town in four days, and I want to say to you before I ask that I am not asking because of a bell, and I do not know a bell from a bollard, and I did not come up this channel for a bell, and if I did I would not say so to you in a yard with four men in it."

"Ask."

"The road. The wharf road." He said it the way a man says a thing he has had prepared. "Is it shut for good, or is it shut for now? Because I have eleven hundred tons of paving stone coming up this channel in the season and a quarter of it wants to come off at Merefen, and a man can take a cart onto a bridge or he cannot, and I have to know by Frostmonth, and this is the last month I can know it in."

There were four men in the yard and all four of them stopped, in the way that men stop when a thing is said that they have all been thinking and none of them has said.

"Iven Sarrat," said Aren Kest. "No. Not for good, and not by anything you can wait out. It is shut until this bench sits again, and the bench sits again in a year, in Frostmonth, and the question in front of the bench in a year is not whether the road opens. It is whether a trust can be put back over a lawful Crown's holding on the same stone, and there is a bell in a storehouse under a seal that a magistrate in this county has found lawful, and I am not going to tell you a thing I do not know, and I have told you the half I do know, which is that the year is a year and not a season."

"You have answered me," said Sarrat, "and you have not told me a single thing I can put in a letter to my house, and I am going to have to write to them by Thursday and say *not known*, and *not known* is a bad thing to write in the month of Longlight to a man with stone on the water."

"I am aware of that."

"And I am aware," said Sarrat, "that the answer you gave me is the same answer anybody in this town would have given me, and I did not come up this channel for it, and I am going to say the other half of it now, out loud, in front of these four men, because I would rather be a man who has said it than a man who has thought it. I have been up this channel four times this month and every time somebody has said a sentence to me and looked at somebody else while they said it. I have a yard two days south of here and a house in it and eleven hundred tons of stone, and I do not care about a bell, and what I care about is a road that a man can plan a season on. So here is what I have actually come to find out, and it is not a bridge.

"Does Merefen want the wharf road open in a year, or does it want to be a town that goes on being a town without it?"

The yard was very quiet, and the iron on the trestles was every four seconds.

And then Aren Kest did the thing he did, which was to answer the question that had been asked and put the load-bearing fact on the table with it, unasked for, in the flat voice of a man reading a bearing off a plate.

"You are asking a town," he said. "That is the right question and you have asked it of the wrong man this morning, and I will give you the answer I have, and then I will give you the fact, and the fact is the more useful of the two and I am adding it because in about a year somebody with your letter in his hand is going to be in a room with nine men from this guild, and I would rather you had it now and did not use it than had it then and used it.

"Wharf road. Four hundred yards of the Old Toll crossing and the kerb on both sides of it. It is in abeyance and it has been since the fourth of Greenmonth and it will be until this bench sits in Frostmonth. Every foot of the wharf frontage, every set of weigh-frames, every loading stage and every cart-turn on that four hundred yards is the trust's, and a trust in abeyance is a road that nobody is paid to keep. It is not shut to you. It is closed to nobody because there is nobody in charge of it to open it. If you drove a cart onto it tomorrow it would take the weight, because the bridge is good. If you asked permission there is nobody whose permission it would be, and that is not a magistrate's answer and it is not mine, it is the absence of an answer, and an absence is the one thing in this county that will not improve in a year if somebody writes to the Crown about it.

"And the market." He said it flat. "The wharf road carries the fourth-day market when the river is under a foot, and it has carried it every fourth day since before either of us, and it carried it on the ninth of Greenmonth, and it will not carry it again until somebody owns four hundred yards of it. Which means a market that does not happen. And a market that does not happen is not anybody's loss, it is a hole in a book, and the hole is in the book's owner's book and not the town's, and I have never once seen a town pay for a market that did not happen, and I have seen four towns this year pay for one that did.

"So the answer to your question is: yes, this town wants the wharf road open, and there is no version of this county in which that is a hard question, and the reason it is hard is that the only document that can open it is a piece of paper and the piece of paper is four days off and it does not exist. And I am telling you that in a yard with four men in it because in a year one of these four is going to be asked to vote on it and I would rather he had it from me and called it a trade."

Sarrat looked at him for a while.

"That is the first straight thing anybody in this town has said to me in a fortnight," he said, "and I want you to know that I have had a sentence from your brother, from the clerk of the guild, from a man at the weir, and from a woman over a salt counter, and all four of them told me the same true thing, which is that nobody knows. You have told me a thing that is not a number. So I will tell you what it is worth and then I will go and look at the third bay of your reach, because there is a thing in your brother's yard I would like to see and I would like to see it before the Friday."

He looked at it for twenty minutes, and asked four questions, three of which were about stone.

---

The letter came on the twentieth of Greenmonth at the seventh hour and Aren Kest was standing in the wharf office when it was opened, because a man goes where Crown paper is, and because the toll-clerk of Merefen had stopped asking him for a reason and had simply let him in on the fourth of Greenmonth and had not mentioned it since.

Dace Millward was in the room, and Dace Millward was sixty-one and had been a creditor in this town for thirty-one years, and had held the tolls of the Old Toll crossing, the foot toll and the cart toll and the wharfage, for two years, by purchase from an abbey in Rainmonth, and it was the first time Aren Kest had been in a room with him.

The post had come in at the sixth and the toll-clerk had put it on the counter and had not opened it, because it was addressed to a man and not to an office, and a toll-house is an office and does not open a man's post; and Millward had come down off the Fishgate at the seventh hour, alone, on his own feet, at his own pace, in a good coat.

"There," he said, to the toll-clerk. "That is mine. You will open it in front of me and you will let that man stand there, and I do not care, because in about a year I am going to have to be able to say who was in the room."

The seal was a disc of grey wax the size of a crown piece and the device on it was a hand laid flat on a book.

Millward read it standing up, twice, and gave it back, and did not say anything for a while, and the toll-clerk said, quietly, "I will want to make a note."

"You will make a note on the outside of the envelope," said Millward, "in your own hand, with the day and the hour, and you will say what it is, and I will tell you the words."

So the toll-clerk wrote it, in chalk, on the outside of the envelope, in a good old man's hand:

*Crown letter, twentieth of Greenmonth, YR 312, seventh hour. To the holder of the tolls of the Old Toll crossing. Suspension of the tolls from the first of Longlight. A direction as to correspondence. Received by the holder. Held in the drawer. The tolls are suspended by the Crown and not by this town.*

"There is a word missing," said Aren Kest, reading it over the clerk's shoulder. "It says what it is. It does not say who sent it."

"It is the same wax," said the toll-clerk, "as the grey on the post at the Old Toll, and the same wax as the door of the storehouse by the weir, and I have now seen that wax three times this year on three pieces of paper and I have had thirty years of Crown paper coming through this counter and I would know it in the dark." He looked at the drawer. "And a Crown's letter in a drawer is a rumour. A Crown's letter in a drawer with the day and the hour on the outside in this office's hand is a thing a man can look for. And looking for it is the only thing anybody has ever been able to make anybody do."

"Read the direction again," said Millward, and gave it back, and Aren Kest read it, because it is a habit and because he wanted to hear it in a room with two witnesses in it, and the direction was in the second paragraph and it was seven words long and it was the reason the letter had been written.

*That the holder shall not permit, and shall use his office to prevent, any passage, entertainment, or correspondence between any person claiming to hold, keep, or have held the said crossing, or any part of it, and any other person, upon the said crossing or elsewhere in this county, upon the subject of the said crossing.*

"Entertainment," said Dace Millward.

"Entertainment," said Aren Kest.

"I am sixty-one years old," said Millward, "and I hold the tolls of a bridge that has been out of cart use since my father's time, and the tolls come to nineteen marks a year in a good year and eight marks and ten shillings in a winter half, and I have never once had to fight for them, because they are not worth fighting for. The wharfage is the part that matters and the wharfage is the part that needs four hundred yards of road, and there is going to be no wharfage this year, and there may be no wharfage next year, and I have been in this town thirty-one years and I have never known a market day that this town did not keep.

"So I am going to tell you two numbers and then I am going to go home, because a man who hears his own figures out loud in a public room in Longlight will find a way of saying them in a market by the end of the month, and I would rather say them here first.

"Eight marks and ten shillings. That is the winter half, suspended by a letter, from the first of Longlight, by an office that has not sent me a name. And that is not the loss. The loss is that I am the only man in this town whose book has a hole in it, and a book with a hole in it is a thing a bank will look at twice, and I have money out on the wharf frontage that I have had out on the wharf frontage for eleven years, and a man in a counting house is going to read my roll and find a line that says *suspended by the Crown* and a line that says nothing at all about what the Crown got for it, and he is going to ask me the second question, and there is no answer to the second question except the one in your drawer."

He put the letter down and squared it.

"I am going to obey it. I want that on your chalk, because in a year the road is going to open and there is going to be a great deal of talk about who kept faith with the Crown in the worst of it, and I will have kept faith, and I will have lost eight marks and ten shillings and the whole of a winter's trade, and I have decided tonight in this office that I would rather be a man who did that than a man who found out in a year that the road shut for a reason nobody wrote down." He looked at Aren Kest. "You. You will tell me one thing and then I will go. Is there anything coming in a year that I should know about, that I cannot get out of a Crown letter, because I have been told four times in four days that you are the man people ask, and I am the first of the four who has come and asked you in a room."

Aren Kest thought about it for the length of four.

"Yes," he said. "There is one thing, and I am not going to tell you what it is, because I do not know it myself and because a man who repeats a thing he has been told is a second version of the thing. But I will tell you that a piece of paper in that drawer is the reason the road is shut, and it is the only piece of paper in this county that says so, and nobody has ever read it, and if a bench in Frostmonth has to decide in a room whether to put a trust back over a lawful Crown's holding, then the whole of the argument in that room will be about paper, and there is a Crown's letter in a drawer in this building with a date and an hour on the outside of it, and you have just had it read out loud in front of two witnesses, and that is now a fact with three hands on it and it was a fact with one hand on it an hour ago."

Millward looked at the envelope for a while.

"You could have said nothing," he said.

"I could have," said Aren Kest, "and I have been in this town eleven years and I have watched it hand a Crown's office every piece of paper it had and then complain that it was being used, and I am tired of that, and I have got a bench that sits in a year and a road that is shut and a crossing I cannot reach, and those three things have a common shape, and the shape is that nobody writes down what they know until it is too late for writing."

Millward put his hat on.

"Enter one more thing on that envelope," he said, to the toll-clerk. "In your own hand. *Read aloud in this office, the seventh hour, in the presence of the holder, the toll-clerk of this office, and Aren Kest, road warden of Merefen.*"

The toll-clerk wrote it, and read it back, and Millward initialed it, and Aren Kest put his own hand to it under the clerk's and not over the clerk's, and it was a trade's hand and not a man's signature, which is the correct hand for it.

The letter went in the drawer. The drawer was in the toll-house, and the toll-house was a building with a clerk in it and a door on the street, and it was the only building in this county in which the Crown's own papers were kept, and now there was one more, with a date and an hour and two witnesses on the outside of it in chalk.

Aren Kest went up Weir Street at the ninth hour on the twenty-first of Greenmonth with eleven days and a quarter of a cutting contract in front of him and no money behind it, and a set of two readings in a coat that were a quarter of an inch out in the direction that makes a bridge look better than it is, and no roll in this county that anybody would let them into, and four hours a day of his own time that nobody could pay him for, and a stair in the dark with a rail on it that nobody knew he had made.

The Sedge Reach was holding.

He had been on it every day since the sixth of Mudmonth and it was not going to fail, and there was not one thing in Merefen that needed him that morning, and the town had four hundred yards of unowned road and a woman at a ferry who could not get her lighter round and a stair in the dark and a guild's mark that was quietly being ground down by four generations of good men, and every single one of them had come to him, and not one of them had asked him for anything, and that was the difference between being asked for and being available, and he had never in thirty-two years had to learn it and had four months to get used to it.

The handrail was the first thing he had ever made for nothing, and it was the best thing he had made since Mudmonth, and there was no roll in the world that had his name against it.
