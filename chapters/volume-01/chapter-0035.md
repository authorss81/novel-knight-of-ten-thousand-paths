# Chapter 0035

The ninth of Longlight was a Sunday, and on a Sunday the salt-loft house was quiet, and three separate people came up the second flight of it between the fourth hour and the sixth hour, courteously, in the way a county asks a thing it is not sure it has the right to ask for, and all three of them wanted to know whether the Bellweather Claims Archive existed.

The first was the magistrate's clerk, at the fourth hour, with a note in Lysa Fenn's own hand and not the clerk's, and the note was a question and the question was: *Does the office claim to be a body corporate, and if so under what instrument, and if not, under whose appointment and at whose charge.*

Mara Vey read it twice and then wrote four lines on the back of the clerk's own paper and sent it back down.

*It does not claim to be a body corporate. It is a body of the town's making, under an instrument of foundation in the year of its founding, preserved entire at the head of this office's own book of instruments, and not in the county's file and not in the provincial court's, and it appoints a keeper and it fixes the keeper's charge upon the town assembly, and it provides that the office may be closed only by the assembly. Whether the instrument makes a body corporate is a question for a court and not for a keeper, and I have had it looked at once in four years by a lawyer of this town, who said that it was not quite and was not not, and told me to stop asking.*

And then, because Lysa Fenn had taught her four years ago that the second half of an answer is the half that costs: *If the court requires the instrument, I will produce it. If the court requires me to say what instrument it is, I will say it. If the court asks this office to state whether it exists, I will state that it exists, that it has held the same two rooms since the year of its founding, and that the only office in this county which has been asked to state whether it exists in four years is this one, and that on the ninth of Longlight is the first occasion.*

She put that in the day-book with the hour, and it was the fifth of the four prices she had costed herself in Mudmonth, and she had said in that room that there would be no fifth, and there were four and there was going to be a fifth, and she had been wrong about the number and right about the direction, which is the ordinary condition of a woman who prices things.

The second was the salt merchant, at the fifth hour, who weighed salt for a living and had owned the building since before she came into it and who came up his own stair to his own tenant, which is a thing a landlord does once and then remembers.

"Mara. I want to talk about the second floor."

"I know you do."

"Right. Three things and I have not said any of them in that order before and I am going to say them in the worst order on purpose.

"One. I want the second floor. It is eleven shillings a year and I have had it for four years and I have had the rent on time four years, and there is a market two doors down that has been empty two years and takes fifteen.

"Two. There is a stair. It is my stair and I put a second door on it in Rainmonth and it cost me two and eight, and since then the stair has been a public stair, and men stand on it, and a man in a good coat stood on it for three hours in Mudmonth with a bell in a sack two feet from my door and my tenants heard a bell rung in this building at night, and one of my tenants is a widow and she asked me in Harvestmonth whether this was going to be a business.

"Three." He put his hands flat on the sill. "I am not asking you to tell me what you keep. I have known for four years that you keep something and I have never once asked and I have told my wife I never asked and she said good, and I have a good answer for anybody, and I have never wanted it more than I want it now. I am asking how long you are going to be here, and I am asking because I have to sign a lease in Longlight and a man does not sign a five-year lease on a floor with a Crown's question in it, and if you tell me the Crown's question is going to be answered by the sixth of Rainmonth I will sign for three years and eat the two and eight on the door and say nothing."

"That is an honest question and I am going to give you an honest answer and you are not going to like either half of it. I do not know how long I am going to be here. I have been here four years and I expect to be here four more unless somebody in a provincial office decides this office is a thing that has to stop existing, and that is not a figure of speech, it is a procedure, and it has a name, and I am going to learn its name today, and I am not going to pretend to you that it will not be in your building."

"I would rather you told me now than in a month."

"Yes," said Mara Vey. "And I am going to tell you one more thing for nothing, because you have earned it by not asking for four years. If it happens, and I cannot stop it, then you will be in it, and there is nothing I can do about that and everything I can do about what is in the room, and the two of those are different sizes and the one I can do about is the one I am going to spend the summer on."

He turned the sill over once and looked at the grain of it.

"Right," he said. "Three years, then, and if you are here in three years with the same two rooms and a door on my stair, I will put a second door on the north side at my own cost and never mention it."

"Deal," said Mara Vey, and went back to the filing room, and put *the second floor is leased for three years, at eleven shillings, and the lease is a town document and not a Crown's, and a town document is a thing with a date on it* in the day-book in the second column with the hour, because it was the first entry in four years that was in that column and was about something other than a record of somebody's dead.

The third came at the sixth hour and was a member of the town assembly, and he had come about the front door.

"Mara. I'm not here about your records. I've come about the door and the stair and whether the town wants them kept free for people, and I've been three months on it and I'm at the point where I have to say something in an assembly meeting and I'd rather say something true than something new."

"Go on."

"Forty-one people came up that stair in the first week of Greenmonth. Sarn counted them, because you asked her to and she told me she had, and that number is not an accident, it is a market, and the reason it is a market is that there is a woman in it who is good at it. In Mudmonth I would have said free. In Longlight I am going to have to say that the stair is free and the door is not, because in Mudmonth there was no question and now there is a question and a question has to be answered somewhere and I'd rather it was answered at a door than in a room." He looked at the door. "If somebody comes up here with a Crown's form about your records, that is not a matter for the assembly and I will say so in the meeting. But a Crown's form about *you* is a matter for the assembly, and I would like to know now whether I am voting for the door or against it."

"You are voting to keep a stair free," said Mara Vey, "and you are not voting for a keeper and you cannot be, because there is no such office in this county and I have read out the finding that says so, and if the assembly votes for me I will put the minute in this office's book and use it and it will be the only piece of paper I have ever had from a body that could help me and it will be worth nothing, because the thing this office needs is not to be liked by a town. It is to be *legible to a court*, and those are not the same and the second one is the one I am working on, and I have been working on it since the sixth of Mudmonth and I have told nobody in this town that I am working on it and you are the first and I would like it entered in your minute that I said it."

---

Petch came at the seventh hour with a leather tube and no coat over his arm, and he had a form in it and a second paper, and he had walked up that stair twice since the spring and was on his third time and he was not a young man any more about it, in the way a clerk of twenty-six gets when he has been frightened at a desk for a month.

He put the tube on the table and took his hat off and did not sit down.

"The return was due today," he said. "I have brought the form. That is the form, and the printed spaces are the form's, and I want you to know before you look at it that I did not write the spaces. I write the paragraphs. The spaces are printed and they are the same in every form in the building and they are there because in my grandfather's time a return that was not in the spaces was a return nobody could file, and that is a good rule, and it is the only good thing about the form.

"And the second paper is not mine either," he said. "It came in the same tube. It is signed by the court. It is a writ of enquiry into the custody of this office, returnable on the ninth of Rainmonth, and it was stamped at the second hour of this morning, and it was stamped at the second hour of this morning because it was made this morning, and it says that pending the enquiry you shall not move, copy out, or part with any record held by this office, and that you shall produce into this court your instrument of foundation, and that you shall state the authority on which you hold each item of your holdings."

Mara Vey did not say anything for about a minute and a half, and the thing she was doing in the minute and a half was the ordinary thing a woman does when a thing she has been half expecting for a month turns up four hours earlier than the shape of it had suggested.

"Sit down," she said. "You have been on that stair twice since the spring and you have brought me a writ and you are not going to do it standing up in my doorway in front of my copyist."

"Ma'am—"

"Sit down, Mr Petch, and tell me one thing before I look at either of those, and I am asking it as a keeper and not as a woman being kind to a young clerk. **Why today?**"

He had clearly been waiting to be asked.

"Because your return is not in. That is the whole of it and it took three weeks to do that to you and it took me four days to do it to you, and I am not going to pretend the two are the same thing. It was returnable this morning. It came into my building on the eighth, which was a Saturday, and it sat on my desk over the weekend because a Saturday is a Saturday, and at the second hour this morning the thing came off the superior's desk instead. On the ninth of Greenmonth I wrote a particular about your exhibit in the margin of a return, in my own hand, in pencil, and I could not put it in the form because there was nowhere in the form to put it, and I went back and read it on Thursday and could not stop, and on Friday a man read it, and the writ is about custody and it has nothing to do with you.

"I know what that sounds like. I have said the same thing to two other offices in nine years and I do not believe a word of it either."

"So the writ is about the particular."

"The writ is about the particular and the writ is about the exhibit, and the exhibit is the thing the particular describes, and the particular names an office, and the office has never been produced, and this morning at the second hour somebody in this court decided that the cheapest way of finding out where the exhibit is, was to ask the person who keeps it what her authority for keeping it is." He squared the form, which is what a clerk does with his hands when he has finished being frightened of something. "That is the whole of it. It took a month and fourpence and a fair copyist's pen and a woman's day-book, and it has arrived this morning off a Saturday, and I want you to know that I know what I did."

"Sarn," said Mara Vey.

"Ma'am?"

"Nobody, Mr Petch. I said a name out loud to myself. It is not a fact and I have entered nothing."

And Sarn Oyelaran said, from the other end of the room, in a voice with nothing in it at all, "I have entered the hour, ma'am, and nothing else."

---

"Right," said Mara Vey. "Now here is the whole of what I know about the instrument in that tube, and I am going to say it in this room and not in a book, and Sarn is going to write it down afterwards and not now, because now I want to be wrong out loud with a witness.

"There are three of these. Everybody in this county uses the same three words for them and they are not the same three things, and in the order they are used.

"A **petition** is a paper with a man's name on the top of it. It is a door, and a door has to be answered, and answering it means producing a record or refusing to produce a record, and a refusal in front of a court is entered.

"A **subpoena** is a paper with a date on it. It does not say you have done anything. It says that on that day you shall produce that thing, and it can be made without anybody being summoned and nobody being told until it arrives, and the whole of its force is the date. That one came off a provincial post on the twenty-first of Greenmonth, stamped on the eighteenth, with a seal on it and a recital in it that named an office this county cannot produce, and it was returnable today, and it is the reason there is a second paper in that tube.

"And the third one is the one that works, and it is the one in your tube, and I have only just worked out its name and I am going to say it out loud because there is no other person in this county who could tell me whether I have got it right.

"**Petrification.**

"A record office can be made to stop existing by paperwork without a single man being hurt, and it is done in three moves and I have watched all three of them get made in this county in eleven weeks and I have not been able to stop one of them.

"The first move is a paper with a hole in it. Not a false paper. A true paper about an office that does not exist, and the hole is the whole point, because every answer to a paper about a hole is an answer about the hole. Ours came on the twelfth of Greenmonth as a memorial and it died in a week, and then it came back on the eighteenth with a seal on it and a date on it, and the date is the instrument.

"The second move is a question about custody. Not a demand. A *question*: what is your authority. And there is no wrong answer. I can produce my instrument and the answer goes into a file. I can decline and the answer goes into a file. I can say the office is the town's and the town has made no provision and the answer goes into a file, and if I say that, then the office has told a provincial court that it cannot prove what it is, and from that hour this office is a thing in a file instead of a thing in a building.

"And the third move is the one I have never seen anywhere and have now had done to me at the second hour of this morning by a piece of paper with a seal on it, and it is the one that does the work, and it is this.

"**The writ does not want to succeed.** It does not need to. It is returnable on the ninth of Rainmonth, and it is worth exactly nothing on the ninth of Rainmonth, and the Crown's office that wrote it knows that it is worth exactly nothing on the ninth of Rainmonth, and it wrote it anyway. What it wants is the ninth of Rainmonth to *arrive*, and for thirty days from the second hour this morning there is not one record in this building that can be moved, copied out, or parted with. And the Crown has not taken anything. And the Crown has not closed anything. And nobody in this county has been hurt, and every single thing in this office is exactly where it was this morning, and I cannot make a copy of anything, and I cannot send a copy of anything, and I cannot make a second of anything, and if a man came up that stair tomorrow and asked me for a copy of the only proof of anything in this county, I would have to say that I could not make him one because a piece of paper in a leather tube in a building in the county town forbids it until the ninth of Rainmonth.

"And in thirty days it comes back unanswered, and unanswered, in the county's practice, is a *finding*. And a finding about a record office is a document about an office, and a document about an office is a thing a clerk can file and a man can quote, and I have been right for four years that a date can be looked for, and a date is what is coming for me."

She stopped there, and drank the water, and did not say the next part for a while, and when she said it she said it to the table.

"And here is the part that is mine, and I have had six days' warning of it and did not know, and nobody could have told me, and I want it written down in this room in this hour and I want it written down badly.

"A writ of this kind takes effect when it is stamped, and not when it is served and not when it is returned, and the stamp is dated, and the stamp was the second hour of this morning. And the twenty-first of Greenmonth was the day the first order came off this post, and the twenty-second, twenty-third, twenty-fourth, twenty-fifth, twenty-sixth and twenty-seventh of Greenmonth were six days in which I could have made a copy of anything I liked, and nobody in the county told me those were the six days, and nobody in the county could have told me, **because the writ did not exist yet.**

"So the whole of my margin was six days, and I did not spend it being careful, and I spent it making a schedule of what this office holds and giving it to three people, and a schedule is a list of where every piece of this office's evidence is, and it is in a press over a salt merchant's head and it is in a yard book on Weir Street and it is in a toll-house in a drawer, and a man in a provincial filing room wrote me a particular and put an office in a Crown's file off the back of a wrapper, and that is what the writ is for.

"That is the answer to whether a keeper of records can be frightened and still be competent, and it is yes, for about a day, and then she has to be dull."

---

"I am not going to insult you," said Petch, "by asking whether I can help."

"You cannot. You have brought me a thing you were sent to bring and you have told me the truth about the day it was made, and that is more than anybody in this county has done for me since the fourth of Greenmonth, and it is worth exactly what it is worth, which is a great deal, and I am not going to have it turn into a debt between us, because I have got one of those already and I am carrying it up a stair twice a week."

"Then what I will do is this." He squared the form. "The spaces."

"Tell me about the spaces."

"Every line on that form has a printed space. If a space is blank, the filing clerk rules a line through it and dates the rule, and a ruled line is a return with nothing in it, and a return with nothing in it is an answer, and it is read as an answer by anybody who opens the file in twenty years, and the person who has to answer for it is not the person who left it blank. It is the person who signed the return.

"So there is a form in this county with a great many ruled lines in it against the keeper of the Bellweather Claims Archive, and every one of those lines is a sentence that says *she had nothing to say*, and I know that because I have seen two of them against a registrar in the year three hundred and eight, and the registrar retired in the year three hundred and eleven and there is a note in his file that says he was not obstructive, he was not available, and that is four words and they are the four words that ended him.

"Fill every one."

"Every one," said Mara Vey, "and I am going to do it in a way that I have been thinking about since the sixth hour and I am going to describe to you now so that you can tell me if I have got the reason wrong, because I have never had to do this on my own office and I have watched four other offices do it and every one of them got it wrong in a different direction.

"I am not going to be rude. A rude document is a document somebody can be pleased with, and a pleased man does not open the file in twenty years, and I want the file opened. I am not going to conceal. A concealment is a hole, and this office is a hole in a Crown's file, and I am not adding a second one.

"I am going to **be dull.** I am going to fill every printed space with a line and a time and a reference, so that there is no blank for anybody to rule through, and I am going to write nine hundred words in a fair hand that can be read in one minute and filed, and a document that can be read in one minute and filed cannot be held up in a room and pointed at. That is the whole technique. It is not a personality. I have watched it used on a man's estate in Frostmonth and it worked on him for four years and it stopped working on the fifth because he could not keep it up for five years, and I am going to keep it up for as long as it takes, and I have four marks a year of the town's money and no clerk but Sarn and a building I do not own, and I want you to write down one thing for me in your own hand and date it, and it is this."

He wrote it as she said it.

*That on the ninth of Longlight, YR 312, the keeper of the Bellweather Claims Archive, in answer to a returnable order of the provincial court of the eighteenth of Greenmonth, filled every printed space in the form with a reference, a date, and an hour, and made no concealment and no argument, and that this is on her own account and is not a plea, and that I have read it and I am going to file it as it is.*

"Take it," she said. "And when you file it, file the form with the spaces full, and do not let anybody rule a line through one of them, and if anybody asks you to rule a line, make him do it himself in his own hand and put his initial against it, and if he will not do it then write *space ruled by the filing clerk at the instance of* and leave the name to be filled in, and leave the space for the name, and let it sit there."

---

The instrument went into court. That was not a decision, it was the only thing a writ does, and the instrument is a town's instrument and the town made it and the town can produce it and there is nothing to be decided about it. But producing it means the reeve produces it, because the reeve is the town's officer and the instrument is the town's, and Reeve Aldis Fenn has to sign for it and answer for it, and Mara Vey wrote her four lines and sent them down the stair to Weir Street and by the seventh hour of the following morning there was a reply in a hand that had not been a clerk's hand for forty years.

*The instrument is the town's and the town will produce it. I will sign for it. I want three things in return and one of them is not a favour.*

*One. That the office keeps a copy of the town's own instrument, and that the copy is not made in your press. It is made by the town and it is held in the town house under the town seal, and your press holds a copy of the copy, and that is the order of it and I am not being sentimental, I am being a reeve, and a reeve knows that a thing held only in the room of a person is a thing held in one place.*

*Two. That you do not answer a single thing in writing in this matter without my clerk's initial on it, and that if a Crown's paper comes to the town and not to you, I will answer it and you will not.*

*Three.* I am not going to put the third one in a letter. I will say it in a room. I have been the reeve of this town for nine years and my sister is the county's magistrate and we have written two minutes in eleven weeks that do not agree with each other, and I want the town to know that, because I have found out this month that a town which keeps its own paper is a town that can show somebody what it thought, and a town that cannot is a town that is told what it thought, and I would like somebody in this town to be in the second position and not the first for once in my nine years.*

And then, in the same hand, four days later, a second note, and this one said: *The third thing. I have read the Crown's second paper, which my clerk read out to me at the second hour, and it puts this town in the middle of a writ about a record office over a salt merchant, and the town did not know and the town will be asked. I have costed the rate for a second clerk in the town house and the town cannot pay a second clerk, and I am not going to ask the assembly for one, and I am telling you that instead, and you will find that more useful.*

Mara Vey read it twice and put it in the day-book in the fair hand with the hour and did not put it in the press, because a reeve's letter to a keeper about a reeve's rate is a thing between two people and a town, and there is no column for it and she was not going to invent one.

---

The thing that went out of the building on the tenth of Longlight went to a man who was not a clerk and not a keeper and not her, and it went in a cart down Weir Street with a boy, and it was a certified copy of the town's instrument of foundation made by the town house under the town seal, and she had spent four days deciding whether the writ's bar touched it.

It touched it, probably. She worked it out for a day and a half and wrote the working out in four lines and then crossed out three of them and kept the fourth, which said that a town's instrument is not a record held by an office but an instrument of the town, and that a copy of a town's instrument made by the town under the town seal is an act of the town and not a copy out of a press, and that she could not prove it and that the writ is returnable on a date thirty days off and that she would rather be a fool for a day than hold a town's charter in one room for a year.

And she told the man she was sending it to the cost of it before he took it, which is a thing she has done exactly twice in four years.

Dace Millward was in the toll-house at the eighth hour of the tenth of Longlight, and he had come in for the week's business and had been told by a boy on Weir Street at the seventh hour that a keeper of records had a sheet for him and had named a price, and he had not taken his hat off, and when he had the sheet in his hand he did not read it for about a minute.

"Three things it costs you," said Mara Vey, "and I am going to name them before you say a word, and then I am going to leave and let you decide and I am not going to come back.

"One. Your name goes beside mine, in a building with two hundred miles of road between it and the court that is going to ask about it, and a year from now a man in a provincial office can put the keeper of the Bellweather Claims Archive and the holder of the tolls of the Old Toll crossing in a sentence, and there is nothing in that sentence that either of us chose and both of us will live inside.

"Two. The writ of the ninth of Longlight says that pending the enquiry this office shall not move, copy out, or part with any record held by it. I have gone to the fourth hour of today to say that a copy of a town's charter is not a record held by this office. I think I am right. I have got it at one and a half to one and I have crossed out three lines of my own reasoning, which I have never done in a file in my life and which I have entered in the day-book with the hour, because if I am wrong then the first thing anybody will look at is whether I knew.

"Three. And this is the one I would not have given you a week ago. If a Crown's officer comes to this counter in the next six months and asks you for that sheet, you have to give it to him, because it is a copy of a town's instrument and a toll-holder is not a keeper of anything and there is nothing in the world you can do about that. What you can do is refuse to *lose* it. You are going to put it in your roll, and the roll is a creditor's book, and a creditor's book does not keep documents, it keeps debts, and a debt is a document that a court in a hundred years will order produced because the book says the paper is there. I want an entry, with a day and an hour, and I want it to say what the paper is, and I want it to say that a paper was lodged and by whom and on what day, and I do not want the paper bound into the book, because a paper in a book is a paper that gets lost with the book."

"And what is the third thing," said Dace Millward, "if I have understood the second?"

"The third thing is the one I would not have given you a week ago and I am now going to say it, and it is not a cost and it is the reason I am here, and I have thought about saying it in a room and I have decided that a man who has held tolls on a bridge and written a book about them deserves to be told a thing in his own counting house.

"I have held one set of records outside Crown custody for four years and I have been frightened every day of it and I have had four marks a year of the town's money and no authority and one clerk, and I have kept it because a thing in a room can be taken out of a room. I have never once had a second place, and I did not know until the sixth of Greenmonth that I did not have one, and on the sixth of Greenmonth I got one, and I have got it in a press and a yard book and a toll-house, and two of those three are this county's fault and not mine, and one of them is a yard book on Weir Street in a man's own hand and that is a whole paragraph I did not expect to be able to write."

"Go on."

"You have never had anybody want a page out of your roll," said Mara Vey. "Not once in two years. And I am asking you for one, and I am asking you for it as a creditor and not as a charity, because a toll-holder's roll is the one book in this county that is not a record and is not a claim and is not a guild minute, and it is a book of what a man is owed, and a book of what a man is owed is the only kind of book a court in a hundred years will take seriously, and this town is going to want one of those before the end of my life."

Dace Millward stood at the counter of his own toll-house with a sheet of paper in his hand and did not put it down.

"Two years," he said, "and I have never once had a man come up that road and ask me for a page. And you have come up it four times in three weeks and asked me for two things, and one of them was for the town and one of it was for yourself, and I would like you to know that I noticed which was which, and that I have been a creditor for thirty-one years and I have been handed a great many things by people who wanted something, and this is the first time in my life that a woman has told me the price of her own favour out loud before handing it over, and it was not the largest price I have ever been told, and it was the only one that included what it would cost me to refuse."

He put the sheet on the counter and squared it and read it all the way through, and then he wrote the entry in his roll in his own hand, and it was seven lines, and the seventh line said: *Lodged with the holder by Mara Vey, keeper, on the tenth of Longlight, YR 312, at the ninth hour. Paper: a copy of the instrument of foundation of the Bellweather Claims Archive, made by this town under the town seal. Held in the chest under the counter, in a wrapper, and this entry is the record of it and not the paper.*

"And the last line of it is mine," he said, "and it is not a kindness, and I would like it read in a room some day: *and this roll is the only place in this county where a thing is kept because somebody was owed money, and that is not an accident, and it is not going to be forgotten in a hurry.*"

---

The form came back on the eleventh of Longlight, on the county's post, endorsed, and the endorsement was one line in a provincial clerk's hand saying that the return was received, was in the spaces, was filed, and that the matter stood over to the ninth of Rainmonth.

And under the endorsement, in the same hand and in the same ink and appended and not part of it, which is a thing a junior clerk does about four times in a career and which is the whole of his career:

*Memorandum, of my own motion, and not entered in the file, and I am putting it here because there is nowhere else in this building to put it.*

*That the county file on the crossing of Alder Vale, upon which the writ of this date is founded, was requested on the second of Longlight by this court by a form, and the county clerk has answered that the file is the file of a court and its purpose is the business of the court, and the request is entered with its day and its hour.*

*That this court has therefore gone, in the ordinary way, to the object, which is a bell.*

*And that the bell is held under seal by the watch of Merefen, and that the officer in whose custody it is held is entered against the establishment of this county, which this court holds because this court pays for the watch of Merefen and the watch keeps a roll.*

*And I have read that name off the roll, and I have not had to look it up, and I am entering the fact that I did not have to look it up, because in about four years the whole of this matter will turn on whether the officer holding that object knew, in the ninth of Longlight, what he was holding and on whose account, and there is no document anywhere in this county that says he was told, and he was not, and this is on me and I am writing it down while it is mine to write.*

*— R. P.*

Mara Vey read it four times, and the fourth time she read it with her hand flat on the page so that she could be certain her own hand was not moving.

And then she wrote a single line on a slip of the office's paper, in the fair hand, and put the hour on it, and did not enter it in the day-book, and sent it up the stair to the watch house by a boy with a shilling of the office's money in his hand.

*Captain Rell. Your name is on an endorsement in a provincial file and it is beside a recital about an office of keeper of the Bellweather hinge, and the office does not exist, and the bell in your storehouse is lawful in your custody and a magistrate in this county has said so in a public room, and from today it is a line in a building two hundred miles off. Come up. I have not filed anything and I have not answered anything. Bring the fourth of Greenmonth with you and read this beside it.*

She sent the boy down the stair and stood in the middle of her own filing room with a lamp lit in daylight, and Sarn Oyelaran did not ask her a single question, and the two rooms over the salt merchant held still, and thirty days had started.
