# Chapter 0041

The twenty-sixth of Longlight was a Wednesday, and the river was in the seat of its bed, and the thing about a bridge at the lowest water of the year is that a man can get at anything he likes and can also see, for about two hours, everything he would rather not.

Aren Kest was on the Old Toll deck at the sixth hour with a bar over his shoulder and a tool bag at his feet, and he had been awake since the fourth, and he had not been ready for any part of it, and he had known he would not be, and had come anyway.

The notebook said the same thing it had said since the sixth of Mudmonth, which was that the best hour of the day in Fallowmonth and Longlight is the one before the dew comes off, because the stone is colder than the air and cold stone tells you things. That was a good hour for reading. It was a bad hour for being a man who has to stand up in front of nine people and say where he is going.

He had been four months in work. That was the fact nobody in Merefen had put anywhere and everybody in Merefen believed, because a road warden on the county's schedule and a farmer's culvert looks, from the wharf, exactly like a man who is indispensable. He had forty-one structures in a county book with a ruled column headed *Keeper* and his name in it forty-one times, and a culvert at Four Elms that a man was very pleased about, and eleven days' wages owed to him by nothing whatever.

He had not been needed once since the sixth of Mudmonth. He had been *available*. He had worked out the difference somewhere around the twenty-first of Greenmonth and had written it down in a notebook, and the sentence he had written was: *A thing you are needed for has a person in it. A thing you are available for has a chair.* He had been sitting in the chair ever since.

---

The two men came up the Old Toll road at about the seventh hour with a barrow.

They were the guild's, and the guild's man with the levels was not with them, and the two of them were hired at the yard rate like everybody else and had been sent, and had a job, and the job was the sort of job a bench votes for in a room on a Saturday and then has to be done by two men in a dry month.

They were clearing the bed of the stone.

That is the whole of what it was. The watch had swept the bed of the Old Toll abutment clean on the nineteenth of Mudmonth with a boy and a broom, in front of witnesses, because Lysa Fenn's finding of the fourth of Greenmonth said the bed had been swept afterwards and the boy had been sixteen, and the finding had used the phrase because a finding uses phrases. The moss had come back over the whole of the swept rectangle inside a month, and by Fallowmonth it was nine weeks old on the sun side and thick as a pelt on the north.

And there is only one week in a year when a man can get a chisel to the bed of that stone, and this was it, and two men from a bench that had voted to keep the oldest bridge in the county at its own cost, out of its own materials and its own men's time and not one penny of anybody's money, were on the north side of it with a cold chisel and a hammer and a barrow.

They were four hundred yards from a seat with two used discs in it. They did not know he was on the bridge. They would not have understood the sentence if he had said it out loud, and he did not say it out loud, and he stood on the deck and watched them work and did not go down.

That was the first thing that happened on the twenty-sixth of Longlight and it was the whole of what he had come to look at.

---

He had not changed his mind. He had decided at the ninth hour on the twenty-second, on the deck of a bridge four hundred yards from his own bed, that he was not spending the crossing on a bench's date, and the decision had been made with no crossing in it on either side and nobody to see it, and it had not moved in four days, and the four days had not changed it, and he had not expected them to.

What had changed in four days was everything else.

He had written to a man with a seal on it on the twenty-first, and the letter was on its way to a building two hundred miles off, and the instruction in the same office's letter was that he was to have no further contact with the object, and there was no court behind it, and there was no law named in it, and it required nothing of anybody except that a man should not touch a thing, which is the one thing in this world you can instruct a person to do without a court and it is the only one.

He had lodged a chain of four documents in a creditor's chest on the twenty-second at the fourth hour, and the creditor had written in it in his own hand that his roll now had four things in it that were not tolls, and the man in a counting house who read that roll once a year would stop at four this year and three times the year after, and the toll-holder had said it out loud in a room and had not been thanked.

He had a piece of paper in a room over a cooper that had a grey wax seal the size of a crown piece on it, and he had not shown it to a woman who worked two floors above a salt merchant, and that was the second time in a month he had decided what a keeper of records might not be told, and the first time had nearly cost him a filing room and the second time was going to cost him something he had not yet been able to name.

And he had eleven shillings and fourpence in a jar on a shelf, and four marks is sixteen shillings, and there is no man in this county who will cut two discs of Steppe glass on four shillings and eightpence, and there is no fresh Steppe glass north of Merefen before the Steppe road comes down in Frostmonth, and the man who wrote a page, in his own hand, on a page he gave away and would not charge for, had said in the fourth line of it that a seat in cut stone will carry a certain number of times and then it will not, and would not tell him the number, and Aren Kest had carried the number about since Greenmonth and had told a bench of nine in a hall four days off rather than keep it to himself.

And the third of three had been sitting in him since the sixteenth of Greenmonth with a date on it in a book in another country.

There was no seat.

That was the position at the sixth hour on the twenty-sixth of Longlight, and it was the same position he had written out in full in a room above a cooper on the twenty-second, and the only thing that had changed since was that the date had stopped being four days off and become the day.

He did the arithmetic on the parapet with his back to the wind because it is the only thing he had, and it came out where it always came out, and it came out like this.

He could not cut a new seat. There was no glass and there was no money and there was no maker within two hundred miles who would cut one on credit to a man with a bond on a tool-chest.

There was the old seat, in the fourth course, with two used discs in it, and a pin, and an oak collar with three blows in the head of it, and a maker's mark under the near disc where a maker's mark goes. Two is easy. Three is not a number a glasswright will sell.

And the thing he had understood in the last four days, in a room, and had not written down because he had been waiting to see whether it was still true at the sixth hour on a Wednesday with a bar on his shoulder, was this:

**He did not have to cut a seat at all. He had to have a line and two hands.**

He had been reading a bench's book for four months in his head without knowing it, and the book was the volume outline of a city that had kept a crossing for four generations by refusing to do two things, which were to lie about a number and to promise a stone would do what a stone cannot be asked to do. And the reason it had kept the crossing was that everything it had ever promised had been a promise about *a book* — that the ring would be marked, that the mark would be read back, that a mark that grew would be a reason to act, and that the bench would say the reason out loud in a hall before it acted.

It had never once promised that the arch would hold. It had promised that they would *know*.

And that was the whole of what a crossing needed, and he had spent four months thinking a crossing needed a hole full of glass.

---

He went down Weir Street at the ninth hour, before the market, and the first thing he did was the thing that took nine minutes to work out on the twenty-second, which was to decide it in a room and not in a notebook, and he did it twice because there were two people and a man who decides a thing in a notebook has decided it alone and that is the whole of the last eleven years.

The first was the yard.

Tovan Kest was on the trestles with a face-stone job and four men and there was no factor's stone in the yard any more, because the contract had gone through in the last week of Longlight and been paid, and what was on the trestles now was a job for a house in the county town and it was small and it was slow.

"I am going out on the date," Aren Kest said. "That is the sentence and I am saying it to you first and I am going to say the same sentence to somebody else at the eleventh hour and if one of us tells anybody that the other said something different, then neither of us said anything worth a road."

Tovan Kest put the chisel down. He did not say *sir*, and he did not say it in a way that meant anything, which is the thing he does about nine times a year.

"Say the whole sentence, then, and say it slow, and I will write it down because I have got a page and a rule and I have never once written a road in that book and I am going to do it this morning and I want you to hear me say that I know what I am doing."

He wrote it in the yard's book at the foot of the page, in his own hand, with a date, an hour and a rule over it, which is the third thing he had invented in a gateway in front of four men in Greenmonth and the first time the third thing was the one that applied.

*A line was dressed and set at the Old Toll crossing on the twenty-sixth of Longlight, YR 312, at the second hour, by four men of this yard, at the yard rate, on a work of the bench of the Merefen bridge guild, being the first of the two visits the bench voted on the fifteenth of Longlight, and the work was not put out by the road warden and is not paid by him. The yard holds the fact of it and not the paper, and the paper is on the bench's roll and not in this book, and the yard has not been told anything else about any road in this county and does not want to be.*

"Say the last clause again," said Tovan Kest, without looking up.

"The account is on the bench's roll and not in this book."

"That is the correct clause and I have never written it before and I have thought about it for four days. A bench's roll is a town's roll and a town's roll is a thing a watch captain can be asked for, and a Crown's man can come into a guild hall and put a paper on a table in front of nine men and say *this is yours, is it not* — and nine men will say yes, because the paper is in the room." He ruled the line over the entry. "So the fact of the work goes in the guild's roll, where a Crown's man can read it, and the paper does not, and you are not in my book and neither is the road, and I have put a road in my book this morning for the first time in nineteen years and I would like the reason noticed."

"What is the reason."

"Because you did not ask me to." He put the pen down. "You have asked me for a line eleven times in four months and every time you asked. And this morning you came down this street and told me a thing and did not ask me for anything, and I wrote it down anyway, and if you had asked I would have said no and it would have been the correct no."

---

The second was the salt-loft rooms, at the eleventh hour, and it took two flights and a landing with a chalked joke on the wall that he read going up and did not say anything about, which was the fifth time in a month and which he had begun to notice, and noticing it was worth a page he had not written.

Mara Vey had the return of the ninth of Rainmonth on the table and had been at it since the seventh hour, and it is seventeen days off, and there are four columns of writing on that table that will be there tomorrow, and Sarn Oyelaran was at the other end with a sheet and a wrist that had gone bad in Longlight.

"Sit down," she said. "I have four things and the first one is that I am going to be busy and the second one is that the fourth of Rainmonth is the ninth and there are seventeen days and I have not got one answer yet out of three, and the third one is your business and the fourth one is that you have an hour and then I have to give the hour back to the paper."

He said the sentence. The same sentence, in the same words, at the same rate, and he said it to her the way he had said it to his brother four hours earlier, and she wrote it down, and then she made him read it back over her shoulder and not behind her, because that is what a keeper does and because she had learned it off a toll-holder in Mudmonth.

*On the twenty-sixth of Longlight, YR 312, I go out of this country on a crossing of the Old Toll, at the fourth hour, and I do not know when I will be back in it, and I am telling you where I am going and what I am going for, and the words are the same ones I said in a yard on Weir Street at the ninth hour this morning, and I have asked you to write them down, and I am not asking you for anything else.*

She read it back, and then she wrote the hour against it, and then she did not put the pen down, which is the thing she does when she is going to say the second half.

"Two things and then I have to give the hour back to the paper," she said. "And the first one is that I have been a keeper of records for four years and I have never once in four years entered a statement like that in this office, and I have just done it, and it is going into the day-book and not into the press, because the day-book is what I say and the press is what people can be held to, and I have put a thing in the day-book that a person can be held to, on purpose, on a Wednesday morning, in Fallowmonth, and I would like it read back in about four years by somebody who thinks I was managing you."

"Write that down as well."

"I am not going to write that down," she said, "because that would be a second statement and you would have to read that one back too, and then we would be here until the light goes. One statement. Same words twice. That is the whole of the protection and you should know that the protection is the repetition and not the paper."

"And the second thing."

"And the second thing is that the instruction which forbids you contact names no law, requires no court, and cannot be refused, and it does not reach me, and it does not reach the yard, and it does not reach a bench of nine men, and it says nothing whatever about two people standing on a bridge at four o'clock in the afternoon putting a line into a hole in a parapet." She capped the ink. "I have been at this table for six days and I have been trying to work out whether I can be got at for being in this room at four o'clock, and I have worked it out, and here it is, and I am saying it now and not in a corridor in a year.

"I was here at four o'clock because I witness things that are set. That is my whole trade and it has been my whole trade for four years and I have done it for the miller and the coopers and a man who wanted a handrail seen to. There is nothing in this room about a bell and I am going to write down in the day-book that there was nothing in this room about a bell, with the hour on it, and if anybody comes up these stairs in a year and asks me what I did on the twenty-sixth of Longlight at the fourth hour, I will be able to answer in a form, and the form will be true, and the true form is that a woman who witnesses things watched a line go into a hole in a stone and nothing else happened, and that is the answer and it is the whole of it, and it is not a lie and it is not the whole of the truth and I have decided that a form which is true and not the whole of the truth is the only instrument a keeper of records has and I will not be without it."

"The bench has not voted for it," he said.

"The bench has not voted for it and the bench does not know it is happening and I have told you that I would not be asked to sign anything that says you went and I am not signing anything that says you went, and that is the last of it and it is at the fourth hour of the twenty-fourth."

He left at the twelfth hour with the bar over his shoulder and the tool bag, and the two stairs and the landing, and he did not go up a second flight, and he noticed that too.

---

At the third hour he was on the Old Toll deck with the reeve of Merefen, who had come in a cart from a council meeting and had brought a sheet of paper and a clerk's attitude, and with Mara Vey, who had come because she had said at the fourth hour on the sixth of Mudmonth that the bell would not be rung and had spent a hundred days being a woman who says things like that and had decided, somewhere in Longlight, that if she was going to be a keeper who witnessed things then she was going to be one in daylight and in front of two other people.

The reeve wrote it. She wrote it standing up on a bridge deck in a cart-skirt in a wind, and she read it back twice, and she read the second time with the pen in the ink and did not put it down, and what she read back was this:

*That on the twenty-sixth of Longlight, YR 312, at the fourth hour, a line of tarred hemp and a dressed stone were set in the fourth course of the near abutment of the Old Toll crossing of this town, by four men of a yard of this town, at the yard rate, on a work of the bench of this guild and not out of any trust, being the first of the two visits a year the bench voted on the fifteenth of Longlight, and that the work was put out by the bench's man and not by the road warden, and that the reeve of this town witnessed it and did not put it out, and that she has been asked to witness the setting of a thing and not the purpose of it and has witnessed the setting of it, and that the purpose of it is not entered and that she was not asked for it and did not offer it.*

"That last sentence is mine," said Reeve Aldis Fenn, "and it is the whole of nine years of this town. I have sat in that room nine years and everything anybody has ever put in front of me has been a thing with a purpose in it, and I have never once been asked to look at a stone and not at a reason. It has never been a comfortable thing to be asked and it is the first time in nine years."

Mara Vey put her hand under the reeve's and not over it, and said the hour, and did not say anything else, because a witness who improves is not a witness.

Then she did the thing she had said at the fourth hour she would do, in the filing room, in words, out loud, to two people and not one.

"I am entering the fact that a thing was set at the fourth hour of the twenty-sixth of Longlight, at the Old Toll crossing of this town, on a work of the bench of the Merefen bridge guild. I am entering it in the day-book of my office and in the roll of that bench, with the hour, in my own hand and the reeve's, and I am not entering the purpose of it, and there is no purpose of it in either book, and if a Crown's officer comes to this town in a year and asks me what I did at the fourth hour of the twenty-sixth of Longlight I will be able to answer in a form and the form is a true form and it is the whole of what was done in that room, and a man came up a stair and a woman went down one and four men worked on a bridge, and nothing else happened on that bridge on that day that I have any part in."

"Will they let you say it is the whole?" said Aren Kest.

"They will not let me say anything," said Mara Vey. "That is the second half of what I have learned this year and it took me the whole year. A witness does not get to certify the whole. A witness gets to certify the thing she saw, and the rest of it is somebody else's sentence, and if the somebody else is a Crown's officer then the rest of it is a Crown's, and the only defence a keeper of records has is that her half is exact."

---

At the second hour of the afternoon he was on the Old Toll deck with the bar and the bag, and the two men were still at the abutment with their barrow and their cold chisel and the sound of it came up four hundred yards of still air with the clarity that a dry month gives you, and he could see the rectangle coming up out of the moss as they went.

It was the fourth time the bed of that stone had been cleared in five months and he had watched the third of them.

The bell-shaped hole in the parapet was four feet from his left hand. There had been moss in it in Greenmonth and there was moss in it now, and the Crown's seal on the post had not run and would not run in a hundred days, and the wax had gone the colour of a dirty thumb.

He put the bag down and took out the notebook and wrote the hour, and then the second line, which was not about the seat.

*Twenty-sixth of Longlight, YR 312, second hour. Lowest water. Cold, no wind, felt it in the back of the left hand before the glove.*

*There is no seat and there is no glass and there is no money and the seat I have has been used twice and a man in a shed wrote that three is not a number he would sell. So I am going out on the second seat for a third time and I am going to write the third time down before I go, in this hand, on this page, with this hour on it, because if I do not write it down then the only person in the world who will know that I did it on purpose is me, and a fact that only one person holds is not a record.*

*And I have told two people the same sentence and one of them has written it and the other one has entered the fact of a line in a rule. And a third person is going to put a row of a page in her book at the fourth hour and the row is going to say there was nothing in the room about a bell, and that row is worth more to me than anything the other office two hundred miles off has got, and she is going to be asked about it in a form in a year and the form is going to be true.*

Then he wrote the third line, which was the one he had been carrying for four months and had only just found the words for.

*The seat is not the road. I have been treating a piece of glass in a stone as though it were a road for four months and it is not one. A road is two ends and a date and a person at each end who has agreed to be there, and everything in the middle is a way of holding the two ends to their promise. The glass is a way of holding the two ends. It is the cheapest way and it is the only way I know of, and it is not the road, and I have never once had to say that out loud because nobody has ever asked me to hold an end.*

He put the notebook away.

The sill in the fourth course was cold under his left glove. Two used discs, a pin, an oak collar, three blows in the head of the pin and a maker's mark under the near disc. He had cut that block out of the parapet himself on the eighth of Greenmonth with a chisel and a drill and his own hands, in six hours, and he had done it in the rain, and he had got the square off the Sedge Reach bench mark and not off anything else, and the quarter of an inch in that mark was on a county's schedule sheet in a remarks column in a building in the county town and was not in this town's book, and that had been the right way round in Longlight and it was still the right way round.

He put his hand flat on it and did not take anything out of it and did not expect anything, because a man who expects a thing out of a stone is a man who has stopped being a bridgewright and started being something else, and he had four hours of daylight and a bar and a bag and a woman in a filing room and a brother in a yard and nine men in a guild hall four hundred miles off, and none of the five of them was him.

The air came off the sill.

Not a sound. That is the thing nobody in this county has ever been able to put in a document and that he had stopped trying to put in one: it is not a sound, and it is not a warmth, and it is not a fold, and there is nothing in it at all that a man standing on a bridge with his hand on a stone could afterwards describe to a clerk. What there is, is that the stone stops being the only thing, and then it starts being the stone again, and the two of those happen in the same half-second and a man who is not braced for it goes down.

He had four seconds and a half last time. He counted them.

*One.* The bench's men and the barrow four hundred yards off, and the rectangle half out of the moss, and neither of them looking up.

*Two.* The bar in his right hand, because the bar is the only object he has ever carried into Bellweather-Living and it is the only thing in this county that knows the trade, and a man who crosses into a city he has been in twice without a bar is a man who has forgotten what the city is for.

*Three.* The Old Toll road going north out of the fen, four hundred yards of it, and the wharf kerb, and the footway, and the parapet, and the coping, and a cross that goes up a hill in a line about nine hundred years old, and a town that has said the chalk on a board every morning since the sixth of Mudmonth and will go on saying it while he is gone.

*Four.*

*Four and a half.*

Then the far end of it, and a river with a low water in it, and eleven sheets and a blanket on a line over a gallery, and somebody's coat, and the smell of hot glass and tar and wet wool, and a cold river with a wheel turning on it, and the north steps, and a girl of about fourteen with a basket of onions between her boots who was not the same girl and was the same age.

There was a bell on the coping of the north bank and it had a rope on it and nobody was near it.

He went up the steps to the farthing, which he had not this time, and the toll man took his coat and looked at his pockets and gave him the whiting strip and said the same thing about paper he had said twice before, and Aren Kest gave him a half mark for the ticket and a farthing for the gate and stood on the bottom step in a city that had been six months and twenty days running its own business and had not sent for him.

He had four hours of daylight left in the day he had, out of the day he did not have, and a woman with a crossing book who had been told twice to keep her hands open on the twenty-sixth of Longlight, and two men and a clerk who were somewhere below him on a ring stone with a rule, and nine men in a hall with a stone in the floor, and somebody four hundred yards away knocking moss out of a rectangle with a cold chisel and not knowing.
