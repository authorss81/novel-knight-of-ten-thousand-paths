# Chapter 0039

The twenty-first of Longlight was a Friday, and a Friday in Merefen is a market day, and the market that morning had forty-one stalls on four hundred yards of the Old Toll road and the Sedge Reach approach, which is a market that has been moved onto a bridge it was not built for, and a market that has been moved is a market that talks about itself, and that is what she went down to at the eighth hour to count.

She counted because she could not stop herself and because a keeper of records who has entered a report in a fair hand with a date and an hour on it is entitled to know how many people have heard it. That is not a service to the office. It is the one thing she is good at that nobody has ever paid her for.

The board had ten lines on it. Four had the clerk's rule over them: the day, the tide, the two notices about a culvert, and a line about a load of stone expected at the mill.

Six did not.

The four standing ones were there. *Ask Kest, he was gone.* *Road warden in the cart on Friday. Asked nobody anything.* And under the first, a small one in a different hand: *Ask him what the three and a half is for.* And last, on its own line and no longer new, because the clerk had written it twice since and neither time had been asked: *Road shut since the 19th of Mudmonth. Ask the road warden why.*

And there were two new ones, and both of them were about a bridge, and neither of them had a name in them, and both of them had been on the board long enough to have been wiped over and re-chalked by whoever put them there.

*The Crown marks a stone in the north twice a year. It was eleven on Sunday. It was eight at Harvest.*

*Bridge in the other world is going. Kest has been there. Kest is not saying.*

Mara Vey read the bottom line four times and then went and stood in a lane off the market with her back to a wall for about two minutes, which is what she does, and which in four years had been done perhaps eleven times.

---

The first version had come into the town on the eleventh of Longlight, off a carrier off the northern post, and it had been roughly true, and by the eighteenth it had been in four hundred mouths, which is a great many mouths for a fen town of this size, and the reason it had gone so far was that it was interesting rather than because anybody was frightened of it. A bridge in the north where a Crown's man comes twice a year and puts a number on a stone. That is a fact you would tell a man at a stall. It is not a story about a fever. It has no dead in it and no fever and no bell, and after eleven weeks of the law being on its side it was a relief to have a thing in the market that was a Crown's fault in a boring way.

The bottom line was four days old and it had been in thirty mouths and it had come from a man selling bracken who had heard it in a tavern on the seventeenth from a barge mate off the northern channel named **Harl Mounce**, and Mounce had not been in Merefen until the sixteenth, and there is a tap-room on Weir Street and it is the tap-room of this town.

The second version had the two halves of the town's whole life — a bridge in another world, and a man who has been to it — joined at the waist, and it was the single most attractive sentence anybody had said in Merefen since the nineteenth of Mudmonth, and the man who had said it was a bracken-seller with a grudge about a carter's season, and the sentence had spread at the rate of a thing that is a joke and not a fact, which is four times faster.

She went and found Mounce at the tenth hour at a table in the tap-room on Weir Street, eating, with a barge's chalk on his right forearm and a coat that had been wet eleven days out of thirteen, and she did not go alone, because she had learned something in Mudmonth about a woman with a press and a lamp walking up alone to a table in a room where a man is telling a story.

Sarn Oyelaran came. And before they came, Sarn had the outer room cleared, and the day-book open, and the office's paper out, and Mara Vey had written at the head of it the words that a keeper of records writes at the head of a page when she means to be exact and cannot be:

*Entered as reported. Limits stated in full. No opinion.*

"Your name," said Mara Vey.

"Harl Mounce."

"And you are a barge mate, and on what."

"Stone. Down from the north road, for a house in the county town that sets paving. Nine loads since Frostmonth." He had a good memory and he used it. "I was in at Gallow Ford on the second of Longlight with the ninth and the tide was wrong and we lay four days and then we were four more on the towpath because a Crown's man came over the second bridge at Gallow with a line and a rule and a boy with a book, and he marked it, and we lay while he did it, and nobody on that towpath said one word about it, and I sat on the coil of the painter and I looked at him for about a quarter of an hour, because I had nothing else to do and I was going to be paid for the four days either way."

"What did you see him do?"

"He marked a stone." Mounce said it the way a man says a thing he has had in his mouth for a fortnight and has been waiting for somebody to ask. "He came up on the bridge with a rule and a line and a boy with a book, and there were two other men with him, and they went down to the second pier on the downstream side and they had a mark cut in the coping where the water had worn it, and the Crown's man took the rule and put it in and wrote a number in the boy's book, and the boy read it back, and then they all three went to the other side and did the same to a different stone, and they were there about an hour and a half and then they went. That is what they do. They have done it twice a year as long as I have been on that water and I have never once seen anybody there but them and the two millers who live under it."

"How long have you been on that water?"

"Fourteen years. I was on that water before I was married."

"And the number in the boy's book. Did you see it."

"I did not go and read it," said Mounce, "and I have thought about that for six days and I would go back tomorrow and I would not, and you are going to write that I did not go and read it, because you will not write it if I do not say it and you are the kind of woman who will not put a thing in a book that a man has not told her."

"I will write that you did not go and read it, and I will write it in exactly those words, and thank you."

"The steward told me the number. That is how I have it. He was the one who let the barge through the second time and would not let it through the first, and he has been at that toll-house thirty years and he keeps the mark's book when they are not in it, because they leave the book and the boy takes it, and he does not read it, and he told me eleven and he told me it was eight at the last one." He stopped. "And he was talking about the number. He was not talking about a bridge going. I want that in it, because I have said the other thing in three rooms since Monday and I would like somebody to write down that I have said the other thing and not him."

"What is the other thing you have been saying?"

"That the bridge in the north where the Crown marks the stone is going." Mounce looked at the table. "It went from eight to eleven. That is three inches in a year. I have built nothing in my life and I have carried stone for fourteen years and I do not need anybody to tell me what three inches is when the number is the number and the number has been the number twice a year since I was twenty. I have seen a foundation go three inches in a year. And I said it in three rooms and I am saying it to you now, and I would say it again."

"You have not been paid for saying it."

"I have been paid for saying it," said Mounce. "That is the whole of the last five days. I have been out of work since I got off the water because the ninth was the last of it and the next thing on the list is a field job at two shillings the day and the man that owns me will not take a boat on the salt cut for nothing. Four of those six days I have had a load because people in this town wanted to know about a bridge, and one man in the tap-room on Monday put money in my hand and I have not spent it and I am not going to, and I am telling you that because you are going to find it out of the room in about a week and I would rather it was in the book."

Sarn Oyelaran wrote all of that down with the hour against it and read it back, and she made Mara Vey read the paragraph about the four days back to her, and then she signed under it, and Mara Vey put the fourpence on the table and entered the fourpence in the day's column and told her it was a fee for an hour and her time and not for the four days of work, and she took it and said it was the first honest wage she had had in six weeks and that she was not to be doing that again.

Then she asked him the four questions, and she asked them in the order she always asked them, and the third one is the one that makes a witness feel foolish and she apologised for it in plain words afterwards and has apologised for it every time since Mudmonth.

"How long was the barge lying at that toll-house."

"Eight days, and four of them on the towpath, and the eighth day was the day we went, and I have the master's note and the note is in the barge book at my mother's and I can get it and I will, and I would get it now if you wanted it."

"And what was the toll-house steward's name."

"I never asked him and I will not now, and I have thought about it on the water and I have decided that not asking is the right of it."

"Then I shall enter that this office does not have it," said Mara Vey, "and I shall enter the day you decided, and the hour, and the reason, and the reason is a good one and I am not going to improve it."

"And on the third question, which I am sorry for, and I am going to ask it and then I am going to apologise and then I am going to ask it again, because you have been in three rooms this week and I have to know the order.

"**What did he say, in the order he said it?**"

Mounce stopped and thought about it, and it took him a real amount of thinking, and Sarn Oyelaran's pen did not move.

"He said: the Crown's been marking the north crossing two year since before your father. He said: they mark the second pier. He said: the ring's gone off a bit since the last one. And then I said, how much, and he said eleven, and I said, and what was it, and he said eight." Mounce's hand went flat on the table. "He never said the word *bridge*. He said the *pier*, and he said the *ring*, and he said *gone off a bit*. I said bridge. He said pier. I said crack. He said ring. I said going. He did not say going. **He has not said going once in my hearing and I have not said it in his.** I said it on Monday night in this room to a man with bracken and he is not a man who would have known the difference, and I have said it twice since, and I would like it written that I have said it four times and that the man it came from has never been asked."

"Write that," said Mara Vey, "exactly as he has said it, and do not improve one word of it, and I am sorry for the third question, and I am going to say the reason for it now, once, because you have earned it and because in four years I have never told a witness why I ask the same question twice.

"The third question is not about the toll house. The third question is about the *order*. And the order is the whole of what a keeper of records does, and there is no instrument in this kingdom that can order a man's mouth and there is no witness in this county that cannot be got at, and the only thing a record can do is hold the order of things down. And the order is the only part of a report that anybody can check, and everything else in it is a man's opinion of what he heard, and a man's opinion is not worthless but it is not a record, and if a keeper does not say so in the room, then she has a file of opinions and not a file of facts, and in five years somebody will read the file of opinions and say *this office did not understand its own work*."

Harl Mounce said, "That is the first time anybody has said no to me about anything since the ninth of Longlight," and she said, "I know, and I am sorry, and it is true."

---

Then she wrote the report out, and it took her until the fourth hour of the afternoon, and it is the longest single entry this office has ever made in a day-book and it is nine hundred words and it is in the fair hand, and it is the only thing she did that day.

It went in with the date and the hour and the source and the name of the man who had said the wrong one, and it went in as *reported*, and it stated its limits in full, and the limits are what she would have to read out in any room in ten years and she wrote them for that room and not for this one.

*Reported. That on the second of Longlight, YR 312, a barge of stone lying at the toll-house at Gallow Ford, on the northern road, was held four days on a towpath, and that during that time two men and a boy with a book attended at the second pier of a bridge at that toll-house, on the downstream side, and that a number was taken with a rule against a mark cut in the coping, and entered in the boy's book.*

*That the toll-house steward of that house, of thirty years' standing, stated to the barge mate that the number was eleven, and that at the previous marking it was eight.*

*That this office has no power to make a bridge sound or to stop one. It has entered a date, a place, four days, a number, a number it was told, and the name of the man who told it, and the name of the man who did not go and read it.*

*That the report is filed as reported and is not offered as evidence of anything.*

*And this office enters the following limits on its own account, and does not ask any person to agree with them.*

*One. That the marks at that crossing are made twice a year, and this office has been told so by a man who has been on that water fourteen years and not by any person who has kept the marks' book.*

*Two. That the number of eight and the number of eleven were given to a barge mate by a toll-house keeper in conversation on a wharf and were not written down at the time, and that the two numbers are in the mouth of one man and are in the book of no person, and that the man who took them has never seen the book and will not be able to see it.*

*Three. That this office does not know whether the two numbers were taken at the same season. It has been told that the marks are made twice a year and it has been told that the first was eight and the second eleven, and it has been told nothing whatever about which of the two marks of the year each number was taken at, and this office is a keeper of records and is obliged to say in its own hand that it does not know, and does not know it after asking a man at a table for an hour, and that a bridgewright in this county would know in a quarter of an hour and that this office has not asked a bridgewright, and has a reason for not having asked a bridgewright, and the reason is that there is one man in this county who could be asked and that he is not in this office and that she does not have him.*

*Four. That the name of the crossing is not in this report, and was not given, and that the toll-house at which the man lay is not named in this office's file in this matter, and that this office has not entered the name of the crossing in this file and is not going to until a person comes up this stair with a name and a date and a reason, and that this office has heard the name of a crossing in a market in this town in the last six days and has not written it in this file and is not going to.*

*Five. And this office enters the fifth limit on its own account and it is the limit that the four above it were written to reach, and it is a hard one, and it was arrived at at about the fifth hour of the afternoon by a copyist of twenty-six years old who is not to be blamed for it because she wrote it out of the four before it and did not see it coming.*

*That the description in this report — a mark cut in coping, two men and a boy with a book, a rule, a number taken twice a year, the second pier, a toll-house on a road with carts on it — is a description of a Crown's office doing a Crown's work at a bridge in this country, and that this office holds it against nobody.*

*And that the same description, word for word, is a description of a bench of a guild marking a ring on a pier twice a year at the lowest water with two men and a clerk, in a city that this county's own finding of the second of Greenmonth records as not built and not maintained, and that no person has said the two are the same bridge and no person in this county is able to say that they are not.*

*And that a keeper of records may not enter an opinion about it, and has not, and that the consequence of not entering one is that a report which is the only news anybody in this county has had out of that city in eleven weeks is, in this office's own hand, of a bridge nobody can name, on a road nobody can name, and cannot be used by anybody in this county to do anything at all, including the man it is about, who is the only person in this county who could say which of the two it is, and whom this office did not ask, and did not ask on purpose, because he came up this stair on the sixteenth with a document for a writ of enquiry in it and this office is not asking him a second thing in the same week while a writ is running.*

*And the day and the hour at which this office decided not to ask him, which is the fourth hour of the twenty-first of Longlight, and which is entered in the day-book and not in the press.*

That last clause was the one that cost her, and she wrote it at the fifth hour and a half and read it back twice, and it is the only sentence in nine hundred words that is about herself, and it is the sentence that means *there is a report in this town about a bridge in a world that this county's own file says was never built, and this office is the office people would bring it to, and this office is not writing the name down, and the town is writing it on a board, and one of the two will still be there in a hundred years.*

---

She went down to the market again at the sixth hour because she could not help it, and the bottom line had still got no rule over it, and the number of mouths was the only measure in this county that moved at all: of the four hundred who had heard the first version in the week after the eleventh, about thirty had heard the second, and every one of the thirty had heard it in a room, and not one of them had heard it from the man who heard the steward. She stood at the edge of it and listened for about a quarter of an hour, and what she heard, in the end, in three separate conversations, was not the story.

It was the joke.

The joke was this. It had started on the sixteenth and had gone round the town every day since, and it was not cruel, which was the part that undid her, because there is a way to be cruel about a thing and it is survivable, and there is a way to be *warm* about a thing that is going to kill a reputation in nine years and cannot be answered.

It went like this. A man would say to another man, in a lane, by way of saying something else entirely:

*Ask the keeper. The keeper keeps everything. The keeper'll have it written down with the hour on it.*

And the other man would say:

*The keeper charges by the sheet.*

And both of them would laugh, and it was a good joke, and it was true — the Archive really did have the hour on it, and the Archive really did charge — and it had been a good joke about a filing system since the sixth of Mudmonth, and since the seventeenth it had been about a bridge in a world where a Crown's man marks a stone, and the joke had got a *name* in it, because *keeper* is a word this town now uses for three different people and the market does not distinguish them and the market is not going to.

A keeper of records on a second floor. A road warden on a bridge. And, in a Crown's own paper, an office of keeper of the Bellweather hinge in a matter of which the province's own clerk had written a return, four days away, in a form, in answer to nothing.

The same word. The ordinaryest word in the language. The cheapest joke in the county. And in about four years, in a room two hundred miles off, somebody was going to use it.

---

She went back up the stair at the seventh hour and Sarn Oyelaran was standing in the filing room with a sheet of paper in her hand and her face doing the thing.

"Ma'am. The report is in. And there is a thing I have not put anywhere, because you told me the rule, and I want it said in a room and not in a book, and I would like you to tell me whether I am allowed to say it in a room."

"Go on."

"The counterfoil is in the press. It has been in that press since the thirty-first of Mudmonth under a claim number with a date and a chain of custody with three hands on it. And it is a stub of a writ of severance tendered against a crossing, and it came out of a creditor's register in a city four days off, and the name of the city is not on it and the name of the crossing is not on it and no person on this earth but him can tell you what it is.

"And the thing Harl Mounce said this morning is a report that a Crown's office takes a mark and a number twice a year off the second pier of a bridge at a toll-house on the northern road, and has done for at least fourteen years, and that the number has gone from eight to eleven."

"Two documents," said Mara Vey.

"Two documents, in this room, in this building, in two presses, under two different headings, entered on two different days, by two different hands, and they have never been in the same room." Sarn put the paper down. "And I have not put them together. And I want it said that I wanted to and that I did not, and that the reason I did not is that I could not write the sentence. I have tried it four times since the fourth hour and I can make it into one of three things and every one of the three is an opinion and not a record, and I am a copyist and I have been taught by a wall with a rule on it in a fair hand that a thing is entered on the word of a sworn tradesman and not on a clerk's word, and I have been taught it in this room by you, and I am not going to be the first person in four years to put a speech in this office's book because I was in a room where two facts were near each other."

"Go on," said Mara Vey. "Say the three sentences."

Sarn Oyelaran read them off the sheet.

"One. *That an office of the Crown's registry keeps a schedule of marks upon a crossing at a toll-house on the northern road, twice a year, and that the county's finding of the second of Greenmonth records that the crossing and its dependent places was not built and is not maintained.* That is two documents and a finding and it is true and it is an inch and a half of paper.

"Two. *That the volume entered on the press register as containing the schedule of anchors at Alder Vale is not on its shelf, and its removal is entered with the date of the nineteenth of Mudmonth, YR 301, and no authority and no office and no initial.* That is a fact she wrote in a fair hand on the fifteenth of Greenmonth and it is worth nothing yet and it is the only document in this county that anybody can look for.

"Three. *That a bell was lifted out of a river in the three hundred and second year, one year after the date of that removal, and entered on a Crown clearance roll as unclassified salvage, of nine stone by the founder's weight, with no city entered and no river entered.*"

Nobody in the room said anything.

"Three sentences and every one of them can be checked by a man who does not trust this office," said Sarn Oyelaran, "and not one of them says what it means, and I cannot get from them to what it means without writing a fourth sentence, and the fourth sentence is a motive, and I sat in this room on the twenty-first of Greenmonth and heard a man in a counting house four days off say *you will not say a motive, not in this room, not in the north, not in the middle*, and I wrote that down in a fair hand in a press and I did not agree with it at the time and I agree with it now, and I am telling you that I am a copyist of twenty-six years old and I have wanted to write a speech since the ninth of Greenmonth and I have not written it and I would like it entered that I did not write it."

"It is entered," said Mara Vey, "and I am going to add one line, and the line is mine and not yours, and it goes in the day-book and not in the press.

"Because you are right, and I want to say the reason, and the reason is not a rule. If I put those two documents in one room and write a sentence about them, then in five years there will be a document in this building with a Crown's registry, a crossing, and a bell in it, in one hand, in one claim, and it will be the best piece of work this office has ever done. And in five years a man in a provincial office will read it and he will not care what I meant, he will care that a keeper of records in Merefen had the registry, the crossing and the bell in one file and had nothing to say about it.

"And I have been right four times in four months and I told Sarn Oyelaran in this room on the sixteenth of Greenmonth that the arithmetic of a woman who is right four times and has never been wrong once is a woman who has begun to price it, and I have watched myself do it, and I did not notice until about an hour ago, and I am not going to do it in a file."

She took a sheet of the office's own paper and wrote, in the fair hand, in the day-book and not in the press:

*That this office holds a counterfoil under a claim number of the thirty-first of Mudmonth, YR 312, in respect of a writ of severance, in a chain of custody with three hands on it.*

*And that on the twenty-first of Longlight, YR 312, at the fourth hour, at the third hour of the afternoon, this office entered a report in respect of a Crown mark and a number twice a year upon a crossing at a toll-house on the northern road.*

*And that these two documents are in this building and have never been in the same room, and that this office has not placed them in the same room, and does not propose to.*

*And that there is a person in this county who could be shown both, and that this office knows who he is, and does not name him in any file, because to name him in a file would be to charge him with something, and this office does not charge people with things it has not proved, and has been held to that in this building since the sixth of Mudmonth by a woman with a rule on a wall, and is holding to it now against a report from a barge mate and a form from a clerk in a provincial town.*

"Read it back," said Sarn Oyelaran.

"It is four lines and it says nothing," said Mara Vey, "and it is going to be the most important page in this office's day-book for a year, and nobody who reads it will know that, and that is what a day-book is for. Read it back and initial it and put the hour on it, and then go home, and then come back at the seventh hour tomorrow, because I want to do the return of the ninth of Rainmonth and I want somebody here who has read all three of the sheets when I do it."

Sarn Oyelaran read it back, and initialled it, and put the hour on it, and went home, and the filing room over a salt merchant in Merefen went dark for the night with a report about a bridge in a world that does not exist in it, and a stub of a writ that was refused on a deck eleven years ago, and a sentence on a chalked stair in a lane that said *ask the keeper, the keeper'll have it written down with the hour on it*, and nobody in the town had the least idea that any of the three had anything to do with the other two, and the only person in Merefen who did was on a stair with a lamp in her hand, and she had written the reason down in a fair hand with a date on it, and the reason was that being right four times is a woman who has begun to price it, and she had started pricing it, and she had noticed about an hour ago, and in this county that was the only thing anyone had done all day that was not in anybody's account.
