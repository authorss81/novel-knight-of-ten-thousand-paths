# Chapter 0050

The last week of Fallowmonth is bridge season, and the river is down, and in a fen county of a thousand people there is one week in the year when the oldest bridge in the county can be got at, and in that week a town that has been shut since the nineteenth of Mudmonth found out it had not in fact been shut at all.

The clerk on the Market Square board wrote the day at the top of the board on the twenty-second, under a rule, with the tide, the culvert at Four Elms, and the load of stone expected at the mill.

*Bell to the Old Toll, Thurs 24th, at the lowest water. The town is doing it. The bench voted. Bring nothing.*

Under it, without a rule, somebody had already chalked: *Ask the keeper. The keeper'll have it written down with the hour on it.*

Mara Vey looked at that for about four seconds and then went up the stair and did not write it down anywhere, because it is not a record and because she has four years of evidence that a corrected market story is a market story with a correction in it.

Captain Rell laid it down on the twenty-fourth at about the fourth hour in the afternoon, in daylight, in front of about three hundred people, and he did it himself, and he carried it himself, and there were two men of the watch with him and a barrow and a chain and a roll of chain paper, and he made the chain of custody on the roll in front of the crowd and read it out loud as he did it, because he is the man who made a chain of custody unbroken on purpose eleven months ago and told a road warden about it on a step in the rain.

The bell came out of the room in the watch house where it had stood since the tenth of Longlight, nine stone, split lip to shoulder, a founder's mark on the shoulder that three men in Merefen read that afternoon and one of them got right and one of them got the year wrong.

And here is the whole of what the town did, and it is nine lines in a clerk's roll in the guild house over the wharf, and it took a bench of nine three sittings and a reeve and a magistrate and a Crown's watch captain, and about six weeks, and none of it was a speech:

*That the bed of the stone at the abutment be dressed and a cradle new-cut and set, being a defect in a parapet upon a crossing which this bench is keeping in repair at its own cost and its own men's time by a resolution of the fifteenth of Longlight;*

*and that upon that cradle be set the iron carried upon this bench's own scrap roll of the eleventh of Mudmonth, YR 312, at a valuation of six marks, as unclassified municipal salvage, and being nine stone of bell metal with a split in it, no mark of ownership entered, no person claiming, and no office of the Crown having produced any authority for the taking of it in eleven months, the asking being dated the fourth of Greenmonth and unanswered;*

*and that the watch of this county be requested to lay down its custody of the said iron upon this bench, the same being a laying down and not a removal, and the same being within the terms of the finding of this county's court of the fourth of Greenmonth, which found the custody lawful and did not find the metal to be any person's;*

*and that the bench records that it does this on its own authority and by no one's leave, and that it has asked nobody's leave, and that the cost is the bench's, and that no toll of the crossing has moved in eleven months and none of them will move today.*

And Captain Rell put the minute in his own hand on the back of his chain paper and gave a true copy of it to the magistrate's clerk, and gave another to the toll-holder, and kept the third, and it is four lines long and it is in a watch captain's record in a county file and it is the shortest thing he has ever written.

*I laid down a custody of an object which the finding of the fourth of Greenmonth found to be lawfully held, upon the ground of a repair ordered by a bench of this town in respect of a defect in a parapet, and upon the bench's own roll entry of the eleventh of Mudmonth, and the object is unclassified salvage and no person claims it.*

*I have not been instructed. I have not been asked. I was told on the twenty-second of Fallowmonth that a bench had voted and that the roll had an entry on it and that if I wished to dispute the matter I should come to the guild house at the ninth hour on the twenty-third, and nobody came, and I have entered that in this roll with the hour.*

*And if any officer of any office above this county thinks that wrong, he may come and take it up off this bridge, and I will put it in his hands myself, and I will say in a room that I did it, and I have said the same thing in a returnable order in my own hand in the fifteenth of Longlight and had a clerk's refusal on the back of it, and I have been right about both, and I have been right about this one as well.*

*There is one thing I want in a record and it is that I believed the fever for eleven months and that on the twenty-second of Fallowmonth I laid down a thing I had no lawful reason to lay down, and that I did it because a bench in this town had a line in a book with a date on it and the office that sealed my order could not produce the order to me, and a watch captain is a man who fills in forms, and the whole of my trade is the order in which the forms come.*

He read that back to the crowd from the parapet, himself, with the bell standing on a barrow behind him, and about nine people took it in and about thirty people argued about it in the market for a month, and one of the nine was a man with a notebook who wrote it out word for word and put it into a filing room over a salt merchant, and the keeper of that filing room entered it in a fair hand with the hour and entered, on the same page and against nobody, that she was not going to correct it, and entered the reason, which is that Captain Rell had read a document out loud from a bridge to three hundred people and the crowd had heard it and a document a crowd has heard is a document.

The band is the town's work. That is the first thing anybody asks and the answer is that the band is the town's, and the new iron came out of a gate-post at the end of Weir Street that the town has been arguing about for two years, and the man who cut it was a blacksmith in a lane who has cut a band for a cracked bell before, in Embermonth of the year before, for the same bell, when it hung on a cradle on the same bridge and the council could not decide whether to scrap it, and the band went on and came off and went on and came off, and this time it went on and stayed.

Six inches wide, riveted, forty-one rivets, and the guild's man with the levels set it himself and would not let anybody else near it, and he is a man who has refused a quarter of an inch onto the guild's roll and put it onto a county's sheet, and he set a band to his own book and not to anybody's figure, and when a man in a lane said *that'll hold a year* he said, *it'll hold as long as the crack does and the crack is not a crack, it's a split, and a band holds a split, and the difference is in the words.*

**And the split is sealed on the far side of the scheduled crossing, and not by him, and not in Merefen, and not by anybody in this county.**

The sealing is a trade of that city and of no other place in the world, and a woman of about fifty with iron dust in the creases of her hands did it on the far side of a line on the morning of the twenty-fourth, and there are four lines about it on a slate and in a book, and the four lines are these:

*A split bell is not mended. It is sealed, and the sealing is poured hot into the split from both sides at once and it is dull where it sets and it is red where it sets, and it is not bright and it must not be bright, because a bright repair is a repair somebody can see and a dull one is a repair that has been agreed to.*

*And it takes two hands, one on each side, at the same hour, and the two hands are of one trade, and this is the only working in our city that cannot be done by a person on their own, and we have argued about it for four generations and we have got it right.*

*And it is sealed with a thing that is not metal and not pitch, and it is a word, and the word is the word a bench uses when it says it will answer first, and the word is put into the sealing and not said out loud, and if it were said out loud it would be a speech and this is not a speech.*

*And the woman of Hushwater said, standing on the deck of the great arch in the middle of a market day with about two hundred people on it: **we are not mending a bell. We are closing a split that a thing came through in a storm in this city eleven years ago, and we are closing it from both sides, and there is a man in a fen county four hundred miles off who is not allowed to be on this deck tonight and is not going to be, and he has been told so, and he has not argued.***

Mara Vey was in the room at the fourth hour on the twenty-fourth and read the four lines off the slate afterwards with the hour on them, and asked the question she asks once and apologises for.

"And nobody on the far side has ever seen that bell sealed before?"

"Nobody," said the ferrywright. "I have been doing it since I was nineteen and I have done four of them and the fourth was this one. We seal a split in this city about twice in a generation and we seal it when the split is going to take somebody's hand off. **That is the whole of it. It is not an honour and it is not a treaty and it is not a road. It is a job in a trade that two people have to be able to do at the same time, and the reason the north man is not on the deck is not politics, it is that a person who is not of the trade cannot stand on that deck and be safe, and I will not have a man on that deck for a speech.**"

And nothing in that bell broke again.

The channel opened on the twenty-fourth, on a named day, and the naming is the whole of the new arrangement and it is in four books in two countries.

A **Merefen steward**: Tovan Kest, carpenter, who keeps the crossings and who is not the man who cut the seat and does not want to be.
A **Bellweather-Living steward**: Sera Quen, ferrywright, who keeps the road and whose consent is hers and not a bench's and cannot be argued with in a hall.
An **independent witness at each end**: Reeve Aldis Fenn at the Merefen end, and the toll-clerk at the north end, who is a man of about sixty in that city and who has never once in his life been asked anything and who said no to being asked twice before he said yes, and the reason he gave the second time was that the first reason was that he did not know the man, and the second was that he did know the man and a man you know is not a witness, and a man you have never met is.

The day is written at the head of a page in a book on the north bank and in the roll of the Merefen bridge guild and in the day-book of a filing room over a salt merchant and in a slate in two hands, and the hour is the fourth, and the tide is in it, and there is a space on the page for the weather, and the space is filled in by the person who is there.

The first thing that came across it, on the twenty-fourth of Fallowmonth, at about the fifth hour of the afternoon, in daylight, in front of about forty people who had not come to see anything, was not a message.

It was a dispute about a load of paving stone.

A man of the fourth camp had bought nine tons of setts out of a yard in the upper valley in this world and had carried them in his own boat, and the toll-man on the north steps had taken a farthing on the gate and a half mark on the ticket, and the man of the fourth camp had said the setts were for a camp and a camp is not a crossing and the toll on a crossing is on crossings, and the toll-man had said the setts came over this arch and everything that comes over this arch is a crossing, and the man had said the farthing was a gate and he had not come through a gate, and the toll-man had said he came up the steps and a man who comes up the steps has crossed and is lying about it, and he did not charge him twice and he charged him once.

They argued it out in two languages for about eleven minutes at the fifth hour of the afternoon on a bridge, with a slate and a book and a toll-man of forty and a sett-cutter with a broken thumb, and the ferrywright wrote down the whole of it in the crossing book because it is a crossing book and that is what it is for, and at the end of it the two of them agreed a thing that neither of them would have agreed in Fallowmonth.

**Nine tons of setts, in a cart, over the crown, at the low water, one farthing on the gate and no ticket, because a cart on wheels is not a man and a man who comes up the steps is not a cart.** And the toll-man entered it, and the ferrywright entered it, and it is line ninety-one of a crossing book, and it is the first line in that book in eleven years that has two languages in it, and about nine people in a market in Merefen asked afterwards what had been said and were told it was about a load of paving stone, and did not believe it, and were right not to.

**Edric Hunn** came to Merefen on the twenty-seventh of Fallowmonth.

He came in daylight, on the county's road, in a plain coat, with one man behind him, and he entered the county court at the second hour of the afternoon of the twenty-seventh and asked, in his own voice, for the court to be made, and a court *was* made, which is what a magistrate may do in her own room, and the room was not the court's, and the clerk made a minute of it with the hour and the names, and the names are in a county file two hundred miles from that room for ever.

There is no officer of any office above that county present. He is not an officer. He is a marshal of a Crown and he came in a plain coat and he asked to be recorded, and being recorded is the single most frightening thing anybody can do to a man like him and he did it on purpose.

**Rowan Petch** came down the road with him and had entered it, as a provincial clerk, in his own hand, on the day, on a sheet of provincial paper, with the hour: *that a person giving his name as a marshal of the Crown, and stating that he holds the office of marshal and the office of the Lantern Council's emergency office, and the office of the marshal's emergency office which sealed an instrument in the matter of the crossing of Alder Vale in the three hundred and first year, was admitted and was recorded, and that the said person was not on a warrant and was not under arrest, and that the county has no power to detain any person.*

So the marshal's name went into a county file in a sentence, in a clerk's hand, in a room with people in it, and that has never happened in this county before and it is now the fourth document in this county that carries it, and the other three are the paper in a room over a cooper, the finding of the fourth of Greenmonth, and the back of a returned order in a guild roll.

He was entirely reasonable. He was reasonable about the fever, in the sense that he never once said the fever did not exist, and he was right about almost everything, and he offered the room the thing that the room wanted.

**Rank.** Restored, by brevet, effective from the date of the original finding, with the eleven years' arrears.

**Back pay.** The full of it, and it is a great deal more than eleven marks and fourpence, and it is a figure that would have taken a road warden in Merefen out of the room above a cooper for the rest of his life.

**A legal shield for the Bellweather Claims Archive** against the writ of enquiry and any instrument of the like, in the form of a letter from an office that could not produce its own authority, which is the single most valuable document in this county and the only one anybody has ever wanted from him.

**And the route.** And an inspection of Bellweather-Living. Not a garrison. An inspection. A Crown's man and a clerk and a writ of production and a year, and the whole of the north's trade, and the three toll houses on the north road reopened, and the northern question answered, and the city's dead made into a Crown's case, and every man in this room given the thing they had wanted since the sixth of Mudmonth.

"I have come four hundred miles," he said, "and I have come on a named day with a minute, and I have asked to be recorded, and I want it understood that I am not here to bargain in a corridor with a man who cannot come up a street. **Everything I have is on this table and I have put it on this table in a room with a clerk in it, and there is a magistrate in the room and her sister is in the room, and I have known for four years that a thing said in a room with a clerk in it is a thing a court has to answer.**"

And then Aren Kest said no.

He said it in about ninety seconds and he did it in the trade's terms and he did not once say the word *no* until the end, and the clerk wrote it down, and it went into the record of a court in a county that is two hundred miles from the place he was born, and it is the only time in eleven years that anybody in this county has heard him refuse anything at length.

**Aren Kest**: "You have come four hundred miles and you have offered me a thing I asked for at seventeen, and a thing I was refused at twenty-one, and eleven years of money, and a shield for the only office in this county that has ever answered a question put to it, and a road, and I am going to say the thing about the road first because it is the only one of the four that is not a kindness.

"You are right. I have a road and no constitution. That is not a complaint. That is the only reason I am dangerous and I have known it since the nineteenth of Mudmonth and I have written it down twice in eleven years in documents you have never seen. **A man with one road and nothing else is a thing that has to be shut, and you are the only man in this kingdom who has ever been in a room where that was argued out, and you shut it eleven years ago with a piece of paper in a hand laid flat on a book, and the hand has not moved and neither have you.** So when you say that a man with a private road and no constitution is a public danger, I am not going to stand up in this room and tell a marshal of a Crown that he is wrong. He is right. He is entirely right, and he is going to be right about me whatever I say in the next two minutes, and that is the reason the answer is not an argument and it is a trade.

"A constitution is not a charter. A constitution is a set of people who can say no to a road, and say it without a court, and be believed, and go on living in the town afterwards. In this county there are four such people and one of them is a woman with a press who has been under a writ for thirty days and has entered a claim against nobody and has not once been compelled, and one of them is a watch captain who laid down a thing he had no lawful reason to lay down on a bridge on the twenty-fourth of Fallowmonth in front of three hundred people, and one of them is a carpenter with a rule and a book, and one of them is me.

"**That is not a constitution. It is four people in a fen town, and it is eleven years old, and one of the four is a woman who is a stranger here and two of the others are the reason this county has any records at all.** It is going to take me the rest of my life and I am not going to have you hand me a rank instead of it in a room with a clerk in it, because a rank is the thing that was done to me at twenty-one and it is the reason there is a bell in a county file under two seals and a date on it, and I have spent eleven years being the best man in the room and you have spent eleven years being right about why, and neither of those things should happen to a town again.

"Take the rank. No. Take the back pay. I will take the back pay and I will take it as a wage, in full, on a schedule, with a date, and I will not call it back pay and I will not let anybody in this county call it back pay, because back pay is a thing a man is given for eleven years of a service that a Crown's office ran, and I was not serving the office, I was serving the men who sent me, and they are in this county, and I have a page in a yard's book about a handrail I was not asked to pay for, and it is better than the arrears.

"Take the shield for the Archive. I want it. I have wanted it since the ninth of Longlight and I have wanted it for a woman who has not asked me for it and will not. **Do not give it to me. Give it to the bench, in a form, with a number on it, and let her enter it against no person in her own hand and let the county clerk's refusal stand beside it, because the shield and the refusal in the same file is the only document in this county that is worth having, and the shield on its own is a letter from a room that could not produce its own authority, and in four years somebody is going to notice that and it will be the day the whole of it goes.**

"And the inspection. You want a man and a clerk and a writ of production and a year, into a city of four thousand people that has not asked to be in anybody's book, that has lost three toll houses and a hundred and twenty marks of roll and a quarter of its gate bridge, and that has a bench of nine that renewed an oath in a hall in front of two hundred people, and that has a woman who writes in a book, and that has twice told this office no and been right both times. You are asking to go in and take a page out of a book and call it a record, and you have done that before, and you did it in this kingdom in the three hundred and first year, and the page you took was a refusal, and the refusal is in a press over a salt merchant, and the office that sent the writ is the office you are sitting in, and the register page of it came south four hundred miles on a line and two hands on the first of Fallowmonth and is in a claim with a number on it and a name on a separate sheet with a separate number on it.

"So I am going to say the last thing and then I am going to stop talking, and I want it entered that I said it and that I have no feeling about it. **I cannot choose what that city does. I have been in it three times and the third time I spent the night in the rain on a scaffold with nineteen men I had not met and I was the man they asked how much of a cutwater, and I was not in the room when the narrow oath was made and I did not make it and I could not have stopped it. If you go in with a writ you will get what a writ gets in a city with a bench and a debt and a grandmother's granddaughter's name on the front of a book, and it will not be a record, and there is no version of an inspection of that city that is not an argument with a creditor in it, and the argument is a war, and a man in a counting house in that city told me so in a room in the middle of my first week and I did not believe him and he was right and I have been wrong about it for six months and twenty days.**"

The room was quiet for a length of time that the clerk recorded.

Then Lysa Fenn said the thing she had said out loud in a filing room in Greenmonth, in the last sentence, and in the exact procedure, and this time it was not procedural:

"And the marshal has been refused, and the refusal is in a minute in this room with the hour on it, and the marshal asked to be recorded and was recorded, and the county has no power to detain any person, and **he is at liberty to leave, and he is at liberty to write, and he has asked in a letter of the eighth of Longlight for the list of those to whom this road warden has spoken, and that request is refused in the same minute, in the same room, on the record, by the road warden, in the words *I will give you the offices, the dates and the numbers, and not one name*, and a minute of a room is a thing a court has to answer, and there is a warrant in that office's file against a woman with a press which names a woman without a name, and that warrant is dated and this county asked about it and was not answered, and the asking is dated the fourth of Greenmonth, and it is now the twenty-seventh of Fallowmonth and nothing has been answered.**"

And the marshal of a Crown picked up his hat, and said the last thing, and it was in his own voice and it was not a threat, and it is in the record because Rowan Petch wrote it down and would not leave it out and was thanked for that by a magistrate in a corridor afterwards, in plain words, which is the only thank you that office has ever given anybody.

"Then I have been refused by a county," he said, "and I have been refused by a trade, and I have been refused by a man who is better at the thing I trained him in than I was in the year I trained him, and I have been right about all three, and I would like it noticed that I have come here in daylight with a minute and I was right, and that is the first time in a year that anything I have done has been right and cheap."

Then he said, to nobody, in the doorway, the thing that the whole of Volume 01 turns on and that he said to a room and not to a man:

"A road that cannot be refused is a road that will be used. **Somebody has to be able to shut it, and I have been the somebody for eleven years and I have shut it once and I did it with a piece of paper and a boy's name on it, and I would like somebody in this room to tell me, now, today, with a clerk writing it down, that there is another somebody, and that he is not you.**"

And there was, and it was a carpenter, and a watch captain, and a woman with a press, and a reeve, and a bench of nine in a room over a wharf, and four people in a city four hundred miles off, and a woman with a book on a north bank, and a woman with a rule and a bench order and a mark in a coping.

The claim was filed on the twenty-eighth of Fallowmonth at the eleventh hour, in the filing room, in the fair hand, with its number against it and the hour, and two copies went out of the building on the twenty-ninth, one by lending to a creditor's chest under a counter and one by transaction to a lock-up on a canal wharf in Harvestmonth at a farthing of salt, and the fourth place was a press over a salt merchant in a building with a door, and the bar of the ninth of Longlight came off the office on the twenty-eighth of Fallowmonth on an endorsement, on a stamp, at the second hour, in a form, because the thirtieth day was up and the court had what it wanted, and the form was answered in the spaces and there was no blank in it and there were no grounds of complaint against anybody and there is a copy in a county file two hundred miles off.

**And the brine weights stayed where they were.**

They are in that building. They have been in that building for four years. They are cited in the claim of the seventh of Fallowmonth and in eleven documents and in a magistrate's finding and they are **not filed and not an exhibit and not part of the claim**, and the wrapper in the counter drawer says so, in a copyist's hand, in an order, in about ninety words, and it is the only piece of paper in that room that anybody has ever written and not been paid for.

Mara Vey looked at the drawer for a while on the twenty-eighth, with the claim filed and the four places sent, and then she shut it and wrote in the day-book, against nobody, with the hour, the second of the two lines about the weights, and it is the third entry in four years that this office has put on its own account and against nobody, and it is this:

*That the roll of the dead is not built, and the arithmetic that would build it is done and is in a drawer and has been since the second of Fallowmonth, and that the reason is entered in full twice in this book and the two entries are four lines apart, and that the keeper of this office has read both of them today in the hour in which the claim was filed, and has read them twice, and has not been able to improve either, and has not tried, and does not intend to try, and would like anybody who reads this in eighty years to know that the not-building was a decision and not an oversight, and that it was a decision about who the dead belong to, and that she is not the Crown and is not the city and is a keeper of records in a fen town and the roll is the whole of the case and the roll belongs to the people it is made of.*

Then she went and read the served schedule of the eighth of Fallowmonth one more time, which is a sheet with a claim number on it and eight ruled lines and seven filled.

Alder Cross, two hundred. Saltbrim, ninety. Wrenlow, sixty. Cobbett, a hundred and forty. Nine Elms, a hundred and ten. Stonebarrow, forty. Hushwater, eighty.

*Sum not stated.*

And the eighth line, ruled, empty, the rule still on it, the space where a figure would go, and a boy of nineteen had copied the reason under it and initialled it and it is the only handwriting in that document that belongs to a person who will still be alive in eleven years.

**It was still empty.**

She noticed it on the twenty-ninth of Fallowmonth at the seventh hour in the morning, standing up, with a lamp in her hand and nobody in the room, and she stood there for about a minute and a half, and then she wrote one line in the day-book, and it is the last line in that book in the year and it is the only line in four years that has nothing against it and no reason on it.

*Twenty-ninth of Fallowmonth, seventh hour. The eighth line is still empty. I have looked at it four times in three weeks and I have wanted to fill it about four times, and I have not, and I am going to write down that I looked and wanted to, because a keeper who has never once written down that she wanted to do a thing is a keeper whose wants get into documents without her noticing, and this is the whole of what I have learned in four years and a month and it took me a page about a bridge.*

And she put the lamp down and did not fill it in, and left the lamp and the rule and the empty line and the reason a county boy of nineteen copied, and went and locked the press.

The last image is on a bridge and it is not his.

The Sedge Reach is four timber bays and a road surface and it is holding, and it is the structure this town knows him by, and it is not the structure the town has been talking about since the nineteenth of Mudmonth, and on the twenty-ninth of Fallowmonth, in the last hour of the low water, there was a man on the Sedge Reach deck with a tool bag at the fourth course, counting load points out of habit.

He counted nine under the first bay and eleven under the second and he wrote the hour and the state of the deck in a notebook, because that is the whole of what he is, and then he stood in the middle of four timber bays in a fen county with a bar across his knees and did not go to the Old Toll, which is four hundred yards up the road and has a bell on it, and which he has not looked at since the twenty-fourth and which has a new iron band on it and a dull red seam in a split nine stone long that was closed from both sides at the same hour by two people four hundred miles apart who were not asked and were not thanked.

And at about the fifth hour, with the tide making and the light going, a note went out of that county.

It was not a good note. It was a note on a cracked bell that has been off its note since the third of a long-ago year, and it went about four hundred yards and a great deal further, and it was answered inside a second and a half, and the answer came back through the rain, and the whole of the sound of the two of them together was in the water and on the boards of four timber bays and in the ears of about nine people in a lane off the Sedge Reach approach who had come to look at something and stayed for the sound.

A boy of fifteen stood on the near abutment of the Old Toll with his hand on the rope, because he is the steward's apprentice on the Merefen end and he has been learning the road for a year and he is not good at it yet. On the far end of a line, four hundred miles off, a woman in a good coat with iron dust in the creases of her hands had her other hand flat on a bell that is not hers and has not been hers for eleven years, and it was her hands that closed the split and it was her hands that were asked to ring it, and she rang it because the arrangement is the arrangement and because a bell is a thing that is rung on a day by the people who keep it and not by the man who found it.

His left palm was warm.

It had been warm three times in five months and it was warm now, in the last hour of the low water of the year, in a bridge season, in a county of a thousand people, in the flat cold light of a Fallowmonth evening, and he put it flat on the rail of the Sedge Reach deck and it was warm the way a hand is warm, and he did not need it.

He stood there for about eleven minutes and then he picked the bar up off his knees and put it in the bag, and shut the bag, and went down off the deck, and did not look back at the note, because there was nothing to look back at.

And nothing in that sound was his.
