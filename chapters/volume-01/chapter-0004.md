# Chapter 0004

The six days between the twelfth and the eighteenth were the longest of his adult life, and nothing whatsoever happened in them.

That is not a figure of speech. He went to work. The water came up four fingers over that week and went down two, and the Reed Line along the old raised road stood up in a straight cut where the flood had been, and the town read the date off it the way the town read everything, by looking at a thing and agreeing about it. He cut out and re-packed nine feet of the sill at the Old Toll crossing, which is not work anybody would pay for, and which he did anyway, because it was his, and because a man who is waiting for a date does not stop working or the date arrives while he is carrying something.

On the fourteenth he was under the third pier at Sedge Reach with a night's flood over his head up to the ribs, feeling for a crack in the nosing that he was fairly sure was not there, and on the fifteenth the millers paid half the boat pass without being asked a second time, and on the sixteenth a boy from the foundry sold him a stick of chalk for a penny and took the penny, and on the seventeenth a carter's man, a big one, a stranger, drowned in the west channel off the reed beds at a place where the current makes a hole nobody can name.

The town's custom was the custom of the water, not of the Crown or the parish. The ferrymen ring for the drowned. They have always rung for the drowned, on this reach, with whatever is to hand, and the handiest thing to hand for thirty years has been the cracked Bellweather toll bell on the abutment of a bridge nobody carts to, because there is no other bell on this reach that anybody will leave alone. Nobody in Merefen had ever decided out loud whether the drowned were still owed that. It went on anyway, which is how most of the town's arrangements go.

So the bell was to be rung on the night of the eighteenth, and the storm was forecast for the night of the eighteenth, and Aren lay in the room over the cooper with his boots on and listened to the rain come across the west channel flat and hard and thought about the two things he had done that week.

One: he had signed a promise with no date on it, in a good hand, to a woman who had asked for it deliberately, having decided in advance that a man with a night already chosen would not part with the date but would part with everything else. She had got the promise anyway. She had got it because he had signed it, and because he had not qualified it, and because he had agreed to the whole of it at once, and now he had a week in which to think about what the whole of it was.

Two: he had a bar, a bar through a headstock loop, and a chime cut into the loop, and he had a hand with a pale seam across the palm that no one in Merefen had ever looked at and that he had never once asked a glasswright about, and he had a bell that had made a sound with nobody's hand on it, and the sound had not been in the bell.

The water under his door on the eighteenth was a finger deep, and it came in under the board and went to the corner, which is what it does in a fen town in Mudmonth, and the cooper's wife put a chock of oak under the door and did not say anything about it.

The sky came up green-black at the fifth hour and by the seventh the rain was the kind that does not fall, it travels, and by the ninth the river had gone from loud to enormous and the ferrymen were out on it in three boats with lamps, going down the reach to where the carter's man had gone under.

Aren came out onto the Old Toll crossing at the ninth hour with the bar over his shoulder and a length of rope, and the wind took his coat out of shape and the water coming up under the arches was black and full of the broken light of the lamps.

Wenna Koll's boat came under the third arch on the ebb. She had a rope across her stern and one of her crew on the pole, and they were not ringing anything. They were waiting.

"You're late for the ringing," she shouted up over the water.

"You've not started."

"We wait till the water's over the marks. That's the custom and the custom's older than anybody who'll argue with you about it." She got her boat under the abutment and hauled herself up on the ladder with the ease of a woman who has done it ten thousand times. "You can have the bell after the fourth mark, or you can have it now and have the whole town on the bridge, but you cannot have it now, Kest, because there are nineteen people out there in the dark on that deck and it is not a place to make a noise."

"I know," Aren said. "The fourth mark."

He stood under the arch with his back to the wind and waited for the marks, and the marks came up out of the dark one at a time, the first one at ten, the second just after, the third in the middle of the worst of it, and on the fourth Wenna's crew put their weight on the pole and the hull came up broadside to the arch and Aren heard somebody on the far bank start a man's name across the water.

Then it was quiet except for the rain, and he went up the ladder to the abutment and got the bar out of the loop.

The cradle had grown in. He knew it had; he had known it since the eleventh, and he had let four days and a foundry bill pass without doing anything about it, because doing something about it would have meant taking the bell down, and taking the bell down would have meant putting it in Tovan's yard, and it is remarkable how many decisions a man can get out of one by leaving a piece of iron rusted in place.

The yoke was rusted to the headstock where the last century's fitter had set it, and the tongue had settled a finger's depth into the lip of the split. He put the bar through the headstock loop — not through the bell, never through the bell, nine stone of split bronze is lifted by its headstock or it is not lifted at all — and he put his boots on the wet setts of the sill and set his feet, and he got the bar under the loop, and he began.

Every handhold in that operation was a decision about where nine stone goes if he drops it. That is the thing about the ritual: not the strength, because nine stone is not heavy for a man who works timber, but the arithmetic of the four places it can land, of which two are his own feet, one is the third step, and one is a boat. The crack goes face up. He had told the men on the arch that the crack must not go face up, and the reason, which he had not told them, is that a bell with a split through the mouth and its broken edge up will do two things at once when it is struck, and one of them is throw a man's hands off it.

It came free in one movement, and the lip knocked the yoke once as it cleared — the dull knock, the one Wenna had heard, the one that is not a ring — and the whole nine stone came up on the loop and swung out over the sill and hung there, and the rain went through the place where the bell had been.

He got the rope on it. He put a hitch through the clapper-eye and hauled the clapper back against the shoulder of the bell, as far as it would go, which was not far, because that is what a cracked bell is. He could not strike it properly. A bell split lip to shoulder cannot speak; it can be leaned on, and it will give you a flat, dead, ruined noise, and the noise will be heard, and that is the whole of what a bell is for.

He stood with the clapper pulled back and the bell swinging a little on the headstock, and he did not strike it, and he said the name.

Not to the bell. He said it out loud on a bridge in a storm to a piece of municipal salvage, the way a man says a name in an empty room, because there was nothing else in Merefen to say it to, and because a thing you are going to do is easier if you have said its name first.

"Bellweather," he said.

The rain went on. Nothing in the world moved, and the fire in the hearth of his mind gave a short careful attention, the way a man who has been half asleep looks up when a door he is certain is bolted opens anyway.

What he had was three things and he knew them in his body rather than in his head, the way you know a load path.

He had a place. A name and a river and a bridge and a hundred and forty feet of arch and a city at the far end of it, and it was a place that had a night in it — he knew that the way he knew a sill under his hand, without being able to say how.

He had something to hold on to. Not the bell. The sill. Three feet below his boots, cut stone, repacked in the autumn and again in the last week, with his date and his mark chalked in the lime bed where the flood would keep it. He put his hand on the top of the stone as if it were a rail, and it was warm, and it was the only part of that bridge that was not streaming with rain, and he thought, with a clarity he will be embarrassed by for the rest of his life: *this is the way back. This is where I packed the stone. This is mine and I can find it.*

He had a witness.

He did not have a witness. That was the part he did not do, and the part that would cost him nine days and three days of fever and the pitch of a verse, and he did not do it because he had nobody to ask: a return stitch is a place you prepare with somebody who will say they saw you prepare it, and there was nobody in Merefen he could ask for that, and he had a promise in his teeth with no date on it, and he had a storm, and he had a bar through a headstock loop, and he had decided — on the eighteenth, at the ninth hour, with the whole thing settled in his own mind the way a load path settles — that the way out would be his own business.

There is a saying among road crews about the old roads that never appear on any map: three days to open one, three days to come back through it, and a man who has not three days to give should not begin, because the road does not care what he has in the meantime. Aren had heard it his whole life and had never given it more credit than a saying. He had a week's flood work in front of him, a Tuesday barge, a sitting on the twenty-fourth, a promise with no date on it, and a storm on that very night, and the saying had never once in his life been a rule about what a man should do next.

He got his hand to the clapper.

The crack in the bell had a seam in it, dull red, the colour of a banked fire seen through a shutter, and the red was on the inside of the split, and it had been on the inside of the split for eleven years in a river in the ground, and it was not rust and it did not come off on a cloth.

He pulled. The clapper went over and hit the sound bow, and the bell did what a broken bell does, which is to answer with the ghost of a note: a flat, stopped, ugly sound with the pitch crushed out of it, that did not carry across the water and did not come back.

It came back into his hand.

That was the thing he would remember. The sound arrived through the left palm before it arrived through the ear, and the palm had a pale seam in it that he had carried since a child and had never had looked at, and the seam was suddenly a join, a place where two edges had been brought together and not quite fitted, and the bell's own broken note went down into it and down, and his right forearm — the one with the burn in it from the working that went wrong nine winters ago — took the counterweight of it, hot, and he smelled hot metal and river mud and something else under both of them, tar, and a forge, and the cold coming off wet stone that has been standing in the dark for a long time.

And a voice, not in his ears, in the place where the Gatehouse is a room he has never been in and cannot describe, said one thing, plainly, and it was true, and it was not the whole truth:

*A road needs a living hand to open it.*

A fold came into the rain.

Not light. Weather. The rain in front of him bent — the way water bends over a sill — and made a seam of moving air from the ground to the sky, and the seam was the size and the shape of a road, and the air on the other side of the seam was colder and smelled of a foundry, and he heard a hammer, and a man shouting a number, and somebody laughing in an accent he had never heard, which was his own.

Aren Kest stood on a bridge in a storm at the ninth hour of the night of the eighteenth, with a bar through a headstock loop and nine stone of split green bronze swinging on the headstock and the clapper against its shoulder, and the red in the crack gone out, and his left hand stinging.

He thought, for about four seconds, in the way a man thinks in the middle of the best thing that has ever happened to him: *I am going to fix it.*

Then he took his boots off the wet setts, one and then the other, and put his hand flat on the sill — on the repacked stone, on his own mark, on the warm stone — and stepped through.

The cold of the other side went into him to the knees, and the smell came with it: hot glass and river mud and pitch, and under that woodsmoke and wet wool and the sour edge of a working town. There was no rain on his face at all. The first thing he heard was a hammer on iron, three times running, and then a man shouting a number at somebody, and somebody abusing a wheel, and a whole sound uncracked bell going across a hundred roofs for no reason anybody would later be able to remember.

It was a city, and it was cold, and it was the sixth hour of the morning, and it was working.

He had a bar's length of time on the far side, and he used part of it on something that had been sitting in him since he was seven years old, without meaning to, the way a man scratches an itch on the way past a wall.

He hummed the river song.

The first line went where it went. The second line went where it went. The third line — the one that always came down onto the low note, the boatman's note, the one a woman had sung by a window with the water going past outside and had never once had to tell him, because you do not tell a child a tune, you only fail to hide it — the third line arrived, and it did not land. It got most of the way down the phrase and then there was nothing under it, a place where the note used to be and was not, the way a plank arrives short of a scarf. He heard the gap in his own head while he was standing in a country he had never seen with his hand on a bridge that was not his, and he did not have time to be frightened by it, and he thought: *somebody has taken that.*

He looked down at his left hand.

The pale seam he had carried since he was a child and had never had examined was black, and it was not a cut, and it ran under the skin and past the edge of the skin the way a joint runs past the end of a timber, and where it crossed the heel of his palm the black had a grain to it, in one direction only, like a road that has been folded rather than drawn.

He put his hand in his coat. The other side of the seam was still falling on him — the water going over four bays in the dark, a carter's man going under off the reed beds, the ringing of a bad bell in a bad storm, and somewhere behind all of that a woman singing four lines near water, in a room with a window open, eleven years and a very long way off.

It was a city, and it was cold, and it was the sixth hour of the morning, and it was working, and the first thing he had to do in it was stand up and find out what a bell that rings by itself is worth.

The rain came apart around the place where he had been standing and went on falling in Merefen without him. The bell, released, swung twice on its headstock, once slowly and once not, and knocked the yoke once with nobody's hand on it.
