# Chapter 0044

The two men came up the Old Toll road at the seventh hour of the twenty-sixth of Longlight with a barrow, and four men of the yard came up behind them at the second hour of the afternoon with the tools, and the whole of what happened on that bridge that day was ordinary.

That is the sentence Tovan Kest would have given anybody who asked him, and nobody asked him, and he has thought about it a great deal in the six days since.

Ordinary. At the lowest water of the year, a bench that owns no crossing sends out its own materials and its own men's time, because a bench voted on the fifteenth of Longlight to keep four hundred yards of the oldest bridge in the county at four marks and six a year, and four marks and six a year is two visits a year, and this was the first of them.

The men under the deck got the footings out and the men on it got the deck to where it wanted to be, and two men from the bench cleared the moss out of the bed of the stone at the abutment with a cold chisel, because there is one week in the year when a man can get a chisel to that bed and this was it, and because the bench had voted to keep the crossing and keeping a crossing in a dry month is exactly that and nothing else.

And on the parapet, at the fourth course, four men of a yard dressed and set a stone and laid forty yards of tarred hemp through the abutment on a splice cut out of a cooper's bin off Weir Street, and it was the best splice he had ever cut, and it was the ordinary autumn work and not a new thing, and it went into the fourth course *beside* the seat that his brother had cut there himself in Greenmonth with a chisel and a drill in the rain.

He worked the parapet himself for the first hour and then went and did his own work, because a brother who works a brother's job is doing a favour, and he had invented a rule in a gateway in front of four men in Greenmonth that nothing that belongs to a man goes in this yard's book and only a thing set, and a favour is a thing set by a man and not by a bench, and the difference between those two things is the whole of what he has been trying to teach the other one for eleven years.

A yard is a crew and a crew is not an owner. Three men and a fortnight and a wage out of a box, twice a year, at the two low waters, and a crew never once decided anything about a bridge. He had said that out loud in a gateway in front of four men in Greenmonth and had been surprised at how much it had mattered to him, and it was the best sentence he had ever said, and nobody had thanked him for it, and he had not expected to and he went on saying it to himself twice a week, which he was aware was a habit and which he allowed, because a man who has one good sentence in his life is entitled to it.

He had been under the deck of that crossing three times. The third was the summer before the storm, when the river was in spate and a boy of sixteen from the mill had put his hand into a hole in the foot of a pier, and a captain in a Crown's cloak had talked him out of it, and had been wrong about the hole and right about the boy.

He did not think about that. He had got very good in eleven years at not thinking about things while he was working, and it was the only skill he had that anybody had ever paid for, and he was not going to give it up for a feeling.

Four men, two days, a dressed stone and a line of tarred hemp with a leather splice, and the splice was a thing he had cut himself out of a cooper's bin off Weir Street in the summer, and it was the best splice he had ever cut and it was the only thing in that yard that had ever been a bad thing to get wrong.

The Old Toll crossing's maintenance is three marks and about six the campaign, twice a year, seven marks and no farthing. What goes in the book is four marks and six a year, because four is what goes in a book, and he had caught himself rounding in Greenmonth and had stopped, and the stopping had cost him a fortnight of being taken seriously by two men in the yard and had been worth it.

So the maintenance line was four marks and six, and the factor's stone contract had gone through in the last week of Longlight and been paid, and the yard had a house in the county town that was setting a yard wall and was small and was slow, and the two lines together meant the yard had about five weeks in it, and five weeks is not a year.

A yard with five weeks in it and four men is four men and five weeks. It is not four men and a year. It is not a bench. It is not an owner. It is a crew.

He had written that in a book in a gateway in Greenmonth and it was the best sentence he had ever said and nobody had thanked him for it and he had not expected to and he went on saying it in his head twice a week, which he was aware was a habit, and which he allowed, because a man who has one good sentence in his life is entitled to say it to himself.

The fourth place was what nobody had told him about.

The yard had been four places since the sixth of Mudmonth. There was the factor's stone, which was finished, and the county town's wall, which was going, and the Sedge Reach, which kept itself at the flood stage and which he took a fortnight of every month of for nothing, on a bench order, out of the bench's materials, because the bench had voted it in the summer and no bench has ever been able to look at a flooded deck in February and remember what it voted in the summer.

And there was the Old Toll, which was nobody's work and was four hundred yards of the oldest bridge in the county.

And the fifth thing in the yard was a Crown's instrument, on a shelf above the bench at the back, in a wrapper with no seal, that a woman had walked down Weir Street on the first of Fallowmonth and put in his hand herself, and which he had read twice and understood entirely and which was going to be in that building until she told him it was answered.

That is a fact and not a paper. He had known that for four months and had been wrong about it, and the wrongness had been the most useful thing that had ever happened to him, and he had found it out at the ninth hour on the twenty-second of Longlight when a man came up a street and asked for a line and not a paper.

There was a Crown's seal in that building too, and it was not on the shelf, and it had been there since the spring, and it was nailed over a doorway at the back of the Old Toll yard in a place nobody could see it from the road, and it was a mould of the grey wax and it had come off a storehouse door by the fish weir in a piece of cold tallow on the nineteenth of Mudmonth, and the watch had let a sixteen-year-old boy take it in a candle and the boy had brought it up Weir Street on his way home from a job because a thing that is a Crown's seal is a thing a boy thinks he should be able to look at, and the boy had asked Tovan Kest what it was, and Tovan Kest had said *that's the mark on the wax* and the boy had gone home, and the mould had been in the yard since Mudmonth.

He had never used it. He had thought about using it every week, which is the honest way of saying he had thought about not using it every week, and the reason he had not was that a yard is not an office and a seal is an office, and a man who hangs a Crown's mark on a wall has made a claim, and the claim is *this thing is mine or this thing is under me or this thing is answerable to me*, and every one of those three is false, and the yard has one thing in it that is true, which is *the fact of a thing set*.

He did not tell anybody about the mould. That was the one thing in five months he had not written down anywhere, and he had thought about writing it in the book and had decided that a Crown's seal is not his and that the moment it is in his book it is in the file of a man who has a provincial ledger in him.

The bench sat on the second of Fallowmonth, at the ninth hour, in the long room over the wharf, and it sat because Reeve Aldis Fenn had called it at the fourth hour of the previous day in a way she had not called anything in nine years, which was that she had not called it at all; she had simply put a notice on the board at the toll-house with the day and the hour on it and a rule over it, and a notice with a rule over it is a thing a clerk of a bench cannot ignore.

Captain Rell stood outside the door.

That is the whole of what happened and it took about eleven minutes and it is the most consequential eleven minutes of Toban's year and nobody has ever put it in a book in a way that reads like a consequence.

The instruction of the ninth of Longlight, second hour, carried out by the watch of Merefen on the tenth at the fifth hour, is a sentence in a letter and not an order, and it names no law, and it requires no court, and it is not a threat. It directs the watch of this county to prevent contact between the object and any person, to prevent any person approaching, examining or speaking with any person concerning it; and it directs the watch **to prevent the assembly of any body of this county in respect of the object, and shall report.**

A bench of nine men in a guild house in a fen town is not an officer of anything. The county had said so in writing on the eleventh of Longlight, on the back of a returned paper, in a clerk's hand, in the only Crown's paper in this county that has ever helped anybody.

Captain Rell stood outside the door for eleven minutes on the second of Fallowmonth with two men, and did not come in, and did not stop anybody going in, and did not write anything down at the time, and wrote the whole thing down afterwards at his own table at the sixth hour of the same day in his own hand with the hour on it, and sent a copy of it to the guild's clerk by hand, unasked, and did not send one to anybody else.

The minute of it is four lines and it is in the guild's roll and the fourth line is the one that has cost a town the most:

*That the bench sat, and that the watch of this town stood at the door, and that the bench has asked the watch of this county whether the bench was assembled in respect of the object, and that the watch has answered that the bench is a town's guild and is not an officer of anything and that this county so certified on the eleventh of Longlight, and that the watch has not entered a view and has entered the time, and that the bench has entered the time also, and that the two times are the same.*

It is the first time in this county's history that a watch captain and a bench have entered the same hour in two books, and the effect of it is that there is now a document in Merefen that says a bench of nine men sat in a room on a Wednesday in Fallowmonth while a watch captain stood outside, and both accounts agree, and neither account says what they were about.

**That is the hole in the magistrate's finding, and it is the first time it has cost a town anything.**

Lysa Fenn's finding of the fourth of Greenmonth said: *any office thereafter acting in the matter is on notice that this court asked and was not answered, and that the asking is dated.* It is a beautiful piece of work and it is the most useful document in the county and it has been carried by three other documents for four months without being asked to carry one itself.

What it does not do is say what a thing in this county may lawfully do in the meantime.

And so a bench of nine men had spent six weeks unable to meet about a bridge they were paying four marks and six a year to keep, and a road warden in the county had spent six weeks unable to be in a room about a stone, and neither of them could say out loud in their own building why, because the reason was a sentence in a letter that named no law, and a man who says *a letter has told me not to meet about the bridge* in a guild hall is a man who has said out loud in a guild hall that there is a bell.

They were governed by an instruction without a court. Every one of them. In a town of a thousand people. For six weeks.

Tovan Kest had worked it out on the twenty-second of Longlight, in a gateway, and had not said it, and he said it now, standing up, at the ninth hour, on the second of Fallowmonth, in a public room, which was the third time in five months and the third time he did not vote.

The vote was on a bridge.

The reeve had called it for one thing and there was one thing, and it was money, and the money was the second visit.

On the twenty-sixth of Longlight the bench's man with the levels had put up a specification of two leaves in his own hand for the first of the two visits a year, and the yard had worked to it in daylight four hundred yards up the Old Toll road with a barrow and a cold chisel and two men from the bench clearing the moss out of the bed of the stone, and nobody had voted on anything that day because it was the work they had voted in Longlight.

The specification was two leaves and the eighth line of it was the one that mattered now. The first line said that no block was to be cut in a place where there is glass in it, which is the only sentence in it that Aren Kest would have put there himself. The fourth said a block was to be set on a bed of lime and oak dust and not on lime alone. The seventh said the line was to be tarred hemp and not wire. And the eighth line said, in the guild's man's hand, thirty-one years in this guild and the best trade reasoner in the county: *that a seat so cut will be used and will fail, and that the number of uses is not stated here, because it is not a thing this bench may know.*

The line that was dressed and set in the fourth course on the twenty-sixth of Longlight at the second hour of the afternoon had been used once, at the fourth hour of that same afternoon, by a man with a bar who went out of this county on it and who was not in this county at the ninth hour on the second of Fallowmonth, and the guild's man with the levels put the specification on the table and opened it at the eighth line and said nothing at all about it, and the room read the line, and the room understood it in about four seconds, and nobody in the room said the word *seat* out loud, because the man whose seat it was had gone and had not come back.

The cost of the second visit was **three marks and about six.** Oak, lime, stone and cartage, four men for a week and a boy for two days, and a pair of iron shoes for the fourth pier that the specification does not mention and that every man who has ever put a boat under that arch has asked for.

The reeve said the cost before anybody else, because she was the reeve and she was the only person in that room who costed things for a living.

"Four marks and six a year is what this bench spends on that bridge and it is the truth and it is not the figure in the book, and the figure in the book is four because four goes in a book. That has been the position since Greenmonth and it is not going to change before the frost. The chest has two marks in it and has not had more than two marks in it since Mudmonth, because there is no money in this town. **This is three marks and about six. There is a fortnight and a half left in the yard and it is spoken for, and the fourth pier's iron shoes are in a dead man's cart behind the mill and I have seen them.**" She stopped. "I have costed the loss of the wharfage and the market at four or five marks a year and a market day, and I have said so in this room twice, and I am not going to say it a third time. I record only that a bench of nine is being asked to do a thing twice."

"Twice," said Tovan Kest, from the stool at the end by the door. "That is the whole of it and it is worth saying in this room before the vote. **The bench voted on the fifteenth of Longlight to keep a crossing it does not own, at its own cost, out of its own materials and its own men's time. That was a vote about a thing nobody else would pay for. This is the same vote again, eighteen days later, and it is not the same vote, because the first one was a bench being generous and this one is a bench being asked.** A bench that is generous is a bench that decides. A bench that is asked is a bench that is being told what it is for."

The reeve wrote that down. She wrote it in her own hand and read it back twice and it is the second sentence in the roll that is Tovan Kest's and it is the only one in five months that anybody has copied into a second book, which the reeve did herself, and she did it in the same ink and on the same page, and Tovan Kest noticed and said nothing about it then and thought about it every day afterwards.

"I will not vote," he said.

"You did not vote on the fifteenth," said the man in the chair. "And you were not a member on the fifteenth."

"I am not a member now either," said Tovan Kest, "and I have not been since I was twenty-one, and I have been in this room and out of it for nineteen years and the only reason anybody lets me stand up in it is that I have never once voted on a thing I was a party to, and I am a party to this one, because four men of this yard were on that deck on the twenty-sixth of Longlight at the second hour and four men of this yard would be on it in the spring at the seventh hour if there were money for it, and the wages come out of my chest, and there are about a fortnight and a half left in this yard and it is spoken for."

"Then say the true thing and do not vote," said the clerk.

"I have said it," said Tovan Kest, "and I have not said the half of it yet. Here is the half.

"**The first time this bench voted to keep that bridge, it voted out of its own materials and its own men's time and not one penny of anybody's money, and it was a bench that had just read a Crown's paper and minuted that it took no notice of it, and it was proud, and it was right to be proud, and it got four marks and six a year out of the vote and a road worth it.** This time the same bench is asked for the same bridge by the same men and the only thing that has changed is that it is a second request, and a bench that cannot tell the difference between the first time and the second time has stopped being a bench and started being a thing that is administered to.

"And I will tell you the worst of it, and then I will sit down. I have stood in this room three times in five months and said a true thing and not voted, and I have got very good at it, and the price of being good at it is that a man with a bar on his shoulder comes down Weir Street every week and asks me for a line in my book, and I give him one, and I have never once said *no*. **I have said no to a road three times in five months in this room and I have never once said no to my brother, and he knows the difference, and it is the worst thing about me and it is not in any book.**"

The room was quiet for the length of a slow count of four.

The vote was carried. Eight hands and the reeve's, and one abstention that was not an abstention because Tovan Kest was not a member and had said so out loud in a public room for the third time in five months.

The Crown's hand came up Weir Street on the ninth of Fallowmonth at the seventh hour, and it was not a courier and it was not a letter and it was a man in a provincial coat with a leather bag and a schedule of a county's season's inspections, and the schedule had forty-one lines on it, and on every line a ruled column.

He read the heading of the column out loud in the gateway, in front of four men, and he read it the way a man reads a word he has never seen before and does not know the meaning of, which is how a Crown's man reads a word that means something.

"*Keeper*," he said. "That is the heading. It is the county's form. I cannot change it. I have had four forms in my bag since Frostmonth and the column is on all of them and it is not mine."

"I have seen it," said Tovan Kest. "It is on the sheet of the second of Longlight in a file in the county town with a remark in a column of my own man's hand, and he took the job and refused the sheet, and he wrote down the reason in his own hand, and one of the reasons is the money." He put the chisel down. "I am going to tell you four things and then I am going to do what I said I would do in this gateway in Greenmonth, and the four things are the price and the price is mine and not his.

"One. **Nothing that belongs to a man goes in this yard's book and only a thing set.** A piece of paper does not go in this book. Ever. I have said that in this gateway in front of four men and I am saying it in front of five.

"Two. **The yard holds the fact and not the thing.** A date, an hour, a name, a number of sheets, and a person. If a Crown's man comes up this street in a year and asks me for a paper, I have not got it, and I can stand in a room and say so and be right, and being right about a thing I have not got is the only kind of being right available to anybody in this trade.

"Three. **If a paper comes up this street it stays where the person who brought it put it, until the person who brought it comes for it.** It does not come in here. It does not go in the book. If it comes in here I put it back in the hands it was given to and I do it in front of the four men and I do it in front of the man who brought it if he is here.

"Four." He held up the schedule. "This is not a paper about anything. This is a sheet with forty-one lines on it and a man is supposed to write a name in the fifth column of each of them. I am not writing forty-one names. I am not writing one name. I am going to give the man the number of the lines and the hour and the fact that this yard has never inspected a crossing in this county because this yard builds them and does not inspect them, and he can put that in his book and it is true, and it is the same answer to a Crown's form as a fact instead of a name, and I did not get it from the woman, I worked it out in this gateway in Greenmonth, and I have been sitting on it for four months waiting for somebody to walk up this street with a form in it."

He said it in about nine seconds and went back to the stone.

The man in the provincial coat took the number of the lines and the hour and wrote it down and asked for the name of the yard and Tovan Kest gave it to him, and it went down Weir Street and into a file two hundred miles off, and there is in that file, to this day, a line that says *Merefen yard, T. Kest, carpenter, at the seventh hour of the ninth of Fallowmonth, states that this yard inspects no crossing in this county and has never inspected one, and gives the number of the lines and not a name*, and in eleven years that is going to be read by somebody in a room and it is going to be the only line in the whole of that office's file that nobody had to be got to change.

The report came in on the twelfth of Fallowmonth off a carrier who had it off a barge mate who had it off a wharf-clerk in a town four days off that no man in Merefen could name and would not be able to for a year.

Four days. Not a bridge that was going. **A bridge that was shut.**

It came onto the Market Square board on the twelfth at the eighth hour, and it had no rule over it, and by the fourteenth there were four lines on that board without a rule and the clerk had stopped wiping the lower half, which he had done every morning since the sixth of Mudmonth, and which he went on doing for another fortnight and then did not.

The four that had rules were the day, the tide, the culvert at Four Elms, and the load of stone expected at the mill.

*Crossing shut in the north city two days on a Wednesday night. Barges put in wrong bank all Thursday. Guild has sworn in a hall to hold it. Stranger from the south is why.*

*Crossing shut in the north city two days on a Wednesday night. Nobody will say which night. Carrier says Frostmonth, his mate says Longlight.*

*They have sworn to hold it in a hall. Whole crossing. All of it.*

*Ask Kest. He knows what the oath is. He has not been here since the twenty-sixth.*

Four lines without a rule on a board that a clerk wipes every morning, and the fourth of them was the one that was going to be on that board for three years, and the reason it was going to be on it for three years is that a man who has been gone since the twenty-sixth of Longlight could not contradict it, and the town had learned about that in Mudmonth, and had decided, as a town, that this was how it was done.

The last line was the town being wrong in the ordinary way and it was wrong in the correct direction. The oath was not his. The oath had been asked for by a creditor and put to nine men in a hall by a bench and it was the fourth generation of the same sentence and he had stood in a hall and said *do not put a number on it tonight, put a date on it and put a man on it*, and about forty people had heard him say it, and none of them were in Merefen.

The sill of the Old Toll crossing opened at about the second hour of the morning of the second of Fallowmonth.

He was on it. He was on it because he had a bar in his hand and a lamp in the other one and he was doing the thing he does twice a year, which is to go down onto the stone at the lowest water of the year with a lamp and look at the fourth course, and the fourth course had a seat in it that had been used twice and had a maker's mark under the near disc, and he had put that seat in himself in Greenmonth with a chisel and a drill at the third hour of the morning in the rain and he had done it alone and had told nobody until the eighth.

He was alone on the bridge and the town was asleep and there was a moon about two-thirds and the wind had gone round to the east and there was nothing in the air at all.

And at the second hour the stone under his right boot said something.

It was not a sound. He had heard the story about it four times in eleven years and he had never once believed it and he believed it now, and he wrote in the yard's book on the second of Fallowmonth, in his own hand, with the date and the hour and a rule over it, the plainest description of it that a carpenter can make, and the description is: *the stone moved under the foot and the whole of the deck came up about a quarter of an inch and set down again on the other side of itself, and the moving and the setting down were not one movement but two, and there was about a second and a half between them, and nothing fell.*

Four hundred yards up the Old Toll road the bell-shaped hole in the parapet had moss in it. A Crown's seal on a post four feet from it had not run and would not run in a hundred days. A watch captain had a room in a watch house with a lock on it and a bell in it and a commendation nailed to the outside of his own wall with four nails in it, and the commendation had been on that wall for about a month, and Captain Rell had told a road warden on a step in the rain that he was being *picked*.

A bench of nine men had a minute in a roll that said the same hour was entered in two books by a bench and a watch captain. A town had a board with six lines on it and no rules over any of them and one of them said *Ask Kest*. A yard had a Crown's instrument on a shelf above a bench that was going to be answered on the ninth of Rainmonth, and a bench's resolution on the deck of a bridge that had cost it one mark and two shillings, and a crown's mould of grey wax over a doorway at the back that nobody could see from the road.

And in four hundred miles, in a hall with a stone in the floor of it, over an arch, there were about two thousand people on a bridge, and a bench of nine men had stood up in a full hall on the night of the market day and had sworn, out loud, in front of two hundred people, with a clerk writing it down, that the crossing would be held.

Tovan Kest stood on the deck of the oldest bridge in his county with a lamp in his hand at the second hour of the morning of the second of Fallowmonth, and he was not a keeper of anything, and he had said in a gateway in front of four men that nothing that belongs to a man goes in his book and only a thing set, and a week ago the yard's book had a road in it for the first time in nineteen years and no explanation, and on that page there was a line that he had entered and ruled over on the second of Longlight and had not read again since, because a man does not read a thing like that twice, and it was this:

*The river is down and the lowest water is on or about the twenty-sixth. The seat is cut and it has two used discs in it and there is no glass to be had for a third before Frostmonth. I am not going to say anything else about it in this book.*

He read it, because it was two in the morning and the stone had just moved.

Then he wrote the hour and the description and one more line under it, in the yard's book, in his own hand, with a rule over the whole of it, and the last line was not about the stone.

*Wrote this at the third hour with a lamp. My brother went out of this county on the twenty-sixth of Longlight and has not come back and there is no road back in this county that I can find, and I have looked for one every day for six days and there is not one, and I am not going to ask anybody about it, and I am not going to stand in a gateway on a Monday and ask a man with a bar whether he knows when he is coming home, because the answer is that a man who has been gone six days is not coming home on a day, and a day is what he is gone on, and the whole of what I know is a number of days and a road-crews' saying that does not have him in it.*

*I do not know what the stone is. I know the water and I know the season and I know that the fourth course of that bridge has been dressed by four generations of men in my family and I have never in my life known a stone to do that.*

*And I am the man that town asks about my brother, and I have been for four months, and I did not ask to be, and there is no way in this county to stop being it, and I am going to go up Weir Street in about an hour and put this page in the guild's roll and not in a book I have got, and I am going to say it out loud in a guild hall, and I am not going to have a version of it, and if this bench wants to hold this crossing for nine months on four marks and six a year then it can have a book with a date in it in the morning, from me, with my hand under the clerk's and not over it, and it can be the third time in five months and I will not vote on it, and that will be all right, because that is the whole of what I have ever been able to do in this room and it has taken me eleven years and two brothers to find out that it is worth something.*
