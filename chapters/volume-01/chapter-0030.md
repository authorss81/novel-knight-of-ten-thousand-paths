# Chapter 0030

He came up out of the sill of the Old Toll crossing at the first light of the sixteenth of Greenmonth and got as far as the third setting and sat down on it with his back against the parapet, and stayed there for an hour and a half. He could not get up, and did not try again for about forty minutes, and in the hour and a half he did three things: he wrote the hour and the state of the seat, and he looked at the empty cradle for a while, and he listened to a town get on with a Friday, which is a thing a town does whether or not there is a man in the world who has been in a city of four thousand, and which is the first sound of his own country he had heard in nineteen days that had not come out of a conversation.

There was no fold in the air. There was no voice. There was a cold river and a wet road and the smell of the fen at six in the morning and a man sitting on a bridge in his own clothes, and the sky over the west channel going the colour of a bad coin, and nothing whatever happening.

The cradle was four feet away with nothing in it. The post was grey. The seal was where it had been, and the wax had not run, and there was moss at the corner of a swept rectangle, and the seat in the fourth course had two discs in it and a pin and an oak collar with three hammer blows in the head of it, and the maker's mark was under the near disc where a maker's mark goes.

He got his notebook out with his left hand, which took a while, and wrote the hour and the state of the seat, and then he wrote the second line, which was not about the seat.

*Seventh hour of the night of the fifteenth into the sixteenth. Cold, no wind, felt it in my back before I came up. Four seconds and a half. Cannot stand yet.*

He was still sitting there when the cart came up the road at the second hour, and he heard the wheels on the setts before he saw it, and he put the notebook away and tried to get up and could not, and did not get up, and the man on the cart pulled the mule up and got down and came the last eight yards on foot at a speed that Tovan Kest would have denied being able to manage.

"Aren."

"I cannot stand."

"You are not going to," said Tovan Kest. "You have got a fever, which I have been expecting since the eighth of Greenmonth, on a bridge, at the tenth hour at night, when you told me three, and you told me three, and I have written the three on a piece of paper in my own hand and I would like you to see it later." He got an arm under him. "Left. Not the right. The right will go if you lean on it and you have got a cut on the right from Mudmonth that has never closed and I have said so for eleven years."

They got him as far as the cart. It took a quarter of an hour and neither of them said anything for any of it, which is the correct amount, and Tovan Kest did not ask him a single question about where he had been, or about the nineteenth, or about the arch, or about the name in a counting house, and Aren Kest understood that as he understood the refusal in Mudmonth, and did not thank him for it, because thanking a man for a thing he chose would have been a way of turning it into a debt.

At the top of Weir Street a boy said a thing about the eel-man at the market, and a woman said a thing about the bench, and neither of them said anything about the road warden sitting in a cart, and that was not kindness, it was a town that had said the chalk and had moved on to the day's trade.

In the room at the back of Tovan Kest's yard, which is the warm one, at the fourth hour, Aren Kest asked for a pen and Mara Vey brought him one, and Tovan Kest took the week-book off the nail and put it on the table and stood against the door, because a thing that is sworn wants a second hand on it and his was going to be one, and it was written out, on a sheet of the office's paper, with the hour in the corner, the whole of it, in the flat hand, and then read out loud to a keeper of records who had to write it down.

"Nineteen days," he said. "I was nineteen days in that world and six days have gone in this one, and the ninth was the ninth and this is the sixteenth, and the saying in this country is three days out and three days back, and I have been nine days in the calendar for every six I spent there and I have checked it four times and it does not add up, and I have written that down, and I am not going to stand in a room and pretend it does.

"The glass. Four marks the pair, and I have nothing. A mark and a quarter of the four was mine and I entered it as paid and not as credited. The other two marks and three quarters is a wage advance against my brother's cutting contract at eight pence a day, carried on if the cutting fails, entered on the guild roll as a debt and not as a surety, and it is in his book as a wage and not a favour, and it is a debt of mine to him and not a debt of his to me, and I have not paid a penny of it and I will not be able to for a long time. Eleven days of crew wages are owed by the trust and the trust has nothing in it until the frost, and eleven days of that came to my brother and not to the trust, and the town bill for those nine days is eleven marks and four pence and it is on a page of its own and I have never once let it share a page with anything.

"The fever. Three days the first time, the twenty-seventh to the twenty-ninth of Mudmonth, broken on the thirtieth. Three days now, from the fifteenth into the sixteenth, and it will break on the eighteenth if it is the same fever, and I have not got anybody to check that against but a day-book.

"The song. I still cannot land the verse on its note. I have tried it every day since the twenty-seventh and I have got the shape of it and about four words in the right order and the tune goes up and there is a place where it comes down and the place is not there. I have had nine weeks. It is not coming back, and I am not going to be able to prove that it is not coming back, and I would like the number of things in this account that cannot be improved on to be noticed and not the number of things that are unpleasant.

"The ninth was a market day. There were four hundred people in the square and there was not a road warden in it, and a clerk in a provincial town put a word in a book, and in a year that word is a fact about me and not an argument. I did not answer the summons. I am not going to tell you that I had a good reason, because I did not, and I had a reason in a way that a court would call a reason and I would call a convenience.

"The sergeant. Bram Ottery wrote a statement at his own table on the night of the second of Greenmonth and gave it to a clerk at the seventh hour on the fourth, and it cost him his stripes and nine and three months' pay and there is no promotion in it. I was not there. I have never spoken to him. He is the reason the finding says what it says about the crew, and I have used that statement in this office twice and never once thought about the man, and I have written that down here and I am not going to put it in a better light.

"And the trust. The Old Toll crossing's trust has been in abeyance since the fourth and it will be in abeyance for a year, because a municipal trust cannot be affirmed over a lawful Crown holding on the same stone, and that is your magistrate's finding and it is correct. So for a year that crossing has no owner. In a year this bench has to decide whether to restore a trust over a Crown's holding, and it will be a vote, and I am on the roll, because a road warden is elected in this room in Frostmonth, and I did not vote on the bond on the ninth because the bond was mine, and in a year the question will not be mine, and I have not got out of that and I have not been asked to."

He stopped, and drank the water, and got his breath the way a man gets his breath.

"And I am not going to get the bell back," he said. "I want that on the same sheet, in the same hand, in the same hour. It is in a watch storehouse under a Crown seal, lawfully, and a magistrate has said so in a public room, and the only way to get it is for somebody to break a law or for a Crown to be wrong, and it is not going to be either of those in my lifetime, and it is not going to be either of those by me, and I have thought about it in a city four days off where a bell with that founder's mark is standing under a tarred cloth on two trundles, and there is one piece of it in this world and it is not going to come down for me, and I have said it out loud in a filing room and I would like it read back."

Mara Vey had written all of it in her fair hand without once looking up, and at the end she read it back, and then she took a fresh sheet and wrote the heading and the date and the hour, and put the account into the same file and under the same claim number as the first one, so that a man who opens it in a year reads a seal and a date before he reads a man explaining himself, with a chain of custody going up from the clerk's hand through hers into a bar on a press in a room over a salt merchant, and a second witness, which was the same second witness as the first one, and his name was on the ninth page of a yard's week-book, and she checked it, because a second witness is worth nothing if she has not looked at the hand.

Then she did not write anything for about a minute, which she had never not done in four years.

"Do you want it read back now," she said, "or do you want it left?"

"Left," said Aren Kest. "It is a day-book and you have written it and it is right."

"No. The bell report." She had it out of the press and in her hand, and it was one sheet with four digits in the corner in her own fair hand, and it had been filed on the eighth of Greenmonth as *reported*, with its own limits written out in full. "The report of the seventh. An hour, a direction, a tower sight-line, three witnesses, and no conclusion. It has been in this building for eight days and it is the only paper in it that nobody can improve on, and you are the only man in the county whose name is on it, and you have not been in this room for nine days and I am not going to file anything beside it without your having heard it read out loud."

"Ma'am—"

"Sit up. It is four lines and it is on the file and you are going to hear it read exactly as it was written and then you are going to tell me whether a thing you said in a filing room nine days ago is still what you said."

So she read it, in the voice of a woman reading a record to a claimant, and she read the limits too, which is the part nobody ever asks for.

*"Reported. A sound described as a bell, heard on the seventh day of Greenmonth between the eleventh hour and midday, on or off the Old Toll crossing, in a dead calm, by two men on the Sedge Reach deck and one boy, thirteen, of Merefen, afterwards at the tower. One note, three seconds, of a bad and cracked sound. No bell is on this reach. The bell is in the custody of the watch of Merefen, under seal, in a storehouse by the fish weir, four hundred yards from the crossing described. Witnessed by the tower's sight-line to the weir and the storehouse door. The report is filed as reported. It is not offered as evidence of anything."*

And then she read the second part, which she had written out in full on the same sheet, and which was hers.

*"This office has no power to make a bell sound or to stop one. It has entered an hour, a direction, a sight-line, and the names of three witnesses, and it has entered them because they are the only parts that cannot be improved on by anybody."*

She put it down.

"Say the next thing," she said.

Aren Kest looked at the table for a while.

"You were going to tell me something at the first," said Mara Vey, "on the sixth, and you did not, and I have not asked you about it in nine days, and I have been told by a man at a bench-house with a bar on it that a keeper who does not read her own documents aloud in front of a claimant is a keeper who has stopped checking. So I am going to ask it once, in the plainest words I have, and you are going to answer it or tell me no, and either of those is the end of it."

"The fever," said Aren Kest.

"I have the three days on a sheet of my own paper, in my own hand, in the day-book, entered the fifteenth night from a watch statement and not from your mouth, and I did not file it, because I do not know whether it is a rule or a habit, and I was not going to write down a rule about a thing I could not check." She had her hands flat on the table. "What I want is not the number. What I want is what it does, because you have been in that world twice and the second time was nineteen days and you came back on a cart in under an hour from the bridge and I would have had to lift you off it."

"It is three days and it is not a number," said Aren Kest, "and you are right that I was going to tell you on the sixth, and I was not going to tell you because you are the keeper of the evidence and I had decided that a man does not hand a witness his own weaknesses, which is the single most stupid thing a man can do in a filing room and I worked it out from the inside, which is worse."

"Go on. You are not going to be able to do this quickly and we have got until the light goes."

"It is the body." He said it flat, and then had to stop for a moment, and it was not a dramatist's pause; it was a man who had got to the end of a sentence and had to wait for his hands. "Not the fever. The fever is the part everybody expects and the fever is three days and it is a cold and a bad head and being unable to stand, and I can bear the fever. It is the other thing and I have not said it to anybody, and I have had it since Mudmonth and I have not been asked.

"Nineteen days in a city of about four thousand people and the whole of my trade is the thing I am good at, and the good at it is the problem. On the sixth of Mudmonth I was the best man in that room and I broke a hull and opened a boy's hand because I was better than everybody there and I did it because being the best man in the room is the only place I have ever been able to stand without a fever, and every hour I spend being good at something in that world I am spending a day I have to pay for here.

"That is the whole of it. The bill is not the crossing. The bill is that the crossing is the only thing I am good at and I am better at it every time I go, and it costs more the better I get, and there is no way to be a man who crosses and is also a man who is worth employing, and the reason I could not tell you is that you are the only other person in my life who is as good at a thing as I am, and I have been managing you for two months."

Mara Vey sat quite still for a moment, and then she said:

"That is the second time you have told me something in this room that you had decided was a fact about the file and not about me, and the first time it was the night you had chosen and the second time it was this, and both times I let you go, and I want to tell you why, and I want to be exact about it, because I have been exact with you since the sixth and I am not going to stop now that you have handed me something.

"First time, on the sixth: I did not let you go. I put a claim number on a counterfoil and stamped a date on it and made you a file, and that is the opposite of letting you go, and you have known that for two months, and the reason it worked is that a file has a chain of custody and a chain of custody has a second witness and a date, and you cannot take a thing out of one of those rooms without a piece of paper saying you did.

"This time: I wrote it down." She had the pen in her hand and she had not looked at it. "The sixteenth of Greenmonth, YR 312, at the sixth hour, in this room, in the presence of the road warden and of a second witness, and the road warden has stated that the cost of a crossing to him is in the body and not in the calendar; that it is greater the better he becomes at the work on the far side; and that there is no arrangement of his hours that makes it less. And I have entered that in the file with the account and not in the day-book, because it is not my observation, it is his, and because the day-book is what I say and the file is what people can be held to.

"And now you are going to tell me what I am allowed to do with it, and I am telling you in advance that whatever you say, I am not going to be relieved by it, and I have thought about that for six days and I would like it known that I have thought about it for six days, and that is the first thing I have done in this office in four years that I have not done on the day."

"You are allowed to enter it," said Aren Kest, "and you are not allowed to make it a condition of anything, and I am not going to be able to promise you I will stop, and I am not going to be able to promise you I will not."

"I did not ask for a promise." Mara Vey wrote the hour in the margin and capped the ink. "I asked a man with a fever on him to hand me one true thing about himself that he had been managing for two months, and he has, and I have written it down, and he is going to lie in that room for two more days and I am going to let him, and on the eighteenth I am going to bring him the third thing in this file, which is the shelf register of the fourth press with a date on it and no name, and the fourth thing, which is a claim number I entered on my own account on Friday against nobody, and he is going to read both of them and he is going to tell me they are a mess."

"They are a mess," said Aren Kest.

"I know they are a mess," said Mara Vey. "They are a mess and they are mine and they are dated and that is all a mess has ever needed to be to survive a man in a provincial office who does not like me."

The fever went the way the road-crews' saying says it goes, and broke on the eighteenth of Greenmonth at about the sixth hour, and he was on his feet by the ninth and out in the street by the eleventh, thin as a wiped slate and with a right hand that had been useless for a day and a half and then was not.

On the nineteenth he walked the wharf road, which is four hundred yards of the Old Toll crossing, and it was shut, and it had been shut since the nineteenth of Mudmonth, and the wharf road in a fen town in Greenmonth is a road nobody uses, and there was a man working the Old Toll abutment with a bar.

He went up and stood on the near end of it.

The cradle was empty. The iron of it was rusted into the stone and the moss had come back over the whole of the swept rectangle, all of it, and would be over the corner of the bed again by the frost. The post was grey and the wax had not run and he did not touch it, and there was nothing in the air.

The seat was in the fourth course with two discs in it and a maker's mark under the near one and a pin and an oak collar, and it had been used twice, and it had a maker's page bound in a press over a salt merchant that said how it will fail, and there is no fresh Steppe glass north of Merefen before Longlight, and he had a third crossing of the season in him and one seat and no money and no maker.

Down on the wharf road the market board had been wiped and chalked again, and the clerk's rule was over every line but one, and the line without a rule was underneath, as it had been since the first of Greenmonth, and somebody had added a third.

It read:

*Bell taken by the crown on the 19th. Nobody asked. Ask Kest, he was there.*

*Bell taken by the crown on the 19th. Nobody asked. Ask Kest, he was gone.*

*Road warden in the cart on Friday. Asked nobody anything.*

Nobody had wiped it, and Tovan Kest was not the sort of man who wiped a board, and there was one line with a rule over it and two without, and a board is a document a town keeps without knowing it is keeping one, and in a year somebody was going to count those lines and it was going to be the only paper in Merefen that anybody could put in front of a bench.

He went to the toll-house at the second hour, because a man goes where the Crown's paper is, and the toll-clerk let him in and showed him a drawer, and on the outside of the drawer, in chalk, in an old man's good hand, there was a date and an hour and a sentence in which the word *no* stood three times.

*Crown letter, twelfth of Greenmonth, YR 312, second hour. Addressed to the road-keeper of Merefen. No such office in this county. Not received. Not answered.*

"Chalked on the outside," said Aren Kest.

"On the outside," said the toll-clerk. "In this office's hand. And there is a date on it, and the date is the whole of it, because a Crown's letter in a drawer in a toll-house is a rumour, and a Crown's letter in a drawer in a toll-house with a date and an hour and *not answered* on the outside in this office's hand is a fact that a man can look for, and looking for it is the only thing anybody has ever been able to make anybody do."

He stood in the wharf office in Merefen on the nineteenth day of Greenmonth with a clerk of the Crown's tolls in front of him, and he had nothing to say, and for once in eleven years the not having something to say was not a thing he was ashamed of.

---

He went to the yard at the fourth hour on the twentieth.

The gate was open and the trestles were up and there were four men on a face-stone job and a fifth doing cartage and the sound of iron on stone was going every four seconds, and a yard that has work in it is a yard that has a reason to be there, and this yard had work in it and the work had a factor's name at the top of the paper and a third party's name in the second column.

"Six marks," he said.

"Against a chest at three and a half," said Tovan Kest, without looking up, "at a shilling a day until there is a shilling a day. Entered as a debt of yours and not of the guild's, entered as not proposed by you, and the minute says the surety was not in front of the bench when it was taken and the reeve was. I have got the minute."

"You had one sentence before the vote."

"I had one sentence before the vote and I have it minuted in the position it was said in, which is before, and I would have said it after if it had been after, and the clerk wrote down the words." Tovan put the chisel down, which was the first time in eleven years that he had put a tool down in the middle of a cut, and he came round the bench, and his brother was thinner and greyer than he had been on the eighth of Greenmonth and was standing in a yard in daylight for the first time in nine days, and his brother looked at him for a while and then said:

"Two marks and three quarters."

"Two marks and three quarters, at eight pence a day, against the cutting, carried on if the cutting fails, and it is a wage and not a favour and my mark and a quarter in the same line as paid and not as credited." Tovan Kest took the week-book off the nail and put it on the bench and opened it at the ninth page. "And it will not be forgiven. If the cutting does not go through you carry it, and I will stand in front of the bench and say the debt is mine and the yard's, and I will not say your name in that sentence, and I said that in front of the clerk on the eighth of Greenmonth in front of five men and it is the fourth page of that week and you can read it."

Aren Kest read the fourth page of the week-book in a yard on Weir Street, at the fourth hour, standing up, in a coat that had been wet for nine days, and his hands were not steady and he put them flat on the bench, which is a thing a man does so that nobody has to notice.

"Right," he said.

"Right," said Tovan Kest. "Now. There is a thing I have to give you and I would rather give it to you in the yard than at the table, because I have worked out that I can be a great deal more honest on a trestle than I can be in a room, and I have known that about myself for ten years and it has cost me a house."

He took a folded sheet out of the bench-clerk's copy and put it on the weight.

"Copied out of the week-book and entered in the guild's book on the tenth, with the date and the hour, in the clerk's hand, and *no reason given*, and there is a copy in the guild's chest and a copy in a press over a salt merchant." He put his hand flat on it. "I want you to read the ninth page in my own hand as well and I want you to know that the clerk came into this yard on the tenth and entered it of his own motion because I asked him to and said so, and I have never in nineteen years had a clerk come into this yard and do a thing I did not have to beg for, and it is the only good thing that has happened to this yard in a month and I am not going to be able to explain it to anybody, so I am not going to try, and I have written it in the yard book with the date and the hour, which is the same trick the toll-clerk did with a Crown letter, and I learned it off him, and I would like that noted."

Aren Kest read the ninth page.

Then he said, "Read it to me again," and Tovan Kest read it again, standing up, in his own hand, the whole of it, and his voice did not go on the page about a road.

Afterwards there was nothing else to say about it.

He walked back down Weir Street at the sixth hour on the twentieth of Greenmonth, on the arm of a man who would not let him walk the last hundred yards, and he was thinner than he had been on the eighth and his right hand had been useless for a day and a half and was now simply a hand, and the fever was gone and the three days had been three days and there was no fourth day, and the thing that surprised him was how little of a man he was and how completely able to walk.

The road did what it always did. The town said the chalk.

He went and looked at the market board, which is not a square but a board a clerk wipes every morning, with a rule over every notice, where a line without a rule is a thing somebody has put there on purpose. There were five lines on it. Two had rules. Three did not. One of the three said *Ask Kest, he was there*, and one said *Ask Kest, he was gone*, and the third said *Road warden in the cart on Friday. Asked nobody anything*, and the man who had written that one was no longer a man who had ever been in a room with him, and it would still be on the board in Fallowmonth, and in Embermonth, and on a day in a year when somebody wanted to know what this town had believed.

He went and looked at the drawer in the toll-house, which had a Crown's letter in it and a date and an hour and three noes in chalk on the outside of it, and the toll-clerk let him stand and read the chalk without saying anything, which was the correct response and the only one that would have been any use.

And then Aren Kest went and got his own notebook out of the coat he had crossed in, and stood on the wharf road where the road was shut, and read the four lines he had written at the fourth camp on the back of his own entry, and then he did a thing he had not done in nineteen days and had not expected to do, which was to turn the page forward instead of back.

There were four entries on it that he had never seen, and he knew that, and he did not know what any of them said, and he would not find out for a season, and the road would be shut the whole of that season on a bench's terms with a named person's hand on it, and there was no door in either world that he could find with a hand and a willingness.

He shut the notebook and put it back in the coat and went home up two flights of stair to a room over a cooper, and he was eleven days in arrears and eleven days further off a wage and two marks and three quarters down on a cutting contract at eight pence a day, and there was a bench that sat again in a year, and a claim number in a filing room over a salt merchant that would be answered in a year and a day whether anybody wanted it or not, and a bell-shaped hole in a parapet four hundred yards up the Old Toll road, and a town that was going to say the chalk until somebody made it say something else.

He had a bell to think about and a road to wait for and a debt at eight pence a day, and he could not do any of them until the third of a season, and he had put that third into a book in a guild hall four days off, in exchange for a promise with a date on it, and the price of the promise was a seat in a bridge that nobody had yet cut.

Tomorrow was the twenty-first of Greenmonth, and there were eight hundred setts to dress, and the iron went on the stone every four seconds.
