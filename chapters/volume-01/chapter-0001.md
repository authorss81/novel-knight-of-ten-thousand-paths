# Chapter 0001

Rain came across the west channel flat and hard, the kind that does not fall so much as travel, and it made a working afternoon of the Sedge Reach deck in the grey hour before dusk.

Aren Kest knelt at the third pier with his left forearm in the water to just below the elbow, feeling along the timber where the nose came out of the mud, while one of the two journeymen he could still afford this month held a lantern over his shoulder and did not pretend to be warmer than he was. The river had a voice that changed with the swell. He had been listening to it in this town for eleven years and he could still read it the way a man reads a hand on a tool.

The scour had opened another hand's breadth since the thaw. That was the whole report, and it was enough to ruin somebody's week.

He came up onto the deck first and walked it, because a man does not go under a structure in flood stage until he knows where it is putting its weight. Eight load points, four bays, two to a bay, at the pier heads and at the middle of each span; he had been counting them off in his head since he was thirteen years old and had never once found a reason to stop. The third bay's inner point was the one he wanted. A four-bay deck at flood stage carries its worst load not at the piers but a third of the way out, where the beam is bending down into moving water and the rail is the only thing between a cart's driver and his own centre of gravity.

"Somebody's board says seven and a bit," the older journeyman offered, nodding up the channel at the gauge post on the Sedge pier. "Seven and a quarter, chalked Tuesday."

"Tuesday's Tuesday," Aren said. "That post reads what the river did on Tuesday."

"Which'll be a comfort to the millers."

"It's a comfort to me. I only have to hold the deck till Thursday." He took his arm out of the water and dried it on his coat, and looked at the hand. Under the nail of the second finger the skin had gone the white of a drowned candle and would blister before dark. Two shillings a month for a room over a cooper on Weir Street, and the room had a draught in it that could carry a candle flame sideways, and he paid the cooper's rent in coin and the cooper's silence in scraps of offcut iron. This was the arithmetic of being thirty-two and able to read a river.

He chalked the pier anyway. A short bar at the height the water had reached this afternoon, a longer one below it, and the date, because the town used marks on the Reed Line to date floods and a mark on a bridge was the same kind of record. The chalk stub was nearly gone. He turned it over and looked at what was left of it and made a decision he would forget to record: he would buy a stick at the foundry on Friday, and the foundry would charge him, and he would pay it.

Then the arguing started on the abutment, as it did every time the water came up.

The toll-holder's man had come out of the rain with a waxed coat and an umbrella nobody else in Merefen could afford, and he stood on the dry stones at the top of the steps where the towpath met the bridge head and asked, for the third time, whether the road would carry a loaded cart on Thursday.

"It'll carry a light load," Aren said. "Not a cart with glass in it. Not on Thursday."

"That's not an answer I can put in a book."

"It's the answer you'll have to put in a book." He put his weight on the rail and looked at the third pier again from above, where the deck boards had been lifted at the near end so a man could see down into the dark under the bay. "Two spans in, the nose is going. If I put oak in it this week I can carry carts by the ninth. If I wait, I'll be doing it in Highsun for twice the money, and the millers will be under a foot of water in the meantime."

The millers' man, who had come up wet to the thigh and had no umbrella at all, said, "Then give us a pass on the water and we'll not be under anything." The ferry company wanted the span declared unsafe. That was the whole of their position and they had it ready, because the ferry company's income died when the road opened and lived when the road shut, and they had been saying so at every bridge meeting for longer than either of them had been in the trade.

"And what do you pay for the pass?" the toll-holder's man asked him.

"It's a bridge crew's loss is a mill's gain," the miller said. "That's arithmetic."

The carter from the fourth-day market stood at the back with his hood down in the rain, because a carter's hood is a thing you take off when you want people to look at you. He wanted the road open. He had no vote, because a carter is not on the bridge trust and never had been, and he said so, cheerfully, twice, as if saying it twice might put him on the roll.

Aren let them finish. He had learned early that a bridge meeting is not a discussion, it is four people arriving with their arithmetic already done and waiting to see whether his would move.

"Here's mine," he said. "The road carries light loads through Thursday. No carts, no glass, no heavy salt. Ferrymen can work the channel as they always do and I will not have your hulls under my piers without telling me first, because I cannot see what I am standing on. The millers pay half the fare for the boat pass, the same as the ninth-day market, or the pass waits until the water drops." He looked at the miller's man. "I'd rather have your money than your gratitude, but I'll take the money, and the same goes for all of you."

"That is not in the trust's book," the toll-holder's man said.

"No. It's in mine. The book's yours. If you want mine in the book, put it to the trust and I'll sign it in front of them and they'll hold me to it."

The toll-holder's man considered this, and the wind came down the channel and lifted the corner of his coat, and he said, "Thursday," which from him was practically a decoration.

They went. The barge was lying off downstream with her mast stepped and a boy on the bow shouting for a line, because a barge cannot sit idle in a fen and a barge that sits idle is paying for the privilege at both ends. Her master came up the steps with a folded paper in a waxed jacket and a voice like a door shutting, and he did not ask how the day had gone, because barge masters never do.

"I want a day, Kest."

"You get the ninth."

"And if the ninth's no good?"

"Then you get the ninth of the next month, and you'll be able to tell that story better than I can." He took out the notebook and stood in the wind with it, which is the correct way to write in a wind and the reason half of his pages are half a line long. "Here's what will happen and you can correct me. The deck's shut from Thursday. You sit off the wharf and lose two days of carrying, because the wharf road is four hundred yards of my bridge road and there is no other way to load. When it opens you come in a day late with a hold of glass nobody in this town wants in Mudmonth, and you go back down the channel a day late, and the millers' man tells every man on the wharf that the fault is the bridge, and the bridge is me, and it will be me for about a year."

"Then I'll hold you to the ninth."

"Hold me to the ninth," Aren said, and wrote it down, and watched a man who had come for a date go away with the truth instead, which had never once in his life made anybody's week better.

"Hold this," Aren said, and put the chalk in the journeyman's hand, and went down onto the third pier with the lamp hooked on his own belt and his back to the rail so the wind would take it and not him.

The batten under the third bay had opened a split at the shear line, an inch and a finger's depth, and the deck was carrying carts across a joint that was busy moving. He could have let it go until Thursday. Instead he got down there and put his palm flat on the batten and made the small promise he knew how to make: not *hold*, which is a promise to a person and would be a lie, but *stay where you are until the water stops and the deck comes off me*. The anchor was the batten. The bound was as narrow as he could make it, the width of the split, the length of the timber. The counterweight was his hand and the burn in the old scar on his forearm where a working had gone wrong nine winters ago and had never quite stopped being a place that hurt in cold weather.

There was no fourth part. Not a proper one. In eleven years of road work he had learned the third part better than the others, and he had learned it the way a man learns a knot he tied in the dark a hundred times.

The wood did not stop moving. It stopped *being heard*, which is a different thing and the only one anybody has ever been able to buy. The deck settled a fraction. Somewhere in the timber the oathlight came and went, dull as a lamp seen through cloth, and the rain went on sideways, and Aren Kest sat with his boot in the mud on the third pier of a four-bay timber bridge in a fen town and held a batten together by wanting it to, and got the day out of it.

The older journeyman found him there when the light had gone entirely.

"The bell's been off the old crossing all morning," the man said. "They've had men up on the abutment looking at it. The guild wants it gone off the books before the sitting."

"I know they do."

"You could sell it. The roll's six marks."

"Less the split."

"Less the split, and there's four men in the yard who'd melt it tomorrow for four and a half and call themselves your friends." The journeyman shifted the lantern. "You know what it is, don't you. It's the one that was on the old Bellweather tolling, before the fever."

"It weighs nine stone and it's split through the mouth," Aren said. "It's the only thing in Merefen I could buy for two pence that was ever going to be worth less than that."

"You paid two pence at the clearance. For a bell."

"I paid two pence for the bronze," Aren said, "which is what it is. It's green-black and it's got a river in it and it isn't going to sell to a man who wants a bell. He wants a story. I've got no use for a story, I've got a use for nine stone of good metal." That was what he told people, and it was true, and it was not the reason.

He had been in the yard at Tovan's on the first of Mudmonth, when the Crown's clearance lot came under the hammer and the whole line of scavengers and speculators in Merefen had laughed at the man who took a cracked municipal bell off the block for two pence. Four hands had bought it before him and sold it on the same day, each of them certain that a cracked bell was not a curiosity but a liability, and each of them right about that. A foundry would charge forty marks to recast the tools he needed this summer, and a man who spends forty marks on tools is a man who has not been paid for work he has not done.

He wrote in the notebook that night, in the room over the cooper, with the draught moving the flame: *Old Toll, near abutment. Sill cut stone, repacked the loose length, three lengths still to do. Bell on its cradle, split lip to shoulder, nine stone by the yoke. Guild wants it off the roll. Hold signed, to the 24th.*

Below the entry, because he was that kind of man, he wrote the number: *Six marks, less the split.*

The cooper's wife brought him bread and cheese and a cup of the small beer and stood in the doorway while he ate, because the draught was on her side and the fire was in the room.

"Thirteen days till the nineteenth," she said.

"Yes."

"They're saying there's a storm."

"There'll be a storm." Every eleventh year, in Mudmonth, the same sky had come over the same river, and the town had watched it do it, and the Reed Line along the old raised road had stood up out of the mud in a straight cut a foot high for a fortnight after, and the story went round the year that the water that took Bellweather was waiting for its date. Nobody said it in front of the children. Her two were upstairs.

"You'll be at the market," the cooper said from the bottom of the stairs, not coming up. "Fourth-day."

"I'll be at the bridge," Aren said.

Which was the answer of a man who had stopped explaining himself to people who were being kind to him, and it was also the answer of a man with a bell on a cradle and a hold in his own name and thirteen days to decide what either was for.

It took him until near midnight to write the hold properly, in the guild's own form, which the cooper's cousin had taught him to keep and which the guild had never once refused to accept from a man who could pay.

*Held at the Old Toll crossing, the bell described as unclassified municipal salvage, Bellweather, crack through the mouth. Surety: the tool-chest of Aren Kest. Good until the sitting of the bridge guild, the twenty-fourth of Mudmonth, YR 312.*

Six marks. He could not raise six marks, not this month, and the guild clerk would write that down, and the clerk would ask whether the chest was good for the sum, because the chest was worth three marks and half of that was rust. He said yes. That was a thing that would be true in a way he had not examined, and he left it true.

He signed it at the top of the page, and beneath the signature he wrote the date twice, the sixth, and then he sat a while with the pen not moving, in a room where a woman he was not married to was waiting for him to stop making noise.

He was twenty-one at the Sundering. That was a number the Crown used constantly, in the taverns and on the wharf and in the middle of arguments about the bridge, and he had stopped correcting it about the fourth year, because a boy could be argued with and a captain who lost a city could not. He had been in the Guard since he was seventeen and had been given a field captain's commission by brevet four months before the nineteenth, and every man who ever made that appointment said the same two things about it: the boy had a head for a load path, and nobody with a following would ever do what they needed done. He had been the youngest captain in the Guard's history. He had held a real bridge with real men under him and he had really failed at it, and there was no version of that anybody in Merefen was prepared to hear, and no version of it which was not, in the end, a true account of a piece of the truth.

He got the coat off the back of the door where it hung.

The breast pocket had a seam in it he had sewn himself at seventeen, and inside the seam there was what had been in it since Embermonth of YR 301: eleven days of a different kind of writing, on nine leaves, in his own hand, taken on the Alder crossing and on the road behind it and in a room in the low quarter of a city he had not reached. Water had got into two of the leaves and taken the ink off one line of a fourth. He had not read them in eleven years. He knew roughly what was on them, because he had been in the room when he wrote them, and what was on them was not the fever.

What was on them was a bridge, a district, forty men, an order that named three things, and a sequence of hours.

And the word he had used, three years running, in four debriefs and twice to a man from the Guard's own office who had been kind about it, was *fever*.

He put the coat back on the hook, and sat down again, and the nib had dried in the well of the pen while he was not looking at it, and he had to fetch water up from the kitchen step to soften it, and the two children came down the landing at some point after that and were put to bed without either of them appearing to notice that a man was sitting in the front room with a coat on and a pen in his hand.

Somewhere below, the river went over the Sedge Reach in four bays and found the third pier and worked at it, the way it had worked at it all day, and the way it would go on working at it through Thursday.

Aren capped the ink, blew out the candle, and slept with his back to the wall.
