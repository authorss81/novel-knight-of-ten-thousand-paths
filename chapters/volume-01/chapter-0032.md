# Chapter 0032

The twenty-first of Greenmonth was a Wednesday, and the Bellweather Claims Archive did not know it was a Wednesday, and Mara Vey came up the second flight at the fourth hour with a lamp she did not need because the light came in sideways off the river and made the floor of the filing room look like a wet road, which is the light of every morning of her four years in this building and the reason she has never once been able to decide whether the room is cold.

Here is the whole of what she had, at the fourth hour, laid out on the table in the order a court would want it and not the order it had arrived in.

The bell report of the seventh of Greenmonth, filed as reported, with an hour, a direction, a tower sight-line, and three witnesses, one of them thirteen years old, and the full text of what it does not say.

A claim number, hers, the thirty-first of Mudmonth, holding a counterfoil that had been read aloud at a north gate in YR 301 and refused on a deck, with a stamp on it and a date and a chain of custody with three hands on it.

A sworn account in a man's own hand, beneath the counterfoil, that put him in the wrong on purpose.

A copyist's attestation, in Sarn Oyelaran's own hand, that a page in a Crown bound order was out of place and in a hand not of the roll, with the date and the hour and no opinion, in a wrapper in the press and not in the building's book.

A magistrate's finding of the fourth of Greenmonth, in a county file two hundred miles off, five clauses and a private minute at the foot saying the finding is not a victory.

A *memorial* that had arrived on the twelfth and was the reason she had not slept on the thirteenth.

And a *petition*, because it had come up the stair at the ninth hour of the previous day and was lying in the outer room on the bench, in a provincial clerk's hand, with a prayer at the end of it that was not a question and was going to cost this office more than the memorial ever had.

Sarn Oyelaran came in at the fourth hour with a cold hand and put a sheet on the table and said, "The order came on the provincial post at the sixth hour yesterday. It was stamped at the provincial court at the second hour of the eighteenth, and it came off a cart at the sixth of this morning, and I have not opened it, and I have read the outside twice, and I would like you to read the outside and then tell me whether I have done the right thing by not opening it."

"You have done exactly the right thing," said Mara Vey. "Go and get the day-book."

---

"It is a *memorial's second cousin*," she said, at the fifth hour, with the lamp lit in daylight because she wanted the shadow of her own hand on the wall behind the paper. "That is what I want said first, before anything else, and I want Sarn to write it down in her own hand and not in mine, and I want the date on it, because in a year I am going to be asked to explain the difference between three instruments and I will not be able to unless I wrote it down at the time."

Sarn wrote it down.

"All right." Mara Vey read the outside of it once and then turned it round so the seal was up. Grey wax, the size of a crown piece, a hand laid flat on a book. "The seal is the third one. Post, storehouse, and now an order on this office. Sarn, that is a fact and not a piece of writing, and I am going to have it in a file in a fair hand inside a month or it will have happened for nothing.

"Three instruments, in the order this county uses them, and the order is the order in which they work. A **memorial** is a paper a person writes who cannot sue; it asks for nothing in particular, and the answer to it is nothing, because a thing with no answer on it has never been answered, and that is our standing rule and it is in the day-book in my own hand and in yours. A **petition** is the same paper with a man's name on the top of it and a court named at the head. An **order** is a third thing and it is the one that works."

She had it open by then. It was one page. It was returnable.

"Read me the returnable date."

"The ninth of Longlight."

"Three days from the eighteenth. Count it. Today is the twenty-first, so it is three days in this building and there are eighteen days to the ninth." She sat down. "Sarn, when a county has no superior court inside a day's ride, a returnable order is worth what the distance is worth. It is worth a day of yours for every hundred miles, and it is worth the clerk's patience, and it is worth the fee, and it is worth precisely one thing above all: it is worth a court. And a return is a date on which this office either produces a thing or does not, and both of those are entered."

"I will be here on the ninth."

"You will be here on the ninth and so will I and so will a man from the provincial court who will be in this room with a form, because that is what the ninth is, and the twelfth of Greenmonth is a *memorial* that came into this room and died in a week, and this is its second cousin and it has a seal on it, and if the twelfth was a complaint and this is a frame, then somebody in a provincial office has read a complaint and written a frame, and the frame has one job and the job is to put a date round this office and then wait for the date."

"And the three of them," said Sarn Oyelaran, "and which is the one that works."

"The order," said Mara Vey. "And I am going to tell you why in a room in a month, in front of a man who is a worse lawyer than you are, and not in this room at the seventh hour with a lamp going, because I have nineteen days to do it in and a return to answer on the ninth, and I have never once in four years got the machinery right inside the hour I was given it." She put two fingers on the sheet. "Write *order* at the head of a page, and the date, and nothing else."

She read the prayer.

"And now the part that is why I have not slept, and I did not sleep last night either, and I am going to say it out loud in a room because the alternative is thinking about it alone until the eighth.

"Read the prayer again, slowly, and count the questions in it."

Sarn Oyelaran read it.

*"And that the said respondent do state in writing, by the day named, all copies of records touching the said crossing of which she or any other person in this county has knowledge, and the names and descriptions of the persons by whom such copies are held, and the places in which they are kept."*

"Two questions," said Mara Vey, "and neither of them is about records."

"No."

"The first is *how many places in this county hold a copy*, and the second is *the names of the people who hold them*, and there is not one word in that prayer about the villages, or about a fever, or about a claim, and there is not one word in it that this office is bound to answer, and it is the best-drafted instrument I have ever read. It asks a question that has no correct answer. If I say three, I have described this office. If I say one, I have lied, and I have also made this office safe for exactly as long as it takes the holder of the one to be found, which is a month. If I say nothing I have defaulted on a returnable order, and a default on a returnable order in a county with no superior court inside a day's ride is a thing a person is built out of over eleven years, not eleven months, and I know that because I have read four of them in this room and helped write one of them for a man in Mudmonth.

"And it is drafted by somebody who has read our day-book." She put two fingers on the paper. "Look at the second column. *A man of Hushwater, twice, in his own name. A woman without a name, thrice, with a stone. A road warden of Merefen, four times. And the petitioner.* That is a schedule of attendance. It is not an accusation. I want that said, and I want it written down, because I am about to be angry about it and the anger is going to want it to be an accusation, and it is not one. It is a *channel*. The petition is not asking what we hold. The petition is asking who comes here, and the answer to that is printed in the second column, and somebody has been in this outer room on four occasions and written down who came, and put a description on each of them that would let a man in a provincial office decide which of the four was worth following.

"And it was written by the same office that wrote the memorial on the twelfth. That is the cousin. Both of them begin with a woman in a room over a salt merchant, and one of them asks that she be instructed and the other one asks that she be made to answer, and the family resemblance is that neither of them wants a thing. They want this office to be *legible*."

---

"He did not tell me to be quiet, ma'am."

"He told you the truth, which is more expensive."

Rowan Petch was twenty-six and had been four years in the filing room of the provincial court and had walked up two flights of a stair in a Merefen salt-loft house on a Wednesday morning and told a keeper of records in her own doorway that the county's own file was going to make a Crown's case better than the Crown could make it.

"Why did you do it," said Mara Vey. "I am asking that as a professional and not as a woman being flattered, and if you tell me it was kindness I am going to stop asking and start arranging my furniture."

"Because I wrote the particular."

"Say the rest of it."

He said the rest of it. He had been a junior clerk for four years and he had a good hand and a bad position, and in Rainmonth, when the county's file on the matter of the crossing of Alder Vale was opened because a magistrate had put a finding into it on the fourth of Greenmonth, the file went onto his desk, and on the file was a claim exhibit wrapper with a four-digit number on the outside of it in a fair hand, dated the thirtieth of Mudmonth, and the exhibit itself was not in the file because the exhibit was held outside Crown custody and had never been delivered and could not be.

"And we do that every time," said Petch. "Every time a file has an exhibit we do not hold, the clerk writes a particular. That is the practice. You state what the thing is, from the description on the wrapper, and from anything the wrapper refers to, and you enter it in the file so that a man reading the file in twenty years knows what is in the box. It is the only part of the work anybody ever looks at and it is the part nobody has ever been taught.

"So I wrote the particular from your wrapper, and your wrapper is a fair hand and it is careful, and it says the exhibit is a stub of an instrument of severance tendered and returned and withdrawn, and it says the instrument was sealed, and it says in the margin, in a smaller hand, *the seal is that of the marshal's emergency office, that office having produced nothing to this court.*

"And I wrote all of that out," said Petch, "because that is what a particular is."

"And in writing it out you put the words *the marshal's emergency office* into a file of this court."

"Yes."

"Which is an office that this court has formally asked to be produced and has not been answered, on the fourth of Greenmonth, by a magistrate, with a clause saying that any office thereafter acting in the matter is on notice, and with the asking dated."

"Yes," said Petch. "And the date on the asking is the fourth of Greenmonth, and the date on the particular is the ninth, and I put it in on the ninth, and I did not think about the first date at all, because I was not reading your finding, I was reading your wrapper, and your wrapper was good, and I am telling you the truth about the order of it because I have not slept either."

Nobody in the room said anything for about half a minute.

"I am going to ask you one question and it is the one question I have," said Mara Vey, "and then you are going to go down that stair and I am going to write down what you have told me and you are going to sign it.

"The same file. In the same file, in your own hand, in the same year — is there any *other* document in it that names an office?"

"No."

"You have looked?"

"I have looked at every leaf in it, ma'am, because I wrote the particular and then I could not stop, and I went back and read the finding, and I read the magistrate's minute, and I read the sheet the town clerk sent up with the village names on it, and I read all of it twice, and I want you to know what I found, which is that the finding of the fourth of Greenmonth is the best thing in that file. It is the only document in the county's file on this matter that anybody has ever written in a hurry and that reads as though nobody was."

"That is not a compliment. It is an attack on every other document in the file, and it is the best attack anybody in this county has made on the Crown in four years, and I am not going to be in the room when you make it again, because I have nothing to add to it. Write it on your own book and take it out of the county. Now the question.

"**Your particular names an office that no court in this country can produce.** And the only reason that particular exists is that a keeper of records outside Crown custody wrote a careful line in a small hand in the margin of a wrapper, in her own building, on the thirtieth of Mudmonth, eleven days before a provincial renewal of the county's finding that nothing survives, and then sent that wrapper into a court, where a clerk in a filing room did his duty by it.

"So here is the thing I want entered in my day-book this morning in my own hand and yours beneath it, and I am going to write it in front of you and you are going to read it over my shoulder and not behind me.

"That the word *keeper of the Bellweather hinge* and the words *the marshal's emergency office* stand in a file of the provincial court, in a copyist's hand, entered on the ninth of Greenmonth, YR 312, and that they stand there by the operation of this office's own rule of producing copies and citing dates, and that this office cannot unwrite them, and that the only instrument that could unwrite them is the exhibit itself, and that the exhibit cannot be produced into that court without the thing it exists to keep out of that court being produced with it."

She wrote it. It took a page and a half. He read it over her shoulder and she did not stop him and did not let him read it behind her, and when she had finished she turned it round and put it in front of him.

"Sign under that. In your own hand. With the hour."

"I would like it to say that I disclosed it of my own motion and not on a fee," said Petch, "because in about two years somebody is going to look at this sheet and decide whether I found it or was given it, and the difference is the whole of my life where I work."

"It already says so. I wrote it before you asked, because I have been in this room four years and I know what a clerk in a filing room needs written over his name, and it is always the same sentence, and it is always written by somebody else, because he cannot write it himself without it looking like a defence."

He signed it. She entered it in the day-book at the fifth hour and a half of the twenty-first of Greenmonth, in the fair hand, in the second column, with the hour, and read it back.

"And now the other thing, and it is the reason I have not slept, and it is not the first reason, it is the second.

"You will bring me a copy of that particular. Not certified. The ordinary copy, taken out of the file for a man who has to see it, with the court's stamp on it and the date on it and no reason entered for the taking, because there is no form for taking a thing out of a file when you are not a party to the file and you are not a lawyer and you are a woman over a salt merchant's counter. I will pay the fourpence and I will enter the fourpence in the day's column, because this office keeps its accounts and I would rather be a fool with fourpence out of it than a mystery."

"You will be reported."

"I will be reported," said Mara Vey, "and there is a stamp on a drawer in this town with three *noes* in chalk on the outside of it, and the *noes* are in a toll-clerk's hand, and one of them says *not answered*. And I am going to be reported, and I am going to be dull about it, and I want you to know that I have thought about the fourpence for about an hour and I am going to spend it anyway, because the thing I have bought with it is not the paper. The thing I have bought is a man in a provincial filing room who now knows that a thing he wrote is going to be read by somebody with a lamp in a salt-loft house, and a man who knows that is a man who is careful for the rest of his career, and I would rather have a careful man in that room than a copy of a piece of paper, and I am aware of how that sounds, and I am also aware that it is a tactic and not a reason, and I have written four years of records about the difference."

---

He went down the stair at the sixth hour, and Sarn Oyelaran stood in the doorway with the order in her hand and her face doing the thing it did when she had been handed a document that was going to be a problem for somebody she loved.

"Ma'am. The exhibit."

"I cannot produce it," said Mara Vey.

"You can produce it. It is in that press, under the bar, with a stamp on the wrapper and a date on the stamp and four hands on the chain. I have seen the chain. I copied two pages of the chain for my own hand when I had the third post in front of me and I did not put my name on anything, which I am now going to regret for the rest of my life, and if you produce it on the ninth of Longlight the file has the thing itself in it and everything Rowan Petch wrote becomes true instead of copied, and there is no hole in it at all."

"And then the thing the office exists to keep out of Crown custody is in Crown custody," said Mara Vey, "and it is in there because a provincial court asked for it by name in an order with a seal on it, and there is nothing in this building that can refuse an order with a seal on it except a magistrate, and there is no magistrate in this county who can touch a provincial order, and I have already spent Lysa Fenn this year."

"You could refuse and take the default."

"And be a woman with a default on a returnable order," said Mara Vey, "instead of a woman with a missing exhibit, and in eleven years somebody writes a paragraph about the keeper of the salt loft who had a thing in her press and would not give it up, and the paragraph is two sentences and both of them are true, and there is no third sentence. That is how a record office is made to stop existing without a single man being hurt. Not by an order that says shut. By a paragraph."

She put the order down.

"Now here is the second reason I have not slept, and it is the one I did not tell him about, because he has four years of his own to carry and it is mine."

She took a slip out of the day-book and put it on the table.

"Jos Rusk," she said. "Second column of the petition. *A man of Hushwater, twice, in his own name.* That is now in a provincial file, on a Crown's petition, in a Crown's clerk's hand, as an attendee at this office on a matter that is a Crown's claim, and it is there because the Crown wanted to know who comes here, and it is *twice, in his own name*, and in his own name is the whole of it, because a man who comes to a claims office under an assumed name has not done anything and a man who comes under his own name has filed a claim in a court, and that is a thing a Crown can build on for a decade.

"I gave that man a claim number in Mudmonth. I entered the cause of his village in dispute in my own hand and on purpose weakened it so that it could be disproved in a year, and I told him the cost of that before I signed it, and he is alive and he is above a chandler's on the north end and he is owed a hearing as long as this case is open. And his name has just gone into a file in a building two hundred miles off because he came here twice and said who he was.

"I told him once, in Mudmonth, that I would keep his name out of things. I have never put that in writing, because I would not put a promise in writing that I could not keep, and that was the correct decision, and I have been right about that four times in four years and I would like the record to show that I was right about that as well and wrong about everything else in the same month."

"What will you do?"

"I will tell him," said Mara Vey, "and it will cost me him, and there is no version of this in which it does not, and I am going to do it today, in this room, with a witness, and I am going to give him the petition's second column and the date on it and the hour, and I am not going to apologise, because I did not put his name in it and I am not sorry that he came."

---

She made the copies at the eighth hour, and she did them in the filing room, at the table, in the light, and she did not do them in a hurry and she did not do them anywhere else, and both of those decisions were made out loud to Sarn Oyelaran and entered in the day-book with the reason, because a thing done for a reason that is not written down is a thing that is done for a reason somebody will invent later.

The document was one sheet and it was the oldest thing in the office and it was the whole method.

*That the Bellweather Claims Archive holds, of the crossing of Alder Vale and of the villages dependent upon it, and does not deliver any of it into the custody of the Crown, and may be closed only by the assembly of this town: entered in the instrument of foundation of this office, in the year of its founding, in the hand of a lawyer of the town, and preserved entire at the head of this office's own book of instruments, and not in the county's file and not in the provincial court's, because the whole of the reason this office exists is the sentence at the end of it.*

*A schedule of what this office holds was then made, in a fair hand, and entered in the day-book with the day and the hour of every item: what it is, where it came from, who brought it, in what hand, and on what authority.*

"Three copies," said Mara Vey. "The press, for the ordinary reason, and the press is inside this office and outside the Crown, and the whole of my life has been the argument that a press inside a room over a salt merchant is a thing a Crown cannot be given by a clerk in a filing room who never went up the stair.

"The second is Sarn's.

"Sarn, look at me. On the eighth of Greenmonth I entered a four-line warrant for a third post in this office in the day-book and not in a file, and I have not looked at it since, and I have been waiting for the warrant to be a lie for five weeks. I am entering it now as a matter of office: you are a keeper of records in this building, of the second press, with your own hand and your own ear, and you will have a key, and the key is a thing a person can lose, and if you lose it I will say on the day-book that I lost it and not that you did, and you will countersign that sentence, and it is in the same ink as the rest.

"That is the second copy, and the reason is that a record held by one keeper is a favour, and I have been one keeper for four years, and a favour can be stopped by one man, and one man is a thing this office has been living on the edge of since the sixth of Mudmonth."

She wrote the warrant out, and Sarn Oyelaran read it, and Sarn Oyelaran signed it, and Mara Vey wrote the hour on both, and then she did the third.

"The third goes down Weir Street in a cart, in an envelope, to a man who is not a clerk and not a keeper and not me, and it goes to him in a yard, and it is a copy of a schedule of holdings, and a schedule of holdings is the single most dangerous paper in this room, because it is a list of where everything is.

"And I am going to send it to the one man in this county who has already been asked three times in a month to put his hand on another body's account and has said no three times, and who has, in a sentence he said out loud in a room to five men, understood exactly what it costs to hold a page with a date on it in a yard on a street where a man comes down it every day."

---

The yard at the fourth hour of the twenty-first, and Tovan Kest had four men on a stone contract and a factor's name at the top of the paper and the iron going on the trestles every four seconds, and his brother was standing at the bench with an envelope in his hand and no envelope-case and no ceremony.

"That is a list of everything in a building two flights above your head," said Aren Kest. "Every item, where it came from, who brought it, in what hand, and on what authority, and there are eleven items and four of them are the reason a bell is in a storehouse under a seal. It is not a copy of any of them. It is a list of them, and the list is what a Crown's clerk would have to be given to take the building, and there is one of those lists in this county and I am not having the only one in a press over a salt merchant."

"You are having it in a yard."

"I am putting it in a book that has a page in it every day and is kept by a man who is a creditor's man in his own trade and is owed by a trust that has nothing in it and cannot be got at. That book will outlast the paper, the room, the salt merchant, and me, and if a man comes up this street in ten years with an order in his hand and a date on the order, there will be a page in it that says what was in the list and what it was in, and it will have been in the list since the twenty-first of Greenmonth, YR 312, at the fourth hour, in my own hand, and you will not have to remember me at all. That is the entire argument and I have made it four times in four months to four different people and I have never once made it in a better room."

Tovan Kest put the envelope on the weight and did not touch it for about four seconds.

"Read me the eleventh item," he said, "and then I will put it in at the foot of the list on the eighth page with the hour beside it, and it will be the eleventh item and not a leaf, and it will be in a yard's book and not a claim's, and you will get no copy back because you have just told me a copy is the wrong thing."

"It is a dressed fragment of a bridge face out of Stonebarrow," said Aren Kest, "brought in a bag by a woman who would not give her name, entered at the ninth hour of the sixteenth by a keeper of records, and refused as a claim and kept as a stone. And it is the only item in that list that is a person, and it is in the list because a woman walked a hundred miles with it and asked for a number, and the number is on it and the number is the reason she is not on it."

"That goes in at the eighth page," said Tovan Kest, and wrote it, and ruled a line above it, because a line without a rule is a thing somebody put there on purpose, and he had learned that off a board on a market morning and had been furious about it for a month and had put a rule over his own line without saying anything to anybody.

Then he looked up.

"Brother. You have been in Weir Street nine days and you have not put a handrail in this yard and you have not put a handrail on the stair to your own room, and I have been up and down that stair eleven times a day since the nineteenth with a lamp and a free hand, and I know the trade of it, and it is a good rail and it is a cheap rail and it is oak and it is draw-bored and it is in a cooper's bin, which means it came out of a cooper's bin and not out of a yard that pays men at eight pence a day."

"I did not want it paid for."

"I did not offer," said Tovan Kest.

---

The stonecutter's woman was on the stair on the sixteenth and on the nineteenth and had not been on the twentieth, and Mara Vey had sent her down a note on the sixteenth that said the copy would be ready on the eighth of Greenmonth, and there had not been a copy on the eighth, and there was not going to be one, and the reason there was not going to be one was four shillings and a form in a provincial town and a claim in the matter of Alder Vale that the keeper of this office could not put in a hand with the thing she wanted certified without writing the word Hushwater into a building two hundred miles off.

She drafted the letter at the seventh hour of the twenty-first, and it was four lines long, and it said that the copy was not made, and why, and that the reason was a fee and a form, and that the fee was four shillings of which this office had none, and that the form asked a question this office was not going to answer for her. And then she read it back and put her own name to it, which is not a thing a keeper does in a letter to a claimant, and which she did because the promise had been hers and the not-making of it was hers and a woman in this building who did not put her own name to the ones that went badly would put it to the ones that went well inside a year and then this office would be a place where the failures were anonymous.

---

They had it on the bench at the eighth hour of the twenty-first and she read it out in the outer room to a man from Hushwater who came up two flights of stair in a coat he had bought new for the occasion, and she gave him the second column of the petition and the date on it and the hour, and she did not apologise, and he read it twice and gave it back and stood a while with his hand on the counter.

"You told me in Mudmonth that my name was not going anywhere."

"Your name is not going anywhere," said Mara Vey. "It is going to a filing room. There is a difference and it is not in your favour and it is not in mine, and I have been in this room four years and I have learned one thing and it is the only thing I am good at: **a thing that is in a room is a thing a clerk has seen, and a thing that is in a ledger is a thing a court has to answer.**" She put her hand flat on the counter, which she had never done in front of a claimant. "There is a returnable order in this office with a date on the ninth of Longlight, and the date is the ninth of Longlight, and on the ninth of Longlight I am going to have to state, in writing, every copy in this county and every person holding one. And your name is in a column of a petition that says *a man of Hushwater, twice, in his own name*, and when that order is answered I am going to have to answer that column too, and I am not going to stand in a room and lie about a man in a petition.

"So there is a thing I can do for you and it is the only thing, and it is the thing I have done four times in four years and it has never once worked, and I am going to do it anyway. A name can only be looked for. A number can only be looked for. **Your crossing book cannot be copied**, and I said that to you in Mudmonth and I meant it, and I am not going to ask you for it, and I am not going to ask you for a list of ferrymen, and I am not going to ask you for anything at all except that when a clerk in a provincial court sends me a form with a column on it for names, I am going to put your claim number in it, and a number is a thing that can be looked for, and a name is a thing that can be used, and the difference between the two is the difference between this office and a Crown's."

He looked at the paper in his hand for a while.

"You are telling me the Crown is going to ask who I am."

"I am telling you the Crown asked on the eighteenth and I got the order this morning and the date is the ninth of Longlight, and I am telling you that on the ninth of Longlight I will know less about you than I know tonight, and that I am spending fourpence of a poor office's money this afternoon to make sure that a man in a filing room is careful for the rest of his life, and that if you think that is me being kind to you then you have not been in this room long enough."

"Right," said Jos Rusk. "Then here is what I am going to do, and I am telling you in advance so that you may write it down and be wrong about it in a year.

"I am not going to stop coming. I am fifty-one years old and I have a bag with a crossing book in it in a room over a chandler and I am going to be at this counter in Fallowmonth and in Embermonth and in Wolfmonth until somebody tells me in writing that there is nothing here to come to, and if I am told that in writing I will bring the book and read it out in the room and let them look at it, and after that I will go and be an old man with a book, and not before.

"And you did not apologise, and I want that read back to me some day when it has been convenient for you, because I have been in eleven rooms in this county in eleven weeks and every one of them had a woman or a man in it who apologised to me inside a quarter of an hour, and I have walked out of all of them, and this is the first one I have walked out of where the apology was *withheld* and said so.

"That is what I have got, and it is not a lot, and it is the whole of what I came up the stair for." He put the paper down on the counter, squared it with two fingers the way a man squares a thing he is going to come back to. "And I have not sent anybody to the five ferrymen, and I have thought about it in this room, and I am not going to do it this winter, and when I do it I am going to write to you first and I am going to tell you the date, and you are going to write the date on a thing, and that thing is going to be the only paper in this county with my word and your hand on it, and I am aware that a paper cannot stop what I am about to do, and I am aware that is the argument for it."
