# Chapter 0045

The twenty-eighth of Longlight was the third day of his being in that city and the river had not come up two inches, and the bench went down onto the second pier at the fifth hour in the morning with a sounding pole, a rope, a chalk and a boy with a book, and Aren Kest went down with them because he had told them in a hall on the twenty-sixth to put a date on it and a man on it and go back to the water in the spring, and a man who has told a bench to do a thing and then does not watch him do it is a man who has told a bench to do a thing for his own comfort.

He was in the water for a quarter of an hour.

"Four feet six," he said. "It was four feet on the twenty-sixth and it is four feet six, and the north lip has gone back a hand's breadth, and there is gravel in the water this morning that was in the bed of it on the twenty-sixth, and the bed under the footing is not a shelf any more in two places. It is a shelf with two notches in it the width of a knee."

"How much stone is that?" said the man with the mallet.

"That is the wrong question and you asked it in a good faith and I am going to answer it anyway." He wiped his face. "It is between four and five tons of stone that is not there any more, and it is in the river two hundred yards downstream in four places where it will make a bar in a freshet, and the bar will move, and the bar is going to be somebody's problem in about four years and it will not be this bench's and it will be a town's."

The boy with the book wrote it down. The foreman of the fourth camp wrote it down. The man with the mallet did not write anything down and stood looking at the water for about a minute and a half.

"You are the best man in this room," he said.

"I have been in the water an hour in nineteen days," said Aren Kest. "That is not being the best man in the room. That is being the only man in the room who has ever put a pole against a scour hole, and I will tell you the difference, and the difference is that I have read forty of them and you have built forty of them, and a man who has read forty of them knows what forty of them look like and a man who has built forty of them knows what forty of them cost. You know more about this than I do. You know it in the only currency that matters, which is what a thing costs to the people who have to live beside it afterwards."

"And you know what a hole of that size means."

"I know that a hole of that size means you have nine months of work and no money and a debt that was let go eleven years ago." He got out of the water and stood on the staging in his clothes. "And I know that I cannot mend it. I want that said in a room as well, and I want it said before anybody offers me anything, because in a quarter of an hour somebody in this city is going to offer me something and I am going to say no and I would like it on the page that the no came first."

---

They offered him a stool in the hall on the morning of the twenty-ninth, and it was a good offer, and it was made in the right way, and he said no in eleven seconds and then spent two hours being sorry about the eleven seconds, which is the correct order of operations for a man like him and which he wrote down that night as a line in a notebook that is not a record.

It was the man with the mallet who offered it, in front of the bench, with the clerk there and the clock on the wall, and it was a bench seat and a wage out of the guild's own box and no rank at all, and it came with the fourth camp.

"And that is not a politeness," the man with the mallet said. "That is a repair. This bench has nine men and one of us is fifty-eight and one of us cannot lift a sett above his shoulder in a heat, and we have a hundred and forty paces of north-bank approach to lay before the frost and a fifth bay to start, and if you cut setts on the fourth camp for four months this bench will finish the approach in one season instead of two, and that is worth about eleven marks of stone and four months of wages, and I have no eleven marks."

"I will cut setts."

"You will not," said the man with the mallet, "and I am going to tell you why, and then you can go away and be offended about it in whatever language you brought with you, which I have not asked about and am not going to.

"A foreign man who cuts setts on the fourth camp is a foreign man on the fourth camp. In four months every man on that floor will have an opinion about you and so will every woman, and there will be a setts-cutter in this city in nine years who says *my father laid setts with the north one* and there will be another in nine years who says *the north one was here and the camp went cold the winter he was here*, and I do not know which of those is true and neither will they, and both of them will be in a market, and this bench will have to answer for it every time.

"And there is a worse one and I will say it because you have said harder things about my ring this month than four men in this hall have said in nineteen years. If you lay our setts and our approach and our fifth bay, then in a year when a stranger from the north comes down that road and asks this bench who built the northern approach, there will be nine men in this room who can point at a man instead of at a book, and a bench is a thing that is pointed at and not a thing that is pointed with. We have spent eleven years being a thing that is pointed at. Do not make us into something else in four months for eleven marks of stone."

"That is the best reason anybody has given me for anything in this book," said Aren Kest, "and I have been given reasons by a marshal."

"I am aware of that and I have thought about it and I have decided that you are the man who is going to say that about this bench, and that if the bench lets that go without answering it then the bench has decided it was a different bench, and this one has not. Take the setts. We will do it slower."

---

Alis Merrow came and found him at the second hour of the twenty-ninth with the bundle of string, and the thing he wanted had changed, and he said so himself inside a minute, which is the single most disarming thing about him.

"You have heard me wrong twice," he said. "In Greenmonth, when I put a bridge in your coat and asked you for the north road. And in the counting house, when I asked you a question and you gave me a motive and the clerk had to stop you. Both times I asked you what a man could give me and you gave me something else, and both times the something else was better and I have used it, and I want that entered before I ask for the third thing, because I am a creditor and I do not like a ledger with a man's name in it that does not also have the day on it.

"Here is the third thing. I am sixty-one. I have four hundred leaves behind me and a grandson who is nine and whose name is on the front of the book and who will keep it after me, and in three weeks I have to go and tell him and his mother what the arch is worth.

"**And I cannot, because a line in this book released it eleven years ago.**"

He put the bundle of string on the parapet and undid it, and inside it were three leaves in a fair hand, and he turned them so that the stranger from the north could read them, which was against every rule in the room and which he did deliberately.

"The writ of severance released the tolls of the arch to the office of the marshal for the term of the severance. The severance was refused. The writ was withdrawn. A writ that is withdrawn releases what it tendered. So the tolls on this arch were let go on the twenty-second of Mudmonth, YR 301, and the pledge to the ninth of a grandson was made in YR 288 against a thing that stopped being ours in YR 301, and every house that put a mark against this arch in the fifteen years after that was lending against nothing.

"I have known that for eleven years and I have never once said it in a hall, and I said it to nine men in this hall in YR 312 at the ninth hour, and the man with the mallet worked out the rest of it standing up, and he is right, and nobody has done anything about it since, because a thing that is bad for a creditor is very bad for a city and a city does not go and look for it twice.

"So that is the position, and now there is a hole in the bed of the river four feet across, and the fix is nine months, and I am going to stand up in that hall tomorrow, at the tenth hour, and ask nine men to say in front of two hundred people whether they hold this arch, and I am going to ask it in the old words, because the old words are the ones my grandfather signed and the ones my grandson will read.

"And I will tell you what I want, and it is not the north road, and it is not a page, and it is not your name.

"I want the oath renewed while there is still a city here to renew it to."

---

The oath is four lines long and it is older than the guild and it is kept in a box in the counting house and it is read out in a hall about once in twenty years.

It went like this, and Alis Merrow read it himself, standing, in a good coat, with two hundred people at the back of a hall over an arch:

*That the crossing at Alder Vale shall be held; that the arch shall bear; that the tolls, rents and dues of the crossing shall be taken and kept to the uses of the crossing; and that the stewards of the crossing shall answer for it, and answer first.*

Four lines. No figures in it anywhere. No date on it. The word *held* in the first line and *answer* in the fourth, and a hundred and forty years of benches, and a city that had watched people keep promises for four generations and could count them.

The bench-clerk read the minute of YR 312, the ninth of Greenmonth, aloud afterwards, and then read the line about the tolls, and then the man with the mallet said the thing that the whole of the year turns on.

"Say what the bench is being asked to promise in plain words," he said, "because there is a man in this hall who does not have your language and there are two hundred people in this room who are not going to follow four lines of it, and I will not have this city find out what it swore to in a market in six weeks."

The bench-clerk read it back in tradesman's words, which he had clearly thought about, because he had been a clerk of this bench for nineteen years and he is the man who always knows what the room is about to misunderstand.

"That the crossing will be held," he said. "That is the whole of it in one sentence, and the other three lines are the reason the first line is not a lie. The arch will bear. The money of the crossing is the crossing's own. And the stewards of the crossing answer for it, and answer first — meaning that when something goes wrong with this crossing, the first men asked are the men who keep it, and not a bank and not an abbey and not a man four hundred miles away with a letter.

"That is the oath. It is not a promise about a stone. It has never been a promise about a stone. It is a promise about who answers, and this city has understood that for four generations and has had no occasion to test it, because nothing has gone wrong with this crossing since the year my father was born."

Nobody spoke for a length of a slow count of five.

And then Alis Merrow asked the question he had come to ask, in the old words, and the bench sat there.

And Aren Kest, who had come up the north steps of that city on a Wednesday morning in the middle of the year with a bar on his shoulder and a date in a book, stood up at the back of a hall over an arch and said nothing whatever, in a public room, in front of two hundred people, because he had worked out in about nine seconds what it would cost him to open his mouth, and he had opened it four times in nineteen days and each of them had been worth about a hundred marks, and this one would have been worth a war.

It is the single hardest thing in this book and it took him nine seconds and he never told anybody about it, and the only place it is written down is four lines in a notebook in a coat, on the eighteenth day:

*Twenty-ninth of Longlight. Asked a question in a hall and stood up and said nothing. There were two hundred people and a creditor and nine men and a clerk and I had the answer in my hand, and the answer is that the oath is a promise about who answers and not a promise about a stone, and if I say that in that room then a stranger from the north has taught a city its own oath, and in six weeks there will be a market in this city saying that the oath was a foreigner's, and in a year there will be a man here who believes the guild of this arch is a thing that was taught to it.*

*I have been the best man in the room four times in nineteen days. This is the fifth time and it is the first time the best thing I could say is nothing, and I would like somebody to write down that I worked it out in nine seconds and did not enjoy any part of it and did it anyway.*

---

The bench sat until the eleventh hour and the answer was given at the tenth, and the answer was yes, and it was given in the old words, and the bench-clerk read it back four times and the fifth time he read it with the hour in it.

*That the crossing at Alder Vale is held by this bench; and that the oath of the crossing is renewed by this bench on the twenty-ninth of Longlight, YR 312, at the tenth hour, in this hall, before two hundred persons; and that the bench records that it makes this promise knowing the state of the second pier as entered this day at the ninth hour in the fair hand of its clerk; and that the bench has not been able to name a date by which the promise will be discharged and is asked by no person to name one.*

The last clause is the man with the mallet's and it is the only clause anybody in that room argued for, and it is in the minute, and it took nine minutes to get in and it nearly did not.

Then the bench broke up and the hall emptied the way a hall empties when two hundred people have heard four lines that they are going to argue about for a year, and the bench-clerk did not rule lines for the next entry. He went and got a piece of chalk.

He chalked the door of the guild hall, and then he took a sheet and ruled it and wrote it out in a clerk's hand, and had it taken to the counting house door and to the gate and to the north steps, and the notice was four lines and a date and it said:

*On the twenty-ninth of Longlight, YR 312, at the tenth hour, the bench of the guild of this city renewed the oath of the crossing of Alder Vale in a hall, before two hundred persons. The state of the second pier is entered in the marking book and in the minute of that hour. The oath is the four lines of it and they are at the counting house and anybody may read them.*

There was no argument about whether to put it up. There was an argument about it for about four minutes, and the argument was not about the four lines; it was about one of them, and the man who lost it lost it cleanly, and the clerk entered the objection in the minute because a clerk enters objections.

Then, at about the ninth hour of the thirtieth, while the hall was still standing open and the floor was still swept in lines where a hundred and forty people had stood on it, the bench-clerk sat down at the table with the stone in the floor and asked the man with the mallet a question, and asked it in front of the toll-house steward and the ferrywright and the sett-cutter and the man with the mallet, and asked it because there were five of them in the room and a bench is a thing that asks things in rooms with people in them.

"The oath says the stewards of the crossing shall answer for it and shall answer first," he said. "And who is the steward of this crossing?"

The man with the mallet said: "There has not been one since the flood of the second year. The last was a woman named Ock and she is a boat on the north bank. The bench keeps it. The bench has always kept it. That is what a bench is."

"That is what a bench has always been," said the bench-clerk, "and I have written it in this minute, and I would like the minute to carry the question as well and not only the answer, because the question was asked in a room with five men in it and the answer is a hundred and forty years old and nobody in this room has ever tested it.

"Because we have just renewed an oath," he said, "in a hall, in front of two hundred people, that the stewards of this crossing shall answer for it and shall answer first, and there is a hole in the bed of the river under the second pier, and the oath was renewed with that entered in the minute, and the bench is the steward, and the bench is the creditor's opposite, and the creditor is the man who has just told us our tolls were let go eleven years ago and has offered to put it in a hall and has been told no because the tolls are not ours to give away because they were let go.

"So the crown's office that wrote the writ holds them, and the writ was withdrawn, and a withdrawn writ releases what it tendered, and so nobody holds them, and the bench has just sworn in front of two hundred people that it will answer first for a crossing whose tolls are held by nobody, and that is either the most honourable thing this bench has ever done or it is a piece of language, and in a year I am going to have to know which, and I am not going to be able to tell from a book." He squared the minute up. "Enter the question."
