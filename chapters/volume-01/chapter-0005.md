# Chapter 0005

What he had expected was a gap.

What he got, at the sixth hour of a morning in Mudmonth, was a set of stone steps going up out of a river town with a smell of hot glass and tar and wet wool on them, and a girl of about fourteen sitting on the third step with a basket of onions between her boots, and, behind her, four hundred feet of stone arch with a washing line strung across the middle of it.

Aren Kest stood on the bottom step with the water still running off him and looked up the steps and thought, with a clarity that would take him years to unlearn: *wrong place.*

He had crossed in the wrong place, in the wrong weather, with no papers and no money and a bar in his hand, and there was nobody to ask about it, and the first thing the world had for him was a question.

"You're up the north steps," said the man behind him.

He turned. A heavy man in a leather apron with a ledger on a board and a string of wooden tokens on his belt, standing at the head of a landing stage at the foot of the steps.

"I am."

"There's no crossing behind you." The man said it with real enjoyment. "The toll's a farthing on the north side for anything that crosses, and the toll's a farthing for anything that comes up the steps, because a man who comes up the steps has crossed and is lying about it, and a man who hasn't crossed and comes up the steps is a fool, and I do not charge fools twice, but I do charge them once, and there it is."

"I haven't got a farthing."

"You haven't got anything. You've got a bar."

"I've got a bar," Aren agreed, "and no money, and I haven't crossed anything, and you're right that I came up the steps." He turned the bar over in his hand. Iron, an inch and a half square at the business end, four feet long, worn to a polish at the grip. In Merefen it had been a bar for shifting a jammed sill stone. Here it was a bar. "Where am I?"

"Bellweather. North bank, above the fish steps, and if that's a question you don't know the answer to, you've come a long way to ask it."

"Yes," Aren said. "I have."

The apron man wrote a half mark in his ledger without looking at it, which is what a man does with a thing that happens eleven times a day.

"Go on, then. The gate's up at the top. Mind the second step, it's been loose since the thaw, and if you say a word about that to anybody I'll have you off the bridge."

He went up the steps. The girl with the onions was watching him and had clearly been watching him for some time.

"What are you here for?" she said.

"I'm a bridgewright."

"Are you," said the girl, with enormous interest. "Are you for the gate bridge? Because the gate bridge's sinking on the town side and the warden's had a man out there shouting at it for a month and my mam says the warden's man is the second-worst man in Bellweather, and the first is the one who doesn't know he's the second."

"What's your mam's trade?"

"Boats," said the girl. "And before you ask, no, we haven't got a bridge. We have eleven of them and they're all being looked at by somebody with an opinion."

He came out onto the deck at the top of the steps and the city went away from him.

It was not a view. It was a working morning, six parts in a hundred, and the bridge was a piece of it. Carts were already on the far half, coming north with the millers' carts and the salt, and the deck was cambered the way a well-made deck is cambered, high in the middle, so that the wheels ran uphill to the crown and downhill off it, and the camber had been laid by somebody who understood that a road is a promise about how tired a horse will be at the far end.

The span was stone, five arches, and the middle one was the great one, and it was not a bridge a man could hold in his head. Four hundred feet from bank to bank, and the arch ring in courses of voussoirs each one deeper than his arm, and the piers down in the river with cutwaters like the bows of ships standing out into the current, and above the arches the spandrels, and a string course of grey stone along the parapet, and under the parapet a course of dressed stone that had been put there by somebody who wanted it to be the last thing anybody saw before they drowned.

He stood on the crown of it and looked for the scar and could not find one, and then he found five, and then he found nine, and he understood that the structure was telling him everything and that he could only read a fraction of it.

Because the gate bridge was on the north bank, eight hundred feet off and up the river where the raised road came in, and it was timber, four bays on four timber bays exactly like the Sedge Reach, and it was sinking on the town side.

It gave him the arch. That was the whole of it. He had spent eleven years learning to read a four-bay timber deck, and what he had never had to do was scale it up, and now he stood on the crown of a thing that was the same argument as his own bridge at four hundred times the size, and the geometry came apart in his head and went back together and told him things.

He could see where the load went. Down the arch, into the piers, into the cutwaters, and out — and the second pier from the north was not doing that. There was a way the water could get under the third course, in the season for it, and it had been getting under it for years, and the stone had been repointed twice over the pointing rather than once under it, and he could see the newer mortar from where he stood because it was a different grey.

He looked for the third pier. It was there, under the arch, wet to the springing of the cutwater, and there was a hole under it, and the water was going through the hole at a rate that made a standing bow-wave a foot high.

Four inches, he thought. Four inches of stone between that and the room upstairs.

Something behind him was shouting. He turned round.

It was a woman on the second-storey gallery of a house that came straight out of the bridgehead, standing at a winch with a line, hauling a washing line across the span. There were sheets on it. There were eight sheets and a blanket and a man's coat, and the line was going over the crown of the arch at a height of ninety feet above the river, and it was going across the main span at a point where the parapet was thirty feet out from the arch ring, and it was going to be attached to a derrick on the far bank, and the derrick was nine hundred years old or four hundred, and the line was one inch of laid rope.

"You'll want to get off the crown," the woman said, without unrolling the line. "She goes over at eight and she has been over every day since Thawmonth and the warden's never once noticed, and I don't want him noticing with you standing there."

"I'd rather she didn't go over."

"You'd rather a great many things." She hauled, and the winch took up, and the line came tight across the arch, and the eight sheets went out over the water in a long white line in the flat light of the sixth hour. "You're a stranger, so you'll be a fool about bridges, but you'll have seen the line. Good. Then you'll be able to say to the bench, when they ask you, that a man on a bridge in Bellweather told you the washing goes over the middle arch and has gone every day since Thawmonth, which is the sort of thing this city would rather be written down before it stops being true."

"I never said I'd speak to a bench."

"You never said anything," said the woman. "That's the same thing, up here. Everybody who arrives in Bellweather says something. Half of them say they came to help and the other half say they came to look, and both halves end up in front of the bench, and the bench has met every kind there is for four generations and the answer is always the same, which is that the arch is mortgaged to the ninth of my grandson and we are all going to be here when it comes due."

The light on the deck had gone from grey to a thin yellow. Downriver, the morning's traffic had thickened, and there was a mill boat turning out from under the arch with a full sail of brown canvas, and the sound of the oars was a man counting.

Below the north parapet, at the foot of the steps, somebody was talking in an argument that had been going for a while.

"—eleven fingers at the slip and rising, and that's the water your boat's got to swim in, and I've been telling you since the thaw—"

"And the board says nine and a quarter."

"The board was painted on the eleventh!"

"The board is the record. The board doesn't lie because the river had a bad week."

"The board is a stick with numbers on it nailed to a post by a man who doesn't pole a boat! It reads what the river did last time somebody wrote on it, which is the one thing a river promises to do and doesn't!"

"It's the only figure anybody can put in a book, and I have to put a figure in a book, and I will not put a figure in a book that I have got off a boatman holding his finger up over a mud bank—"

Aren Kest stood ninety feet above both of them with his hand on the parapet and felt the whole argument go through him like a current.

It was the argument he had lost on Thursday at Sedge Reach — the same mud bank, the same painted post, the same two kinds of man, and him on the wrong side of it, telling a boatman he could not read water.

A man on the deck above him, holding a line, had heard it too.

"Fish-scale," the woman said. "That is the entire of their quarrel and it has been the entire of it since my father's time. The table tells you what the river has done. The fingers tell you what your boat is going to find at the slip. Write it down like that and you'll understand why they still shout."

Aren took his hand off the parapet, and took out the notebook, and wrote it out, and put the book away without marking it, which is a thing a man does when he does not yet believe he has been told anything.

He had been wrong at home on Thursday. He would go on being wrong at home for a long time, and it would cost somebody something, and he had no idea yet who.

He was still standing there when the aproned toll man came up the steps behind him and said, in the voice of a man closing a matter, "You didn't come across."

"I came the whole length of it and back on the same stones," Aren said. "Which you watched me do, and did not charge me for, and are not going to tell anyone about."

"I haven't decided yet."

"Decide quickly," Aren said, "because that woman on the gallery is going to take your bridge down with a washing line at eight o'clock and I'd rather be fifty feet elsewhere."

The toll man opened his ledger, made a mark in it with a thumbnail, and closed it. "You'll be the warden's business, then. I keep the money and he keeps the trouble." He looked at Aren with a sudden, unaccountable interest. "Where did you come off? Because there's a gate, and a bridge, and a road out, and a man who can't say where he came off is a man somebody's family will want."

"Merefen," Aren said. "Three days' riding north, in a fen."

"I've heard of fens." The man wrote something down, and then stopped, and looked at the bar in Aren's hand, and then at his own ledger, and then back at him. "You said Merefen."

"I did."

"That's not a Crown name."

"No."

"Well." The toll man closed the ledger, and did not write it down, and Aren noticed that he had not written it down, and understood in the way of a man who has been reading people for thirty years that a man who decides not to write something down has been told something about it recently. "Then you'll be for the bench, and you'll not be the first of your kind to want the bench, and I'll tell you the one thing worth telling a stranger who can't pay a farthing: they don't want a farthing. They want an answer to a question they'll put to you twice."

He went back down the steps.

Aren stood on the bridge in Bellweather at the seventh hour of a morning, in a city of four thousand people, none of whom was the four thousand the Crown had, and none of whom knew that yet, and the arch was under him and the laundry was going over it and the bell that was not cracked was going somewhere else across a hundred roofs.

He looked at his left hand. In the grey light of the north bank, the pale seam he had carried since he was a child was a black line, healed into the palm along the crease where a join would run, and it was not a cut, because it did not sting when he made a fist, and it did not wash off, and when he turned his hand over and looked at it the way a man looks at a new mark that he has decided to explain, he saw that it ran under the skin and did not stop at the edge of the skin, and it was the shape of a road that had been folded rather than drawn.

He had no explanation. He had a habit of writing things down, so he wrote it down: *Roadmark, left palm, from the bell. Black. Not a cut. Does not hurt. Runs under the skin.*

Then he said the thing he had come to say, out loud, on a bridge in a city that the Crown had erased, because it was in him and it had to go into the air before anything else did.

"My name is Aren Kest. I came from the north. There is a bell with your founder's mark on it and a crack through its mouth and it hangs on a bridge in a town called Merefen, and I know that no Crown finding in the north says this city stands, and I know what my own country says happened to it on the nineteenth of Mudmonth, eleven years ago."

The deck went quiet in the way a crowd goes quiet, and the sheets went on going over the arch because nothing stops a washing line for a sentence.

A man on the near bank, older, with a ferryman's cord round his neck, said: "Bellweather's on the board. The board's got it. You've come to the wrong bridge, friend."

"Bellweather is this city."

"Aye. And?"

"And I am from the north, and the north has a finding that says the fever took it, and says a captain lost a bridge, and says four thousand dead, and my own name is in that finding." He put his hand out, flat, and showed the black line, and heard how much better it sounded as a piece of evidence than it was. "I am not here to fetch anybody. Nobody is owed to me. Nobody in this city owes me a thing and I have not come to claim one. If the north has sent a man at last, you will hear of it from the tower and not from a stranger on a bridge, and it will be a different man with a different errand and I will not be in his way."

He stopped there, because the aproned man and the ferryman and the woman on the gallery were all looking at him, and because the thing he wanted to say next was the thing he wanted, and he heard himself wanting it.

*Tell me what happened. Tell me what you remember of the nineteenth. You are the only people on earth who were there and you do not know that you are the only people on earth who were there, and I need to know it, and I need to know it before I go, and I have a right to know it, because I was sent to hold a bridge—*

He shut his mouth on it.

That was the moment the elapse turned, and it turned in a street with the onions going stale in a basket on a step.

He had come a long way to be given a story. He was standing on a bridge listening to a mill wheel and a foundry hammer and a woman winching a washing line across an arch, and the first thing he wanted from these people was their grief, and the second thing he wanted was their version, and he had already begun arranging his face to be the face of a man who has come a long way and would like to help, which is his father's face, which is a dead man's face, which is the face that had gone into a room in Embermonth eleven years ago and said the word *fever*.

The shame arrived before the understanding did, as it usually did.

"Is the fever back?" said the ferryman on the bank.

"No."

"How would you know?" said the woman on the gallery, not unrolling the line. "You've been in the city a quarter of an hour."

"Then it isn't," Aren said.

"It came up out of the low quarter," said the ferryman, "in Mudmonth, the year before last, and the year before that, and there's a woman on Ferry Lane who keeps her shutters shut and a man at the tanner's who hasn't been out in daylight in three years. That's the fever. That's all the fever there has ever been in this city, and we buried two hundred and eleven of it ourselves and we have the ledger and we do not need the north to tell us what it was."

Two hundred and eleven. The number was in his chest like a swallowed stone, because he had four thousand in his head and he had never once been told what the other figure was, and now a ferryman on a north bank had handed it to him without knowing that he was carrying anything at all.

"I am sorry," Aren said. "Then it isn't back. I know nothing about fevers and I know less about you, and I have said the wrong thing twice."

"You've said about four of the right things," said the gallery woman. "The wrong one was your hand. Nobody here has ever wanted to see a stranger's hand."

At that moment, at the bridgehead behind him, somebody said his name.

It was not his name. It was *the man from the steps*, and then it was *the stranger*, and then a voice with a cold, unhurried, working voice cut through the whole thing from a gate in the north parapet and said: "He is a bridgewright, and he says he reads timber, and there is a hull on the blocks at the third slip with a split garboard and a full crew standing about it, and the bench would like him to come and be useful before the tide."

"Who's asking?" Aren said.

"The bench," said the woman, "and I am not the bench. Go and be useful. Come back at the ninth hour and tell us the north's news, and be warned that whatever you say will be written down by somebody who was not in the room, and that we will ask you the same question twice."

The apron man was already holding the little ladder up to the gate.

Aren Kest went down off the crown of the great arch of Bellweather, past the washing line and the loose second step, with a bar in his hand and a roadmark on his palm and a lie he had not yet finished telling still sitting in him, and the ferryman on the bank did not ask him for a toll.
