# Chapter 0038

It came on the county's post at the fourth hour of the eighteenth of Longlight and it was not sealed as an order.

That was the first thing he noticed and it was the thing he went on thinking about for four days. It came to the room over the cooper on Weir Street, in a man's hand on the outside, in a county's brown paper, and the county's brown paper was the paper the county used for its own post and its own notices and its own tax lists, and the county's brown paper had a postmark on it in the county's own hand at the county town, and a Crown's office two hundred miles away does not use a county's brown paper. A Crown's office uses a paper with the office's name on the back of it, and this had the county's name, and it was addressed to him by name, and the sender's name was written at the foot of the address in the same hand as the address.

And there was a seal on it. A letter does not need a seal. A letter is a thing between two men and a seal on a letter says that the man who sealed it wants it known to whoever opens the envelope first, and the seal was grey wax the size of a crown piece and the device on it was a hand laid flat on a book.

He stood in the room with it in his hand for about a minute and a half, and then he broke it, and read it, and it was eleven hundred words.

---

He read it four times, standing, and then he sat down, and then he read it twice more sitting down, and then he went and got the notebook out of the coat and the room had no fire in it because there was never any fire in the room, and it was a Tuesday in Longlight and there were four hours of daylight left in it and he used all of them.

He did not write anything down while he read it. That is the part he could not get past afterwards: eleven hundred words in a fair strong hand with no blot in it, and he read it seven times over two days and did not write one word down, because there was nothing in it that was not already the shape of the last eleven weeks, and a man who copies a thing he has already got is a man making a second copy of it, and he had promised a creditor in a city four days off not to make a second copy of anything.

The letter began:

*I am going to set out what is known, in the order in which it was done, with the dates, because the dates are the part that has always been the difficulty and I have never once been able to get a date out of a man who was in the room.*

*On the first of Mudmonth, YR 312, a bell of nine stone was bought at a clearance for two pence and entered as unclassified salvage, and on the eleventh the bench of the Merefen bridge guild issued a notice of removal, dated the morning of the day it reached him, and on the same day he signed a bond for six marks with his tool-chest as surety and named the surety before the clerk had finished asking him.*

*On the sixteenth the office sealed an instrument in the matter of an object of that description. On the evening of the eighteenth it was delivered to an officer of this county and he was told to keep it shut. On the morning of the nineteenth, at first light, the object was lifted from a bridge in Merefen by four men and a cart, and the bed of the stone was swept afterwards, and a boy did the sweeping, and the boy was sixteen.*

*Aren Kest.*

*He had been in a fever three days from the twenty-seventh and the fever broke on the thirtieth. He had come back into this world out of a stone he had cut himself, at a quarter to seven in the morning, and there is a book kept by the watch of that town in which it was entered, and I have it.*

*On the twentieth he went into a filing room in that town and put into a file a sworn account in his own hand, and with it an exhibit he had brought out of another world, and the exhibit was filed with a date and a chain of custody and a number, and his own account was filed beneath it so that the seal and the date would be read before the man. That was not his doing and I have thought about that for two days and I have arrived at the conclusion that it was very much his doing.*

*On the second of Greenmonth a county renewed a finding that nothing survives, and on the fourth a magistrate in that county found the seizure lawful and asked this office to produce its authority, and it did not, and she entered the asking and the not being answered, and that is the only document in this business that has ever frightened anybody.*

*On the ninth of Greenmonth he was four days off in another world, and the word in the ledger in this town is what it is.*

*On the sixteenth of Greenmonth he was in a city of four days off, and a creditor's register in that city was opened to him, and four conditions were set, and the third of them was that the fact leaves and the line does not, and the fourth was that he would not say a motive, and he said one anyway and was stopped, and a clerk in that city entered the arrest against him and not against the man who signed the line, and I have that entry and I have read it four times and I would like you to know that a frightened clerk writes beautifully.*

*On the eighteenth day of his being in that city he stood in a hall of nine men and said out loud, for the first time, that he had been on the deck of the arch on the night of the nineteenth of Mudmonth in the three hundred and first year, at the second hour, in a Crown's cloak, with two hundred people on it who had not been ordered there. He put a field book on the bench's table. The bench-clerk entered the hour and the fact and the words and nothing else, and entered that the said Kest is a witness against himself in this matter and against the account his own office has kept since the thirtieth of Mudmonth, and that the bench has not been asked to believe him and has not been asked to forgive him, and that it is entered because the bench does not have a column for the other things and has refused to invent one.*

*On the eighth of Longlight the office received from a court a return in which a particular had been written describing his exhibit in terms which name this office. The particular was written on the ninth of Greenmonth by a clerk who was doing his duty. On the ninth of Longlight a writ of enquiry into the custody of the records of that town was issued, and I signed it, and I am telling you that I signed it because the particulars are good and the office is a genuine question and I would rather answer it in a file than in a newspaper.*

And then the letter did the thing that killed eleven years, and it did it in four sentences, in the middle of a page, in the register of a record.

*There is a version of this that is easier for both of us and I am going to set it out, and I am going to set it out because you taught it to me and I have never said so.*

*At twenty-one years of age you were the best man in the room. That is not a compliment; it is a load-bearing fact. I chose you because you were the best man in the room and because you had no following and because your own advancement made it easy. I have never in eleven years put a figure on what that cost a valley, and a figure is what the room wants, and the room has had a figure, and the figure was a boy of twenty-one who lost a bridge. **The boy was the mechanism. The boy was excellent at it.** And every account since has been slightly wrong in the boy's favour, including his own, and I have let it stand for eleven years because a correct figure would have been a speech and a speech is a thing a man can be got to change.*

The letter went on for two hundred more words about the volume, about a toll house's northern trade, about a fair copy in a counting house's press and a press-mark of press four, die eleven, and about a market in Merefen in which a sentence of his own had been chalked on a board and had gone into four hundred mouths, and then it came to the end, and the end was this.

It is a record. Every sentence in it has a subject and a verb and they are all third persons, and the whole of it is the voice of a file being read out in a room by a man reading a man's own record of himself, and it is competent, and it is correct, and it is not an argument, and not one word in it can be refused, because a refusal of a record is an argument and an argument can be answered.

And in the middle of the last paragraph, without a change of voice, without a change of size, and with the same steady hand:

**You will have no further contact with it.**

One sentence in eleven hundred words with a subject and a verb and a second person in it, and an imperative, and no court behind it, and no law named, and it did not require a court, because you do not need one to instruct a man not to touch a thing. You need one to make him touch it.

And then, in the ordinary courteous close of an old commander's letter to a young man he taught, which is the part that finished it:

*I do not think you will write to me. I want to say that I have thought about whether I ought to say it and I have decided that I ought, because you were honest to me for eleven years and I have given you a page of your own record and no argument whatever, and if you write, then you have read all of this and you are a man who can be trusted to have read it, and that is worth more to me than a rank or a shield for a filing room, and I shall be obliged for the list of those to whom you have spoken of this matter.*

---

He sat in the room above the cooper for two hours after that without lighting anything, because there was no fire and because the light had gone.

Then he did the thing that a man does when he has been handed eleven hundred words that he cannot argue with, which is that he went and looked for what was not in them.

And it took him about nine minutes, and it took him nine minutes because he is a man who reads a document as a structure and not as a complaint, and the structure of the letter was a list.

The letter had the dates. The letter had the hours. The letter had the bench of nine in a hall in a city four days off, and a creditor's register, and a press-mark, and a field book on a table, and a sworn account in a filing room in Merefen with a number against it, and a chain of custody, and a boy's age, and the bed of a stone swept at first light on the nineteenth of Mudmonth.

And there was not one other person's name in it.

Not a keeper. Not a clerk. Not a toll-holder. Not a brother. Not the man whose counting house had opened a four-hundred-leaf book and imposed four conditions and stopped a motive in a room. Not the frightened clerk who had entered an arrest against a stranger. Not the woman on Ferry Lane. Not the ferrywright. Not the magistrate. Eleven hundred words that accounted for every room he had been in, and every hand that had touched a paper, and every day of his life since the first of Mudmonth, and the people in it were *a man of Hushwater* and *a woman without a name* and *a road warden of Merefen* and *the petitioner* and *a keeper of records* and *the creditor's man* and nothing else, in the whole of it, not one of them.

He got the notebook out and read his own account of the last six weeks in it, and then he wrote, at the ninth hour of the eighteenth of Longlight, in his own hand, on a clean page:

*The letter has no names. It has everything else. A list of what a man did, in order, with the hour against it, and not one person in it who was not me.*

*And the only book in this county with that in it is an attendance book, and the only attendance book in this county is on a table two flights above a salt merchant, and somebody in a provincial office has read it, and the second column of a petition of the twenty-first of Greenmonth is a list of four descriptions and no names, and I have been in this town five months and I have never once been in that room and I know what a second column with no names in it looks like, because I read one over somebody's shoulder in Mudmonth and it was the same shape and it had three names in it and one of them was mine.*

*So they have got the dates and they have got the rooms and they have got the presses, and they have not got one name, and the reason they have not got a name is the reason this office exists, which is that a thing held in one room by one keeper is a favour and a thing entered in a book with a number is a thing a court has to answer, and the woman who worked that out before I did it is two flights above a salt merchant in this town and has a writ of enquiry against her office this month because of a paragraph in a marginal note.*

*And a courteous man at the end of it has asked me for the names.*

*Which means the only thing this office has left to give is the last thing, and they know that, and they have not taken it, and they have written me a letter with a seal on it to ask me for it in a hand I have known since I was seventeen years old.*

He read that back and then he sat with it for a long time, and the thing that came out of the sitting was the thing that had come out of every room since the sixth of Mudmonth, and it came out this time with nothing at all attached to it:

Edric Hunn had never lied to him.

Not once, in eleven years, in a briefing or a corridor or a mess or a parade, and not in the eleven hundred words, and that was the whole of the argument and there was no argument, and a man who has spent eleven years being the mechanism in a room and has just been told, by the man who used the room, that he was the mechanism and that it was his own excellence that did it, and has been told it *kindly*, and accurately, and without a single word that could be answered —

He put his face in his hands for about a minute, and then he took it out of his hands, because there was nothing to do with it.

---

He had four days and he used them badly and on purpose.

The first day he wrote nothing. The second day he wrote a draft of two thousand words and destroyed it, in the fireless room, by taking it to the window and tearing it into six pieces, and the six pieces went into the cooper's bin in the morning, and the cooper's bin is where the rail timber came from in Rainmonth, and he noticed that and thought it was a joke and it was not one.

The third day he wrote it in four paragraphs and it came to about five hundred words and it was the hardest thing he had written in eleven years and he has never said that out loud and did not then.

**The receipt.** *Received on the eighteenth of Longlight, at the fourth hour, sealed, with a device of a hand laid flat on a book, addressed to me by name. I am answering the seal and the date and the hour and nothing else, because those four things are the part of a letter that cannot be improved on.*

**The date.** *The middle of the year, at the lowest water, on the day that a bench in a city of four days off marks the second pier. I will be on that crossing or I will not, and no person in this country will be able to tell you which until I am there or am not, and that is deliberate. It is deliberate because a man who can be found can be watched, and a man who is watched does not go, and a man who does not go writes to a keeper in a filing room and the keeper writes it down. If you want to know where I am on that day, the way to know is to wait.*

**The fact.** *The object is held lawfully in this county under your office's seal and a magistrate of this county has found the seizure lawful in a public room on the fourth of Greenmonth, and the finding is in a county file two hundred miles from where I am writing this, and it asks your office for its authority and does not get it, and the asking is dated. I did not go and get it. I have said in a public file that I am not going to try for it and that I will not be the man who breaks a law or finds a Crown wrong to get a bell back, and I have put that in writing on the thirtieth of Mudmonth and in a room on Weir Street and in a hall in a city of four days off, and it is dated in three places, and I have got two marks and three quarters of a cutting contract and eleven days and a quarter of a wage advance at eight pence a day against it, and none of that is a complaint. It is what a man has got.*

**The condition.** *You have instructed that I shall have no further contact with it, and I am not going to answer that, and I am not going to break it, and I am going to tell you what it costs in this country, because you taught me to answer a question and put the load-bearing fact on the table whether it was asked for or not.*

*A bench of nine men in a fen town cannot meet about the oldest bridge in their county, and the reason is a word in a paper of yours that does not require a court. A toll-holder of that crossing has had his wharfage suspended by your letter of the twentieth of Greenmonth, and his winter half is eight marks and ten shillings, and he is sixty-one, and he has put a date and an hour and his own witness on the outside of your envelope in chalk, and he is the only man in this town who has ever come and asked me a question and been given a true one. A man with a bar and a notebook is now a thing that is to be watched in a county of this size, and there are nine men here who watch roads, and I do not know how many of them have been told.*

*And this, which you did not ask for and which is the only part of this letter I am certain of: a court in this county asked your office for its authority on the fourth of Greenmonth and was not answered, and the asking is dated, and any office that acts in this matter again is on notice of a dated question it has not answered. Your commendation of the eighth of Longlight is nailed to the outside of a watch house in this town with four nails and a seal, and it praises a watch for the execution of a lawful instruction in the matter of an object held in use among a company of persons, and that phrase is in your order of the sixteenth of Mudmonth, and it is a phrase about a bell that four hundred people walked over every fourth day, and somebody in a provincial office has read it and understood it, and I do not know who and I am not asking.*

*And the names. I will not give you the names, and I am not going to give you a speech about why, because you have never once in eleven years needed one from me. I will give you the offices, the dates, and the numbers, because a number can be looked for and a name can be used, and I have been taught the difference this year by a woman who is not yours and not anybody's and who is the best keeper of records I have ever worked beside, and who is two flights above a salt merchant in this town at this hour and under a writ of enquiry this month because of a paragraph a clerk wrote in a margin about a fair hand in a margin.*

And then: *Aren Kest. I sign with my name and no other, and there is no office in this county and I have not got one, and if your file wants a title it may have this one, which I am told has been used of me eleven times without ever being produced.*

He read it back four times over two days, and it was five hundred words, and it did not contain a refusal and it did not contain a defence, and it contained a date, a fact, and a condition, offered in the plainest form he could find, to a man who had taught him the trade.

---

And on the twenty-first of Longlight at the ninth hour he addressed it, and that is where the four days went wrong for him and stayed wrong.

It is a thing to write a man's name. He had not written it once in eleven years and he had told a bench of nine, in a hall four days off, with his hand on a small oilcloth bundle, that he had never made a second copy of it and never would, and a clerk had entered that he was a witness against himself, and the entry was in a book in a city that does not admit to the Crown that it exists.

The name went on the envelope. Two words, in his own hand, in a small good trade's hand, with the county's post added at the foot.

And the price of it was not on the envelope. The price was the eleven hundred words inside, which had come into this room in a county's brown paper with a wax seal on it, and which were signed, and which were now a document in Merefen.

Here is the whole of the thing he had to get straight between the ninth hour of the twenty-first and the seventh hour of the twenty-second, and he got it straight in a room over a cooper with no fire in it and a piece of chalk and no notebook, because the notebook was not for this.

He could not give it to the Archive.

The instrument of the ninth of Longlight forbids this office to move, copy out, or part with any record held by it, pending an enquiry returnable on the ninth of Rainmonth, and it is in the hands of a court, and the record in question would be a Crown's own letter of eleven hundred words setting out everything the Crown knew about him, signed, sealed, and dated.

If he put it in the press, then the office that the Crown has spent eleven weeks trying to make stop existing would be holding the Crown's own case against him, and a writ would be a matter of days, and the salt-loft rooms would be shut by a bench of a town, and the town would be asked to shut it by the same machinery that shut the trust.

If he took it to the magistrate, he would be handing a Crown's letter to a Crown's county, and Lysa Fenn would have to enter it, and entering it would be the end of her finding of the fourth of Greenmonth in about four minutes.

If he took it to a bench, the bench's roll is a town's roll and he has spent ten weeks in a yard telling four men that nothing that belongs to a man goes in this yard's book and only a thing set.

So it stays in a rented room, in a locked chest that is not his, in a building over a cooper, and it is the only document in his possession that is the other side's own account of him, and there is no chain of custody on it, and there never will be, and in a year or in eleven he will have to say so in a room to somebody.

And he could not show it to Mara Vey.

That was the price that was not about paper and he had not seen it coming until the second of the four days, at about the fifth hour, sitting on the sill of the Old Toll deck.

She would not file it. He knew that inside about four seconds and it was the only thing in eleven hundred words that he was certain of and it was certain in the wrong direction: a keeper of records has a rule on her wall that a thing is entered on the word of a sworn tradesman and not a clerk's word, and a Crown's letter is a clerk's word, and she would want to enter it, and she would have to enter it within a year and a day, and entering it in a press over a salt merchant is the one thing in this county that the Crown wants and the Crown's own instrument of the ninth of Longlight has made into a crime.

Or she would refuse to enter it. And a keeper who refuses to enter a document is a keeper who has a rule she applies when it suits her, and the whole of her four years and the whole of a wall with a rule on it in a fair hand is that the rule does not care, and a woman who has priced being right four times in four months cannot survive one occasion of applying her own rule to a man she has an opinion about.

And she had written to him on the third of Longlight, in a line, in a fair hand, with a date on it: *do not write to me about this. There is nothing you can do about it and I would rather you spent the ninth of Longlight on whatever you are doing to the bench of that guild.*

He had read that as a kindness.

It was not a kindness. It was a woman telling him, in writing, with a date, a fortnight before, that the next thing he brought her would cost her, and she had put it in a line and a date so that she could not be accused of having managed him about it, and he had taken the line and the date for permission and it was the opposite of permission, and he was doing it now, for the second time in a month, to a woman who had said out loud in a filing room that he had managed her for two months and had got worse at it.

So he did not show it to her, and he knew the price of that exactly, and it was the only one of the four costs of that evening that he could put a number on, and it was this: she would find out. Not in a year. Inside a week, he thought, and he was wrong by four days, because a man with a bar and a notebook comes up a street twice a day and there is a stamp in a county's brown paper and a wax seal on it the size of a crown piece, and a woman who has spent four years learning the difference between an argument and a fact does not need to be told about a seal.

And when she found out she would know that he had had it for four days and had not come up the stair. And she would be right, and he would be right, and there would be nothing in either of those facts that anybody could do anything with.

---

He put the letter in the county's brown paper, wrote the address at the foot in his own hand, and sealed it with a disc of tallow out of the cooper's bin because he had no wax and no right to a seal, and the tallow had his thumb in it and the county's post would not have cared.

And at the seventh hour of the twenty-second of Longlight, in the last hour he had, he put it in the bag on Weir Street, and a man came up the street at the eighth hour with the bag on his shoulder and did not know what was in it, and went down to the county town in the rain, and the post goes to the provincial town on the third day, which was the twenty-fourth, and a clerk in a filing room took it out of a bag at the ninth hour of the twenty-fourth and entered it, and a man in a provincial office read the summary at the second hour of the twenty-fifth, and read it twice, and then read the letter, and then did what the marshal's office had been doing since the twelfth of Greenmonth, which is to go to a book.

The book was the county file on the matter of the crossing of Alder Vale, and it was on a desk, and it had a magistrate's finding in it, and a sworn account in it, and a return in it with a particular in the margin, and a schedule of attendance in it with four descriptions in it and no names, and a press-mark of press four, die eleven, cut into a page of a creditor's book in a city four days off, and a page of a bench's own minute four days from here with a hand laid flat on a book in wax.

The man in the provincial office read the letter, and then he took a clean sheet, and he wrote at the top of it, in a good hand, the date and the hour and his own initials in the margin the way clerks have written their initials in the margin for six hundred years:

*That the said Aren Kest has this day returned a letter of eleven hundred words, dated the eighteenth of Longlight, and has answered the instruction in it with a date, a fact, and a condition, and has refused no request and made no argument;*

*And that he has declined to give the names of those to whom he has spoken, and has said so in a manner that was not an argument;*

*And that this office has before it, on the dates set out in his own letter, every place he was, every paper he touched, and every hand that wrote on any of them, in this county, in a county four days off, and in a press, and a yard, and a bench-house, and a hall in a city that does not admit to this office that it exists;*

*And that the names are therefore the only thing in the matter not already held, and that the office has asked for them once, in writing, on the seal of the office, and been refused in writing, on the eighteenth of Longlight, and that the refusal is the first refusal of anything that has ever been made to this office by any person since the office was founded, and that a refusal is a fact and not a defeat;*

*And that he was refused in a room in this county, and a keeper of records in this county has answered every question this office has put in eleven weeks and has not been compelled and has not been bought and has not been sent anything, and that this office's paper of the nineteenth of Greenmonth asked for her to be instructed and nothing came of it, and that the only office that has ever answered her has been the one in the filing room, in a particular in a margin, in fourpence's worth of a clerk's curiosity;*

*And that the question of who else this man has told is therefore not a question about this county and has not been a question about this county since the twenty-first of Greenmonth, and that this office will require the assistance of another office, and that the other office has been asked, and that the other office has a woman in it, and that the other office has a form.*
