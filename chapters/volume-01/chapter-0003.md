# Chapter 0003

The Bellweather Claims Archive was two rooms over a salt merchant, which meant it smelled of brine in Highsun and of wet lime in Mudmonth, and meant the river was audible through the floorboards in a low way that everyone in the room had stopped hearing after a year and everyone new noticed within the hour.

Mara Vey had noticed it every day for four years. She had a way of sitting with her back to the wall and both feet flat, which is not a posture anybody teaches you and which she had arrived at by being the only person in a room who could not leave.

She was twenty-nine. She had been a conservator at Morrow Abbey for two years and a copyist before that, and she had taken this office because a man in a damp cloak at the abbey had said, *you are quick and you are exact and you are bored, and the Bellweather file is a room nobody has been paid to be bored in.* What she had not been told was that the office had no money, no authority, and a name that made her letters get answered slowly.

On the twelfth of Mudmonth she had sixty-one days of work in front of her and three weeks in which to do the one piece of it that mattered.

The renewal line was the problem. Every year the province renewed its finding that no claim to Bellweather survived, and the renewal was dated, and anything entered before the date of a renewal was a claim of the old year, while anything entered after it was a claim made under a finding that nothing survived. It was a piece of administration. It was the difference between a document and a rumour, and the Crown had never had to be cruel about it, because cruelty was expensive and dates were free.

The anniversary was the nineteenth, and the nineteenth was a Sunday, and the office had been shut on it with the blinds down like a house with somebody dead in it. The anniversary was not the date that mattered. The province's own renewal was dated for the second of Greenmonth, and it had nothing to do with anybody's grief and everything to do with a clerk in the provincial town who had copied the same finding out of the same book every year since 301, and who would copy it out again next year, and who had never once been asked whether the thing he was renewing was true.

So she had until the second of Greenmonth, and after that a year of paper in which everything she filed sat underneath a finding that nothing survived. And she had one object in Merefen that had come out of a river.

She had the parish list first, and it was the best thing in the building. Bess Alder had kept the parish list at Alder Cross, which was two hundred people in a village the Crown had never once named, and a list of two hundred names is a list that cannot be argued with about quantity. It sat in the second drawer under a linen sheet with a paper weight of River glass on it, and every time someone asked the Archive how many had died at Bellweather, Mara turned to a different page, because the answer to that question was not a number.

It was four thousand, and the Crown's own figure. And then the villages: Alder Cross two hundred, Saltbrim ninety, Wrenlow sixty, Cobbett a hundred and forty, Nine Elms a hundred and ten, Stonebarrow forty, Hushwater eighty. Seven hundred and twenty.

She had written both figures on the same day in the same hand at the top of the first page and had ruled a line under them, and the two numbers had never once been added together, on that page or any other, because the moment you added them you had made a place called the casualties of the Alder Vale, and a place is not a place. A place has a ford, and a tithe barn, and a schoolmaster, and nine people who argue about a boundary stone every Longlight.

The second drawer also had Ferris Oat.

Oat had been the weighmaster at Saltbrim and had written down the brine weights for thirty-one days before the cull, in a clear arithmetic hand, with the tonnage and the grade and the number of pans and the day of the week in the margin, and he had carried the sum forward correctly. It was the single most useful witness document the office held, and Mara had understood that within a week of arriving, because it was the only thing in eleven years of salvage rolls, claims, and hearsay that could not be argued with. A man may lie about how many he saw drown. He cannot lie about a hundred and forty pans of grey salt, and Ferris Oat had written the number in two different inks because one pan had been short and he had gone back and looked again.

The roll of names she proposed to build out of those weights was not ready and would not be ready this year, and she had a rule about it that she had told nobody: a roll built out of a ledger had to be entered as a ledger, in its own order, with the weights standing beside the names, and not as a speech. A speech could be answered. A ledger could only be checked.

There was also the matter of the registrar at the Crown registry in the provincial town, who held the Crown's own Bellweather entries, and who in Hearthmonth had quoted her four shillings to consult one salvage roll, and had been paid, and had then written that the roll was with the marshal's emergency office and could not be moved. Four shillings for the discovery that a file exists and is elsewhere. She had entered it in the day-book as an expense and as a fact, because a man who has quoted you a price for nothing once will quote you a price for nothing again, and it is worth knowing which.

So: a bell.

The Archive's rule was that an anchor is recorded on the word of a sworn tradesman, in his own hand, and not on a clerk's word, and not on a witness's word, and not on the word of a man who has an interest. She had had that rule written on the wall of the outer room in a fair copy, so that it would be her rule and not her taste, and she had had to fight somebody about it once, and it had held.

Aren Kest was not hard to find. He was a man who repaired the sill of the Old Toll crossing, and the sill was chalked, and the ferrymen knew the chalk, and on the afternoon of the eleventh a woman with a clerk's manner had stood on Wenna Koll's slip and asked which repairer signed the sill and in what year. The answer had been *Kest, the older one, and it's the road warden, and he won't want it said that he's the one who bought the bell.* Which told her two useful things at once: that the town had already joined the bell to the man, and that the man had not asked them to.

He came at the second hour of the afternoon, and he came wet, and he stood in the outer room and looked at the fair copy on the wall long enough for her to know that he had read it, and then he came in and sat down without being asked and put his hands on the table, palms up, the way a man shows that he has nothing in them.

"You sent for me."

"I did. You'll want to know why, so I'll save us the afternoon." Mara set a sheet of paper in front of him. "I want a sworn tradesman's account of what that bell is attached to. Not what it is. What it is a piece of bronze in a river that the Crown lifted and sold for two pence. What I need is the second thing, and I have nobody in this county who can say it, because the only man who will touch the thing is you, and I would rather have your sworn word than a witness's."

"And what's in it for me."

"A custody entry under a claim number of my choosing, an extract from a document I hold, and my signature on a filing that goes in front of a magistrate in this county instead of a letter to the province that will be answered in Hearthmonth." She turned a second sheet round. "And a promise."

He read the first sheet. It was short and it was in the trade's language, which she had learned to write in.

"The anchor," he said. "You want a word for the anchor."

"I want four parts and I will not have three of them. What it is attached to, what you promise about it, what it costs, and who stands there and sees it done. If you swear to three, I'll have you wait in this room until you can swear to four, and the twenty-third will come and go."

"Attached to nothing." He said it without heat. "It's bronze from a founder's yard that went into a river, and the river's got in under the lip and it'll be in the rest of it in ten years, and it hangs on a cradle on a bridge nobody carts to, because a man at a clearance thought nine stone of it was worth two pence. That's the whole of what I can swear to. I'd put my chest against it and it's worth three and a half."

"And if it were attached to something," Mara said, "which it is not, on your evidence — if a thing like that were attached to a memory, could a record show you which memory?"

Aren's thumb moved once across the callus at the base of it. The room was quiet enough to hear the river in the floor and the salt merchant's boy starting a barrow down the stairs.

"Why do you want to know that?"

"Because in eleven years nobody has ever asked it. The Crown's account says a fever. Every claim in this office says a fever with a different cause. And the one object out of that river is hanging on a bridge in this town, and there is a man in this county who thinks it is a liability." She set her pen down. "I am not asking for magic. I am asking whether a record exists that I do not have, and if it does, whether it can be got in front of a magistrate before the province says nothing survived."

"No," Aren said. "Not that I know of."

"That is a fair answer and I will write it exactly as you said it." She wrote, in a small steady hand, *Attested by A. Kest, road warden: to his knowledge the bell is not attached to any remembered place, and no such record is known to him.* Then she put the pen down again. "May I ask one question that is not part of the filing?"

"You may ask one."

"If it is attached to nothing," Mara said, "why did you buy it?"

It was a cheap question. It was also the only question in the room that mattered, and she had asked it in a voice that had no apology in it, and she watched a man of thirty-two decide what to do with his hands.

Then she took it back, which she had learned to do from a witness at Morrow Abbey in her first winter, and which almost nobody in this county can do at all.

"I am sorry," she said. "That was ungenerous. I would rather be wrong in this room than wrong in front of a magistrate, and I have used the same question twice this week on two different men, which is a habit and not a method. Take it as the first of many, and answer it or do not."

He thought about it properly, which she had not expected, and when he spoke he gave her the thing she had actually asked for, which was not an explanation but a number.

"A foundry would charge me forty marks to recast the tools I need for this summer," he said. "I am paid a shilling a day. Nine stone of split bronze, once the split is cut out of it, is a season's tools and a set of irons I do not have to buy off my brother. That is the whole reason, and it is a good reason, and you can write it in the filing exactly as it stands, because it is true."

"That is not what I asked."

"I know what you asked," Aren said. "I am telling you what is true, which is what you wrote on the wall."

She almost liked him for it, and wrote the extract instead.

She gave him the brine weights, copied that morning, thirty-one days of Ferris Oat's pans and grades and marginal days of the week, on one sheet, in her hand, with the sum footed twice and marked as an extract and not a copy. It is the only thing she owns that cannot be argued with, and she handed it across a table to a man she had met an hour ago, and she watched him read it the way a man reads a load table and not the way a man reads a story about a dead weighmaster, and she thought: *he will keep this. He will not know why he is keeping it, and he will keep it.*

"In return," she said, "the promise. That you will not ring that bell. Not at the market, not on the anniversary, not on your own. I will not put a date on it, and you will not give me one."

"You want it undated."

"I want it undated," Mara said, "because a dated promise tells me only which day you meant to keep it. If you gave me the nineteenth, I would know you had chosen a night already, and a man who has already chosen a night is a man who will be busy on it, and I would be a fool to trust the rest of him." She squared the sheet in front of her. "An undated promise is a promise about every day. That is the only reason I am not asking for a date. Write it as I have said it, in your own hand, and it goes in the file with everything else."

The room went quiet in the way a room does when something has been agreed to and neither person has moved yet.

"All right," Aren said.

He wrote it in the fair hand of a man who can sign a bridge contract: *I, Aren Kest, do promise that I will not ring the Bellweather bell.* No date, no place, no witness, and his name under it in a hand that did not shake, and it was the whole of it, because she had not qualified it, and because he had not offered to.

Mara countersigned as the keeper who took it, dated it the twelfth, and put it in the folder, and then she put the folder in the press and turned the handle and sat with her hands in her lap for a moment longer than the work required.

He was at the door when she said his name.

"Kest. One more thing, and then I'll let you go to your flood." She had the day-book open, because she was a woman who could not help preparing the next sentence while she was in the middle of this one. "The Crown's account says four thousand. It has said four thousand for eleven years and it will say four thousand at the renewal, because four thousand is the city and the city is a number a man can hold in his head. The villages are not in it. Not once, in any of the four years of finding I have read. Alder Cross, Saltbrim, Wrenlow, Cobbett, Nine Elms, Stonebarrow, Hushwater. Seven hundred and twenty people who were not the point of the decision and are now a rounding error, and the only reason I know their names is a schoolmistress who wrote them down in a fair hand and a weighmaster who wrote down how much salt went out of a village in the first week of Highsun."

"And the Crown will quote four thousand on the second of Greenmonth."

"The Crown will quote four thousand on the second of Greenmonth, in a finding that nothing survives, and after the second a claim of mine is a claim made under a finding that nothing survived, for a year." She closed the book. "I do not give them one number. If a man in a magistrate's court asks me how many died, I give him the city on one page and the villages on another, and I make him read both, and it takes him twice as long, and that is the whole of my craft and I am not going to apologise for it."

"Then what do you want from me."

"Eight days," Mara said. "Give me eight days and a sworn account of a bell, and I can put a dated object in front of a magistrate in this county on the twenty-third, which is three days before the renewal, and if he rules for me, the provincial finding has to answer a physical thing it cannot explain away. That is not justice. It is a door, and I only want the door."

He looked at the press, and then at the fair copy on the wall, and then at her, and she watched him decide something and not say it, and she filed the not-saying away, because a keeper's first habit is to notice the shape of what has been left out.

"Six days," Aren said. "Bring me a magistrate's paper and I will have it sworn on any working day before the twenty-second, and it will be a tradesman's account and not a story, because that is the only kind you would take."

"You will give me your account by the twenty-second and I will file it on the twenty-third."

"You will get it."

He went out into a rain that had not changed in eleven hours, and the river went on under the floor, and Mara Vey opened the day-book and wrote the day out in full, because that is what a day-book is for and because a file that only records what is convenient is one day going to be quoted back at the people in it.

She wrote the promise in the entry. She wrote the extract of the brine weights, and the number of the custody entry, and the fact that a sworn account was promised for the twenty-second and had not yet been given.

And at the bottom of the page, in the same hand and the same ink, she wrote the other thing, because she had been trained to write down the shape of a silence while it is still fresh:

*He did not tell me that the bell rang on the eleventh with nobody's hand on it. He signed the promise and he looked at me once while he signed it and he has not offered the one fact I have been asking everybody about since Tuesday. Either he does not know it, which I do not believe, or he has a night in mind, and a man who has already chosen a night will not part with the date and will part with everything else. I am not going to press him today. I am going to watch what he does on the nineteenth, and I am going to write down that I asked and that he did not answer.*

Then she noticed that the parish list beside the day-book was lying open at Hushwater, the way it lay open more often than it should, and that under the second name in the second column there was one she had been reading for four years without once letting it mean anything:

*Ollett, Verity, 11. Vey, Hanna, 9.*

Mara Vey read it once. She wrote in the margin, in a hand that did not change at all: *Surname common in the Sable country; no descent traced, no relation, matter closed.* And beneath that, because she was honest with her own day-book when nobody else would ever read it: *I have noticed this once. If I notice it a second time it has stopped being a name and become a coincidence I am using, and that is how archives start lying.*

She turned the page, and shut the book, and sat with her back to the wall and her feet flat while the water went under the floor of the Bellweather Claims Archive, which held thirty-one days of a dead weighmaster's arithmetic, four thousand in the Crown's mouth and seven hundred and twenty in hers, and a promise with no date on it, signed in a good hand by a man who had bought a cracked bell for two pence and who now had eight days in which to prove that it was a piece of metal.
