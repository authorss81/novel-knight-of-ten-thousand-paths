# Chapter 0020

The eighth of Greenmonth was a Thursday and the yard had been cutting face stone since the fourth hour, and by the sixth the dust of it had got into the roof beams and the light in the shed end of it had gone the colour of a bad tooth, and there were four men on the stone and a fifth man doing the cartage and the sound of iron on stone every four seconds, exactly as it had been all day, and it was the sound that told Aren Kest, before he was through the door, that Tovan Kest's yard was not a place where anybody was idle any more.

He stood in the doorway with his notebook in his coat and did not go in.

He had a mark and a quarter in the bag and eleven days of wages that the trust would not pay until there was money in the trust, and a bond standing on the guild's roll for six marks against a tool-chest the roll had priced at three and a half, and about three weeks of shilling-a-day work in front of him that nobody in this town was going to give him, and a bell under a Crown seal four hundred yards up the Old Toll road, and a sill with a dry cross in it. And he was going to need four marks by the morning, and there were two men in this yard between him and four marks, one of whom was his brother and one of whom was a stranger who had come twice and had never yet lied.

It was the stranger who got there first.

---

The guild's clerk came at the sixth hour with the roll under his arm, and came correctly, which is to say he came into the yard instead of sending the boy, and he took his hat off at the door, and he stood clear of the swing of the door, and none of those three things is in a guild rule and all three of them are the reason a town gets anything done.

"Master Kest," he said. "The bench sits tomorrow. I have the roll and I have the bond of the eleventh on it, six marks, surety a tool-chest at three and a half, and I have to write what is proposed, and I have had three answers off three men in a fortnight and I would rather have one off you." He put the ledger on the bench, not on the stone, and kept his hand flat on it. "I have a second bond here. Same roll, same chapter. I can enter it before the bench and the bench can vote on both at once, and I will tell you, as I told you on the twenty-eighth, that I do not think the bench will take it and I think you know that too."

"Read it out," said Aren Kest.

The clerk read it out. Six marks for the removal of an object from the roll. Surety, a tool-chest, three and a half. It was the same sentence twice with the date changed.

"No," said Aren Kest.

The yard had not gone quiet, because four men on a face-stone job do not go quiet for anybody, but there was a four-second gap in the iron where there had not been one.

"Master Kest—"

"You priced it at three and a half on the twenty-eighth of Mudmonth and you said a sentence about it, and I have carried the sentence about for eleven days and I am going to say it back to you in front of my brother and four men who know me, because you did not say it in front of anybody." He put his hand flat on the ledger. "*A man who signs six and has three and a half is a man who has signed on faith, and faith is not a currency we take.* I am not signing a second one of those. You were right then and you are right now and if the bench wants my tools after that it can come and take them and I will not say a word against it in the room."

The clerk looked at the ledger for a moment.

"I did say that," he said. "I do not often hear a man hand it back."

"It was yours. I was in the room."

"Aye." The clerk squared the ledger against the bench. "Then I will tell you what the roll will take, since you have asked me a question and I am the only man in this county who can answer it without a magistrate. The roll will take a debt. Not a bond — a debt with a rate and a term and a third party's name on it, because a third party's name is a thing a roll can hold and a chest of rust is not. I have entered four in eleven years and they were all farm stock and two of them went bad and the bench let me off the other two, which tells you the bench is not a hard court and is only right by accident."

The bench of the guild was against the far wall, and a man at the shave horse had his back to the room and had not turned round once since Mara Vey had been in the yard, and Tovan Kest had not been at the shave horse.

He had been at the bench with a contract on it and a lump of chalk and a pencil behind his ear, and he had been listening to all of it, and when the clerk had finished he put the chalk down and came over, and he did not look at his brother.

"Not a bond," he said. "A wage advance. Four marks out of the chest against the cutting, cleared at eight pence a day off the yard rate and taken at the first Friday after delivery and not before, and it goes in my book on the eighth with the date and the hour and the contract number on the top of the page, and the same line goes in the guild roll as a debt and not as a surety."

"Tovan—"

"You did not ask me," said Tovan Kest, "and I want that said in the room, because you did not, and because in three weeks there will be a man in this town saying a road warden came into a yard and took four marks off his brother, and he will be half right, and the half he is right about is the half that costs me." He turned to the clerk. "Enter it as a debt. Wages advanced against a cutting contract at eight pence a day. And put the factor's name on the line as the third party, because that is what makes it a debt and not a favour, and if anybody in this room including me wants to be able to open a book in six months and see that this was real, that is the line that will do it."

"And if the cutting does not finish?" said the clerk, who was a careful man and had asked the right question.

"Then it is carried on," said Tovan. "Not forgiven and not written off. Carried on, at the yard rate, against the balance of the contract, and if the contract is ever broken I will stand in front of the bench and say the debt is mine and the yard's, and I will not say your brother's name in that sentence, and that is the whole of what the yard is doing."

Aren Kest stood in the middle of his own brother's yard and worked out, with the plainness a man gets when the thing he has been avoiding turns out to be the only clean thing in the room, that he was about to be helped, and that the terms were correct, and that a man cannot decline correct terms without saying why, and that he has no why that is not a lie.

He got the bag out.

He put a mark and a quarter on the bench on top of the contract, in coin, and it was all of it, and the coins made a small flat sound on the paper that four men cutting stone heard and did not turn round for.

"That comes off the top," he said. "So it is not four marks. It is two marks and three quarters, and it is cleared at eight pence a day like the rest, and it goes in your book the same way and on the same page, and I want my mark and a quarter on that line as well and I want it entered as paid and not as credited, because the difference between paid and credited is the difference between me and a stranger and I am not going to be a stranger in my brother's book."

Nobody in the yard said anything about that, and Tovan Kest picked up the coin and put it in the yard's chest and did not make a single remark, which was the correct response and was the only one that would have been any use.

"Right," said Tovan. "Two marks and three quarters at the yard rate. Friday, the yard book, your mark and your brother's, and the clerk will put his own hand on the copy because I have found out this month that a thing in one hand is a thing a man can deny and a thing in two is a thing he cannot."

---

The clerk ruled his line, and then he did the thing that made Aren Kest understand that the man had come to the yard for this and not only for the roll.

"Master Kest. I have to write a head to this. I have *wages advanced against a cutting contract*, and that is the whole of what I can honestly put down, and above it I have to put a name, and against the name I have to put what a road warden is doing with four marks of glass, and I have to write it before tomorrow at the tenth hour or the bench has nothing to vote on." He put the pen down. "And I will tell you what else I have to write. On this same roll there is a sworn account in your hand that says a city of four thousand people is standing, and a stamp on it from a magistrate of this county, and there is a second paper in a Crown hand that calls you the keeper of a thing this bench has no entry for, and both of those came into this building inside ten days, and tomorrow nine men are going to sit in a room and decide whether the guild's money goes on the bridge or on the man. So I am going to ask you a question I have no standing to ask and I would rather have your answer than make one up."

"Ask it."

"How many."

The iron went on the stone every four seconds.

"The crossing," said Aren Kest. "Or the villages, or the fever, or whatever a man means. Say which and I will answer it and I will not answer the others, and if you write one figure on that roll I will hold you to it in a year."

"The villages," said the clerk.

"Then the villages are counted by name and not by me and not by the Crown, and the Crown's figure of four thousand is a figure for a crossing and belongs to the Crown, and it is not a figure for seven villages, and I am not going to stand in a yard and put it beside them, and I am not going to put one village beside another either, and you can write that second sentence down as well and it is the one I would fight for."

He said it and then waited, because a man who has refused a question and answered it in the same breath has not refused it, and the iron went on the stone every four seconds and nobody in that yard filled the gap, which was the correct thing for all of them to do.

Then he recited them, and it was a recitation and not an answer, and he made his voice do it the way a keeper of records does it, flat, with no help.

"Alder Cross, two hundred. Saltbrim, ninety. Wrenlow, sixty. Cobbett, a hundred and forty. Nine Elms, a hundred and ten. Stonebarrow, forty. And Hushwater, eighty. Those are the figures of a keeper of records in Merefen and she is the one who counted them, and I am repeating them, and I have not checked one of them against a parish book, because I do not keep parish books, and I would not put them on that roll in my own hand if you asked me to."

"And the sum?"

"There is no sum. Not on this roll and not on mine and not in the yard." He said it the way a man says a thing he has been given rather than found. "And I am going to tell you why, because you have been decent about a bond that was not decent and you have come into a yard instead of sending a boy, and because the reason is worth more than the figures. There is a keeper of records two floors above a salt merchant in this town, and on the eighth of Greenmonth, in a room, in front of the clerk of this court, who is no friend of hers and had walked four miles up a hill to be in that room, she was asked to write one number and she would not, and she gave the reason, and it is a good reason and it is hers and I am not going to improve on it or explain it for her."

He said it, then, in a yard, in the dust, to nine people, and it took a little over a minute, and he said it badly compared with how she had said it, and it did not matter.

"A total is one number. One number is a thing somebody can check once and disprove once, and nobody has to look at why it was given. The Crown does not have to answer one word she has said in four years. The Crown has to find one error in it. One. And then seven villages are dependent places on a bridge that was never laid and there is nothing left of them to appeal with, because the finding is in and the sum is out and the sum is hers. A name is not that. Nobody disproves a name, they look for it, and looking for one of them is a thing a man does for a year and comes back with a book in a woman's hand." He stopped. "A magistrate of this county wrote a finding on Sunday that says a Crown paper is a Crown paper and nobody behind it can be named. That is the same sentence. It is in two places, and neither place is a speech, and I am the only man in this yard who has read both."

The clerk wrote nothing for a while.

"I am going to enter *no reason given*," he said.

"That is correct."

"No reason given," said the clerk, "and the figures of a keeper of records, unverified by this office, and I am going to leave the sum line blank, and I am aware that a blank line in a guild roll is a thing a man can look at and draw whatever he likes out of, and I have decided I would rather be argued with for six months than be the clerk who wrote the number."

The bench of the guild was against the far wall, and a man at the shave horse turned round for the first time all afternoon.

"Tovan," he said. "Is that a debt or a gift?"

"That is a debt," said Tovan Kest, "and I will answer for it at the bench, and it is a debt because there is a rate and a term and a third party's name, and you may have the line and read it and I would rather you did."

The man turned back to his work.

---

He went up the Old Toll road at the seventh hour with two marks and three quarters of his brother's money in a bag and his own nothing in his pocket, and the road leaves the river a quarter of a mile above the crossing and starts the long climb to the Steppe, and the glasswright's lean-to was a hundred yards into the climb.

The fire was lit.

That was the first thing. On the first of Greenmonth the furnace at the back of that shed had been cold, because there had been no order in the county. There was an order now, or there was a man with four marks, and the two are the same thing on the Steppe, which is a thing a man learns there and never anywhere else.

"Quarren."

"Master Quarren, if it's going on a page." He did not offer the stool. He was standing, which he had not been on the first, and he had his sleeves turned up, and there were two journeymen in the back who had not been there on the first either, because on the first there had been nothing to be anybody's journeyman about. "You have come back in a week, which in eleven years is twice, and you have come back without the money, which is the other half of it, so you have come to be told the price again."

"I have come with the money and I have come to say the words you asked me for on the first," said Aren Kest, "and I am going to say them before the money, because you told me you would ask the question again and I am not going to have you ask it with a bag on the table."

He put the bag on the bench and did not open it.

"Illvane Quarren," he said. "There is a man in Merefen who will be standing on the stone when it goes. He is my brother and he is a bridge carpenter and he has told me in a yard tonight that he will not promise me anything, and I have not asked him to, and I would rather have a man who says he will not promise than a man who says yes, and that is the whole of the answer and it is not an answer to your question and I know it is not."

Quarren looked at him for a while. Then he pulled the cloth off the rack and took a disc out in it and did not open it, which was exactly what he had done on the first.

"And the other end," he said. "Who is standing on the other end of it."

"I do not know," said Aren Kest. "There is a city with about four thousand people on it and a bench of nine men who have asked me for a promise to come back and I have given it, and there is a woman there who would not be grateful to me for saying so, and I have not been in the room for eleven days and I do not know who is holding anything at this hour or whether they are holding it well. I do not know. It is the whole of my position and it has been the whole of my position since the twenty-seventh."

"Then I am not selling you a promise," said Master Quarren, "and I never was, and glass is what I sell, and I have been selling glass in this shed for thirty years to men who wanted a promise and who went away and were disappointed in me, and I am not going to do it to you. I have a pair in the rack and they are not a promise and they are not a mystery. They are two discs of Steppe glass that came down the road in the winter at the count price and have been in that rack since the first of Frostmonth, and they are a year annealed and they have been a year annealed since before I ever paid for them, and I have never had a use for them and there is one other."

"Two marks?"

"Four the pair, and I told you the price on the first and it has not changed while you have been standing in my shed and it is not going to change." He put the disc back in the cloth and the cloth on the rack. "And here is the thing you did not understand on the first, and I am going to say it once and then never again. The slip on the post is not a lie. The slip is a price list, and a price list assumes that a man comes in today with the money and I cut today, and what he gets in a fortnight is a thing that has been through its year, and the year does not begin when I cut. It begins in the hole. That is the whole trade, and it is the whole reason it is four marks and not four shillings, and every man in this kingdom who has ever cut a disc and used it inside the year has had an axe head and did not know it."

He turned round and looked at the rack.

"That pair has had its year," he said. "It has had its year and it has been standing in a cold shed since the first of Frostmonth with nobody's name on it, and I can have it dressed to a pin and fitted to a seat and in your stone inside two days, and if I cut you a new pair today you would be standing on your arch in a year and if I hand you that pair you will be standing on it in the morning."

"Two days."

"Two days, and I will do it tonight because you have been up that road twice and I do not like a man who comes twice." He came and stood over the bench. "I am going to tell you what this costs me, because you have given me a true price twice and I am not going to sell you out of a rack for the same four marks without saying what is on the shelf after. That pair is the only glass in this county that is not a promise. When it goes, there is nothing in this shed but rough stock and my own hand, and there is no fresh Steppe glass north of here before Longlight, and every man on the Steppe road with a hole to put something in is going to be told by a man with a counting-house in the provincial town to come back in the autumn. That is thirty years of a complaint I have been making to nobody, and I have just answered it with my own shelf, and I would like you to understand that I have thought about that for about four seconds since you said the words about your brother and I have decided I do not care."

He opened the bag on the bench and counted it out onto the plank, and it was two marks and three quarters, and it was a mark and a quarter less than the slip, and he did not look up.

"Somebody has been telling you nine shillings," he said.

"Somebody has told somebody nine shillings."

"Nine shillings is more than half my price and it is a lie that came off my board, and it is the reason a market works, and I would like the man who told you to come and tell me his name so that I can look at him." He put the coin in a box and shut the box. "There. Now say the rest of it, because you have been in my shed twice and you have not asked me the thing you actually came to ask, and I have watched you not ask it for about a minute and a half."

Aren Kest stood in the open front of a glasswright's shed on a road going up out of a fen.

"I want to know what happens to a scar," he said. "Not to the glass. To the sill. There is a cut cross in the stone a quarter of a mile down that road, two hands across, dry inside while the whole bridge is wet, and it is not a stain and it is not a fill and it is where a thing came through eleven days ago, and I have put my hand on it twice and had nothing, and I have been told that a scar in a sill is a scar forever, and I would like to know from a man who sets glass in stone what the third one is."

Master Quarren was quiet for a moment.

"It is the third one," he said. "A scar is a place a thing came through and a place a thing came through is a place a thing can come through again, and if you put glass in it, you have made an anchor of it, and an anchor that a man made himself will do what he tells it to do until the day it is tired. Two, easy. Three is not a number I would sell to a stranger and I would not sell it to a friend." He picked up his tool roll. "Set it tonight. I will be down your road in an hour and I want the seat cut and I want it cut by you and not by me, because the man who cuts the seat is the man who chose it, and I want that on your paper and not on mine."

---

The light was going off the fen by the time he got to the crossing, and it went fast, and by the time he was on the abutment a man could have read the chalk in the joints of the parapet and no more than that.

He had not looked at the post going up the road and he did not look at it now, and the not-looking was a decision and it had cost him something, because a wax seal is the most interesting object in that county this month and it was four feet from his elbow.

He wrote two lines of it in the notebook, the hour and the state of the wax, because he was going to write down everything this month and a man who records a fly on a seal has told you what his standard of evidence is. Then he put the notebook away and did not go and look at the crack in the bottom edge of the wax, which he had looked at on the seventh, and which he was going to have to tell a glasswright about in an hour.

The cradle was empty and the iron was rusted into the stone and the moss was green over the corner of the clean rectangle.

He got down onto the sill and got his hand flat on the cross, because he was going to do it every time from now on and he had better be in the habit.

Nothing.

Not a warmth, which he had got on the twenty-seventh. Not a fold in the air, which he had got in a storm. Not a word. There was a cross of dry stone under his palm and a hand in the road and a black road in the hand, and after a while his hand got cold, which was all that happened, and it was the correct condition of things and he had written that sentence in a notebook on the seventh and he had not written it once since.

He took his hand off and got the tools out of the bag on the parapet and laid them in the order he would want them, which is the order that means he is not frightened.

Then he cut the seat.

It is a slow job and it is a stupid-looking job and there is nobody watching it. The stone is the third and fourth lengths from the abutment, three courses of setts in a lime bed, repacked in the autumn and again in the week before the storm, and you do not cut a seat for a pin in a bed of setts, you cut it in a single block, so he lifted two of the setts out of the third course with a chisel and a bolster and set them aside in the order they came out, and he found a block of the fourth course that would take it, and he cut the seat into that block, and the seat was six inches long by two and a quarter deep and an inch and a half in the face of it, and it was cut square and it was cut to a line he struck twice from the parapet, because a seat cut by eye is a seat that will not hold a thing for long.

It took him fifty minutes. The cold came off the river and his hands went at the fourth hour and he got them back twice by breathing into them, which is a thing a man does on a bridge in Greenmonth and does not mention in a yard.

Master Quarren came at the ninth hour with a lamp in one hand and his tool roll in the other and a sheet of paper folded inside his coat, and he looked at the seat for a long time without saying anything, which is the highest thing a maker can do.

"That is square," he said at last. "That is properly square. I have dressed a hundred seats and I have seen nine of them square and I have seen the other ninety-one cut by a man who was in a hurry and who believed the glass would not know." He set the lamp down. "I said I wanted it cut by you and I am glad, and I would like it understood that I have not said that to a customer before and I may not say it again."

He dressed the two discs.

That is the part nobody sees. A disc of anchor-glass comes out of the annealing lehr a year old with a face like the bottom of a pond, and it has to be brought to a true edge and then to a true face, and the face has to be flat enough to take a bed and true enough that a man can put a thumb on it and know in one second whether it is right, and the whole of the making is in that second. He worked it on a block of stone on the parapet with a wheel and three grits, and he did not hurry, and he turned the disc four times and looked at the light through it each time, and once he put it down and stood looking at the river for a while and Aren Kest did not say anything to him, because a man at that stage of a job is not a man you talk to.

Then he set them.

Two discs, edge to edge, across the two lines of the cross, the pin through both and into the block, a bed of lime and oak dust under the seat and a collar of oak over the head of the pin, and the maker's mark cut under the near disc where it would never come out and never be seen. He put the collar on and tapped it three times and stopped.

"Quarren."

"Kest."

"I want your part of it in writing." Aren Kest had the notebook open and a stub of chalk and no pen, and he said it anyway. "I cannot pay you for a thing that is only in your head. I need the setting you have used, the two discs, the date, and your name, and I need it in your hand and I need it to say what a thing that fails will look like, because I am going to be the man standing on a bridge when it goes and I want to be able to tell the difference between glass letting go and stone letting go."

Master Quarren stood on a bridge in a lantern's half-mile with a wheel in one hand and looked at him for a while.

"That is the first time in thirty years," he said, "that a man has asked me for the way it fails instead of the way it holds." He took the folded sheet out of his coat and gave it to him, and it was not folded, and it was not a sheet, and it was a page out of a book of his own accounts with a heading and eleven lines under it in a small, level, unhurried hand, and the heading was *setting of anchor-glass, seat in cut stone, and how it will fail.*

He wrote his name at the foot and the date and the hour and nothing else, and he did not charge for the page, and Aren Kest noticed him not charging for the page and did not say anything about it then and thought about it for the rest of the night.

Then Tovan Kest came out onto the parapet with a lamp of his own and a sheet of paper in his hand, and stood at the other end, and would not come closer than a man needs to.

"Right," he said. "I have read yours."

"You have had an hour."

"I have read it twice and I have one thing to say and then I am going home, because I have a cutting on at the fourth hour and a fifth man doing cartage who will not do it alone." He held up the sheet. "This says a bridge, and a sill, and a seat cut in the fourth course, and two discs set across a mark on the stone, and the maker's hand at the foot, and that is all facts and I can be the man who says I saw them set, because I did, and I will put that on the paper in my own hand and the hour on it, and that is a thing I can stand behind in six months because I stood here and watched it happen.

"I am not writing anything else. I am not writing that it will hold and I am not writing that you are going and I am not writing one word about a bell. If I write a sentence about a road, then in a year there is a piece of paper in a bench-room in this town with my name at the bottom saying that I believed in it, and I do not believe in it, and I have not believed in it since I was seventeen, and a man should not put his name under a thing he does not believe in. So that is the line and that is the whole of what I will do, and you knew that when you asked me, and you asked me anyway, and that is not nothing."

"It is not nothing," said Aren Kest.

"And I am going to say the other two things now, while there are three men on this bridge," he said, "and I am not going to say them on a street afterwards, because I have worked out that I can be a great deal more honest at the eleventh hour on a wet bridge than I am at noon in a market, and I would rather find that out about myself tonight than in a year."

Nobody in the yard said anything. Down at the shed the fire had gone down to a red bed, and the two journeymen were still at their work at the eleventh hour, which they had no business doing, and which said something about a yard that had work in it.

"One. It is the ninth in the morning. Not a fortnight and not tonight. There is a bench of this guild sitting tomorrow at the ninth hour to vote on a bond of mine, and I am not going to be in that room, because I have a market and a date and a thing to be doing at the sixth hour. And there is a clerk in the provincial town who is expecting a keeper of the Bellweather hinge in the morning, and there is not one, and I am not going, and he will write *absent* in a book two hundred miles from here. In a year that book will be a fact about me instead of an argument."

"And the second one," said Tovan Kest, without moving.

"The second one is that I do not know what is at the other end of it. Not a man and not a name and not a room. There are four thousand people living on that side of the door and a bench of nine who have asked me for a promise to come back, and I gave them my word instead of terms, and there is nobody at that end who has been told one word about tonight. I have a maker's account and a carpenter's hand and a date and an hour, and not one of those is a person, and I would rather say that out loud to two men on a bridge than carry it up Weir Street and say it to nobody at all."

Tovan Kest stood on his own parapet in the dark with a lamp in his hand and did not put it down.

"Then write on it," he said, "that I would not put my name under a road, and that I would put my name under a bridge. I have not believed in a road since I was seventeen and I am not going to start at thirty-one in a wet coat. But a bridge I have watched get set this evening, and I can say what I saw and no further than that. And if you write that distinction on the paper yourself tonight and not on Friday morning, then a man in a bench-room in six months will be able to tell the difference between a brother who did not believe his brother and a brother who did not believe in a road."

Master Quarren, who had been closing his tool roll and had not been asked to stay, stopped with a strap in his hands.

"Master Kest. I have been in this trade thirty years, and a man has just told me the date of a thing he cannot describe, on a bridge I shall be on again in nine years, in a county where I have no other work. If it goes, I can tell you what a seat in cut stone does about it, because you asked me and I wrote it down. If it holds, come and see me, because a man who comes back is worth the glass, and I have not said that to a customer before and I do not expect to have to say it twice."

"Good." Tovan put his lamp on the parapet. "Then read me the bit you have written for yourself, because I am a carpenter and I know what a man writes when he is writing to himself and I have read a great many of them in a bench-room in my life."

Aren Kest read it out on a bridge in the dark, in the flat voice a man uses when he is reading a thing and not reciting it.

*I, Aren Kest, road warden of Merefen, on the eighth day of Greenmonth, YR 312, at the tenth hour, being on the Old Toll crossing above the seat cut this evening in the fourth course of the sill at the near abutment, where two discs of anchor-glass have been set by Ilvane Quarren of Merefen, whose hand and whose account of how it will fail are on the sheet bound with this — set my purpose down in plain words, because a man who keeps it in his head keeps it in his head alone. The purpose is this. I intend on the ninth day of Greenmonth to go out of this world through this seat and into a place where about four thousand people are living, and to come back, if there is a way back, through a seat I have prepared and somebody else has put a hand to, and I do not know how many days that will be and I do not know what the door is made of and I have been told by a woman keeper of records on the sixth that a path does not write letters. I do not have a person to witness what I am about to do. I have a maker's account, a carpenter's sight of a thing set, a date, and an hour, and I am putting them on a page because a page cannot be asked to turn round.*

*I do not promise that it will hold. I do not promise that I will come back. I promise that if I go, I went from here, on this date, at this hour, on my own purpose, and that these four hands are the record of it and not the road.*

He read it twice and folded it and gave it to his brother, and Tovan Kest read it standing up in his own hand and signed it at the foot with the hour, and it went into the yard's book on the ninth page of the week, and the clerk of the guild was sent for and put his own name on a copy in the same hand at the same hour, and a third copy went up two flights of stair to a press in a room over a salt merchant, because an office that keeps a record outside the Crown's custody is an office that has thought about the morning it loses the room.

---

He went up the bank towards the town at the eleventh hour with the bag empty, a mark and a quarter of his own gone into a chest in Merefen in front of five men, a seat in a bridge with two discs in it and a maker's mark under the near one, and the moon was not doing anything and the wind was from the south-west and smelled of the fen and somebody's fire, and he was not cold and he was not warm and there was nothing in the air at all.

Then he stopped on the wharf road, in the middle of it, and made himself do the arithmetic out loud, in the dark, in the street, to the river, because a man who does it in his head is a man who has already decided what it comes to.

"Four marks," he said. "Two marks and three quarters of it mine to pay back at eight pence a day, and a fortnight and a bit of Fridays off my brother and a factor's contract standing behind it, and two men in this town who are owed money by me and one of them is a clerk with a blank sum line in his roll and one of them is a woman with a finding that says a Crown paper is a Crown paper. That is the glass."

He stood there.

"That is the second of three this season," he said. "Three is what a man gets and there is no arguing with the three, and I have spent one of them in Mudmonth and it cost me nine days of a calendar the saying allows three, three days of fever, a market that did not happen, and a bell, and a bell is not a thing I can get back this year or any year, because it is under a Crown seal and the Crown was within its rights, and I want that said out loud on a street, because I have been not saying it for eleven days."

Nothing answered him, which was the correct condition of things.

"Tomorrow is the ninth," he said. "It is a market day and the yard will be shut and there will be four hundred people in that square and there will not be one road warden in it, and there is a clerk sitting in a room in the provincial town who is expecting a keeper of the Bellweather hinge, and there is not one, and I am not going, and he is going to write *absent* in a book on a piece of paper in a building two hundred miles from here, and that book will say it in a year, and in a year it will be a fact about me instead of an argument."

He started walking again, and then he stopped once more, because there was one more line and it was the worst one and he had been walking toward it for an hour.

"And I am going to stand on a bridge in the morning with a maker's account and a carpenter's signature and a date, and I have told two men on this bridge tonight what I can do and not one person what happens at the other end, because I do not know. I do not have a person on this side and I do not have a person on that side, and the thing I am going to do on the ninth is the second time I have done it and the first time I did it on a bell in a storm in the dark without saying a word to anybody, and it cost nine days and a bell and a market, and this time I have written it down and paid for it and given it to three other people to hold, and that is better, and it is not enough, and I know exactly what it is not enough in and that is the thing I am carrying up Weir Street tonight."

Then he went home, and he went up the stair to the room above the cooper and shut the door on a draught he could not shut the door on, and the moulding was damp and the stair smelled of oak shavings and tar because the man downstairs had been a cooper for thirty years, and he was eleven days in arrears and eleven days further off a wage and a mark and a quarter out of pocket, and the river went over four bays in the dark and did not answer him, and he had not expected it to, and there was a market in the morning.

A quarter of a mile up the Old Toll road a bridge with no bell on it had two pieces of Steppe glass in the scar of a cross, cut square by a man with a chisel, dressed by a man with a wheel, set by a man with a lamp, and a pin through both of them with an oak collar on its head, and the maker's mark under the near disc, and the maker's account of how it will fail bound in a book over a salt merchant, and a page in a yard's week-book with three hands on it, and nobody standing on it, and nobody waiting for it, and the ninth of Greenmonth twenty-four hours off and closing.
