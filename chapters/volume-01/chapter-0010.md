# Chapter 0010

The fever let go of him at some point in the small hours of the thirtieth, and by morning he was weak and upright and had to put a hand on the frame of the chair to convince himself that the floor was where he thought it was.

He came down Weir Street at the ninth hour with his notebook in his coat, because the notebook was in the coat and the coat was the only thing he owned that had been to Bellweather, and there was a folded sheet of oilcloth inside it that was not his, and the eleven years of field notes in the breast pocket that were his, and both of them had come back with him through a crack in a bridge nine days late.

The salt-loft rooms were on the west side, over the man who weighed salt for a living, up a stair that took the light from a window on the half-landing, and the smell of it got into his clothes and stayed there for days afterwards, and he had not been able to explain to anybody in Bellweather-Living what a thing it was to have an office that somebody had to climb a stair to reach.

The door was open. The blind was up.

She was not alone.

Mara Vey had a table clear of everything that was not work, which was the first thing he understood about the room on the second day he had ever seen it, and there were three chairs at it, and in one of them was a woman he did not know, in a magistrate's grey with a paper in front of her and her hands folded on it.

"Mr Kest," said Mara. "Sit down. You are grey and I am not going to have a man fall over in my filing room, and if you do I shall have to write down that you fell over."

He sat down.

"You have three days," he said.

"You have three days," she agreed, "and you will spend two of them in a chair being asked the same question twice, and the third one you will spend being useful, and that is the entire of what I can promise a man who was nine days gone in a country that does not exist." She turned her chair. "Magistrate Fenn, this is the road warden. Mr Kest, this is the magistrate of Merefen, who has come out from the provincial town this morning at my written request, and who has not sat down since Longlight."

The woman looked at him for a moment with the flat attention of somebody who has been looking at documents all morning and has not yet looked at a person.

"Fenn," he said, and then stopped, because he had been about to say *your worship*, which is what a man says to a magistrate, and which Lysa Fenn would find as interesting as a horse's opinion.

"Fenn will do," said the magistrate. "And do not put your hand on my table. The clerk of the provincial court is going to be very interested in how a bell came out of a river in a place the crown says there is no city, and I am going to be the one who has to explain it, and I intend to explain it with a table that nobody has leaned on."

He took his hand off the table.

She was thirty, and she had been a magistrate for five years, and she had the manner of a woman who has discovered that procedure is the only thing standing between a frightened person and a generous stranger with a sword, and who has not yet found a case in which that was untrue.

"Now," said Lysa Fenn. "Three documents and no speeches. I will read them because I am the magistrate and that is what I am for, and neither of you is going to help me."

She took up the first paper.

"A sworn account of a bell by one Aren Kest, road warden, made before me this morning, in which he swears to the anchor, the bound, the counterweight, and the witness, and states that to his knowledge the bell is not attached to any remembered place. He is a tradesman and he swears as one and I accept it." She put it down. "Second. A claim exhibit with a number on it and a custody entry, offered by the keeper of this office, dated the thirtieth of Mudmonth. Third. A witness statement entered in the Merefen watch book on the twenty-seventh, signed by the sergeant and read aloud in my hearing this morning, in which a man named Kest states that he stood in a city of four thousand people on a bridge that stands."

She looked up.

"I have read a great many extraordinary things in five years and two of them were extraordinary. The first was a man's application for a bridge repair in Frostmonth, three years ago, which was not extraordinary at all and which I mention because I did not believe it, and believing it turned out to matter." She squared the exhibit. "This is not extraordinary. This is a man who says he went somewhere. What makes it a case is not what he says. It is that he wrote down a document that predates every word he has ever said out loud, and he carried it out of a place that the crown's own finding says was a fever pit, and he brought it back in his coat, and he is here on the thirtieth instead of the twenty-second, which is the day he promised the keeper of this office, and I want the twenty-second accounted for before anybody in this room gets comfortable."

The room got less comfortable.

"I was nine days gone," Aren said. "I was nine days gone and three days of it was a fever and I could not hold a pen, and I did not break a promise to a person who trusted me. I broke a promise to a person who did not, and she told me she would not, and she was right to."

"Miss Vey." Lysa Fenn did not look at Mara. "Did you take a promise from this man on the twelfth?"

"I took a promise that he would not ring a bell. Undated, at his request to leave it undated, against my advice. I have it in the file, signed, and it is a good signature, and it is a promise without a limit, and it was given to me by a man who had already chosen a night."

"What night?"

"The nineteenth," said Aren.

"The nineteenth was a storm," said Mara, "and on the twentieth the watch was on a bridge in this town with an empty cradle, and there is a statement in a watch book that puts a man on the crown of an arch at the seventh hour of the morning, and the arithmetic from the twelfth to the twentieth is a bell in Merefen and a bell in a city four hundred feet over a river, and I did not have to be a clever woman to see it. I had only to be an archivist, which is a woman who counts." She put her pen down. "He did not lie to me. That is the part I have not been able to put down in a file, and it is the reason I asked him for a promise and not for an oath. He has not lied to me yet, and he decided on the twelfth what I was to be allowed to know, and he is a better bridge-builder than I am a keeper of records, and both of those things are the same thing."

Aren Kest put his hand flat on his own knee.

"Then I will make it a matter of file," he said. "There were two things I did not say, and both of them are mine, and if this is going in a page then the page had better have all of it or none of it."

"Go on," said Lysa Fenn, and did not move.

"I told Miss Vey I would not ring the bell and I gave her a promise with no date on it and I had already chosen the night of the nineteenth before she asked me for anything. I knew the nineteenth was the night when the storm would be loudest. I had worked that out on the eleventh. I did not tell her, and I signed anyway, and a man who signs a promise he has already decided to break is not a man who was honest, he is a man who was careful about where the paper was."

"And the second thing."

"I refused my brother six marks on the eleventh and signed a bond for six against my tool-chest instead, and the reason was not the money. The money would have bought the bronze and nothing else. The bond put the bell in my name in front of the guild, and I chose to be the man answerable for it, because being answerable is the last thing left that nobody has given me and I would rather have that than the four and a half. I have not said that out loud to anybody, not then and not since, and it is in the file now because you are putting my own part in with the rest."

Lysa Fenn wrote for some time.

"You are a nuisance," she said, without any particular feeling. "You know that. Every man who has ever come into a room like this has been either a liar or a nuisance, and the ones who are nuisances are at least readable, and I can work with a nuisance." She put the pen in the inkstand. "I will rule on what I can rule on. Miss Vey's exhibit is dated the thirtieth of Mudmonth, which is three days before the province renews its finding, and I take it into the record of this court as a document produced by a subject of this county, with a chain of custody from the hands of the man to the hands of the keeper to this table, witnessed by me and by nobody else, because there is nobody else."

"I am the second witness," said Aren. "The sergeant's book has my statement in it, in his hand, with the date, and the watch captain read it to you this morning. That is two men who have had it out of the same mouth on paper, one of them hostile to me."

"That is a record of a man's account of a crossing," said Lysa Fenn. "That is not a witness to the document. If you want the document witnessed twice, you need a man who was in that room who is not a watch officer, and I do not have one, and I have thought about it for a quarter of an hour while I was on the road and there is not one in Merefen who is not either related to you or paid by the town."

"I'll do," said Mara Vey.

"You will file it."

"I'll witness it and file it," said Mara, "and those are two different acts, and you will have noticed that they are, because you have been in this room five minutes. Yes. I'll do."

Aren Kest took the counterfoil out of the oilcloth.

It is one thing to carry a thing for nine days. It is another to put it down. He had held it against his ribs in a ferry yard and in a hall with a stone in the floor and on a road of raised causeway that turned round after four miles because the river turned, and he had thought about it every one of those nine days, and now he put it on a magistrate's table in a salt-loft office in a fen town, and took his hand away, and did not ask for a copy, and did not ask how long it would be in the room, and did not ask whether a man could get a thing back after he had given it away.

"Chain of custody," said Lysa Fenn. "In words."

"Out of a room over a toll house in Bellweather," said Aren, "where it lay on a table under a cloth, and where a bench of bridgewrights wrote down in its own book that the stub existed and that the other half did not. Folded in four, in the breast of a coat. That coat was the coat I was standing in on the first of Mudmonth, YR 312, in a yard in Merefen, when a Crown officer lifted this out of the river and put it on a clearance roll and sold it for two pence. So it has been in the same fold since the first of the month. In this room since the ninth hour this morning."

"Then the chain has a hole in it the size of the province," said the magistrate.

"The chain has a hole in it the size of a bell," said Mara, "and the hole is the document. It is Crown salvage. It went on a Crown clearance roll in YR 302 and came off the block at a Crown clearance for two pence, and every page of its history for eleven years belongs to the Crown's office and not to us. In eleven years this office has kept one set of its records outside Crown custody, and I have never once known why the rule mattered.

"It matters this morning. An exhibit in Crown custody is Crown property, and Crown property can be produced, produced at, and produced for, by an office that has already told us where this bell came from. An exhibit in an outside record is a thing a magistrate is obliged to answer for, and you are the magistrate. Chain it before the province renews, and put the number on the outside of the wrapper, where a clerk has to touch it."

Lysa Fenn read the counterfoil.

It took her a long time and she read it three times, and when she came to the seal she turned the sheet so the whole table could see it, and it was a small disc of grey wax with a device on it: a hand laid flat on a book, and a road, and a lamp.

"That is the seal of the marshal's emergency office," she said. "That is also, and I have seen it twice this morning on two pieces of paper, the device on the order that the Merefen watch executed on the nineteenth. The same wax, the same press, the same hand, and I will put that in a record in my own hand this afternoon, because it is the one thing in this file that does not depend on anybody's memory."

She put it down, and looked at Aren.

"I am going to tell you two things, and I am telling you them because I would rather be fair in advance than merciful later. One. I am going to hold a hearing on that order on the fourth of Greenmonth, in this room, with the watch captain under oath and the bridge guild's notice of the eleventh in front of me, and I will decide whether a sealed paper from a provincial office can put a merchant's bell into a provincial storehouse in a town that is not the crown's. I have already read your trust's charter and your town's boundaries and I can tell you that the answer is not the one either of you wants, because the honest answer is *yes, and it took eight days, and somebody must answer for it*, and I will be the one who answers, and I will do it in a room with the people of this town sitting in it.

"Two, and I put it to you, Mr Kest, and not to Miss Vey. I took a filing from you eleven years ago. It is not this one, and I would be obliged if the two were not spoken of in the same breath in a room with a keeper of records in it, because the first thing I was taught is that two documents about the same man are not the same document. You will be asked, in this room and in the provincial town and possibly by somebody with more than a clerk's ear, whether you have ever filed anything else out of that river. The answer is yes, and it is not this, and I am not going to discuss it here, and I am telling you that I am not going to discuss it here so that you are not surprised when I refuse to discuss it." She looked at him steadily. "That is not a threat. It is a courtesy, and it is the last one you will get from me this month."

"Two. This court will not entertain a claim that a road exists to Bellweather. I will not, and I will tell you why, and it is not cowardice, it is craft. A claim to a bridge is a claim about a bridge, and I can hear it, and I will hear it, and it will be in a book. A claim that a man can walk to a place the crown says is a fever pit is a claim about the crown's own finding, and if I hear that first, the first thing that happens is that the finding gets defended before the exhibit is read, and you will lose the exhibit to win the argument. So the exhibit goes in first, dated the thirtieth, and it is a seal and a date from YR 301 in a place that does not exist, and it is in the record of this court, and after that they have to answer it in a document, and a document is slower than a man and it lasts longer, which is the only advantage a court has over anybody."

She took the exhibit **beneath** his account, so that the first thing a reader sees is a seal and a date and not a man explaining himself, set the seizure hearing for the **fourth of Greenmonth**, and refused to hear the crossing first, on the ground that a claim the court cannot test would be answered by the finding rather than by the document, and that a document is slower than a man and lasts longer, which is the only advantage a court has over anybody. And she said the thing nobody in the room had said yet and said it to Mara and not to Aren, because a magistrate says things to the party who has to hear them: the exhibit is dated the thirtieth of Mudmonth, and the province renews its finding on the second of Greenmonth, and after the second every page in this file is a page under a finding that nothing survives, and the Archive's thirtieth of Mudmonth is therefore a good deal more use than it was worth eight days ago.

Mara Vey had gone very still.

"You are going to let the exhibit go in on its own," she said.

"I am going to let it go in on its own," said Lysa Fenn, "and you are going to hate me for it, and you are going to hate me for about six weeks, and then in about two years you are going to write to me and say that I was right, and I shall be very rude about it."

She took up a third paper, which was not in the file, and had come out of her own coat.

"This arrived at the town trust at the ninth hour yesterday morning," she said, "by the provincial post, and it is the second document I have seen today with that wax on it. It is addressed to the trust of Merefen. It concerns the *road-keeper of Merefen*, of whom it says that the office notes the keeper's attendance at the crossing of the nineteenth instant, and instructs the trust to provide the keeper's attendance at the provincial town on the ninth of Greenmonth, and to permit the removal of the Bellweather hinge from its present place of keeping to the provincial store, and it refers throughout to the Bellweather hinge as a thing *held in the keeper's charge*."

She put it on the table between them.

"Magistrate," said Mara Vey, "there is no keeper."

"There is no keeper," said Lysa Fenn. "There is no such office in the charter of this town, in the county's list of appointments, or in the crown's schedule of provincial offices, which I have had out of the record store this morning and read myself, because I did not believe it either, and I have been a magistrate for five years and I have never once been surprised by a person. I have been surprised twice this morning by a seal.

"I can find no authority, no commission, and no person to whom that word was given. Mr Kest has never held an office in his life except a brevet in a guard he was discharged from, and the word has been applied to him as though the office existed and had been filled, and it has been applied twice, on two papers, in one hand."

Mara Vey wrote a line in her day-book. Aren could see that she wrote it, and could not see what it said.

Then she stood up and went round to the press, and lifted the heavy bar off its seat with both hands to get at a folder, and her hands did a thing on the way.

It was a small thing. It was the movement of a woman who has lifted a hundred iron clamps out of a hundred presses, and the clamp she lifted had a warp in it where the bar had been twisted out of true, and her hands found the twist and took the bar straight, one-handed, without a tool and without stopping, and set it down.

Aren Kest watched her do it and felt the back of his own neck go cold.

That was the motion. Not the working — he knew as soon as his hands were still that it was not a working, there was no anchor in the room and no promise in it and no witness but three people who had all been talking about other things — it was the *motion*, the shape a hand makes when it sets a thing true against a thing that is out of true, and his hands had learned it on the third of a ferry slip in Bellweather over a split garboard with a hull-hand of eighteen and a woman named Sera Quen, and it had come back in him without asking and it had travelled nine hundred feet of open air and a crack in a bridge to come out of his fingers in a filing room over a salt merchant.

He had carried back a motion. That was all. It belonged to a block of granite set in a slip wall in a city four hundred feet over a river, and it wanted a stone and a narrow bound and somebody to say the terms aloud, and he had none of the three, and he could not do it again today, and there was nobody in this country he could have taught it to.

Mara Vey put the folder down and looked at him.

"You made a noise," she said, "like a man putting his weight wrong on a stair."

"Clamp's out of true."

"The clamp has been out of true since Longlight and I have been meaning to have it seen to." She wrote something else, briefly, and did not look up. "You watched my hands. I watched you watch them. We will both be extremely careful about that for some time, and I am going to be considerably less careful than you are, and I am telling you so in advance, because I find that I would rather be trusted with a known risk than managed, and I expect you find it the same way, and if you do not, this will be a very short and very dull arrangement."

"That," said Lysa Fenn, without looking up from the exhibit, "is a mend."

Both of them stopped.

"Narrowly done, on an object with nothing in it to hold, with no anchor in this room except a bar of iron and a bad clamp, and it worked." The magistrate put her pen in the inkstand. "I am a magistrate and I have watched a great many sworn tradesmen's accounts, and that is the first working I have ever seen performed in a filing room over a salt merchant, and I intend to record that I saw it and to record also that I do not know what I saw, and that is a legitimate entry in a book."

"It is not a working," Aren said. "That's the motion. That's a hand setting a thing true against a thing out of true, and I did it on a block of granite in a ferry slip four hundred feet over a river and it belonged to that stone and to a woman who made me say the terms out loud, and it does not travel, and I cannot do it again this afternoon, and there is nobody in this county I could teach it to."

"Then it is a motion," said the magistrate, "and motions are not evidence, and I have a file to finish."

She sat down.

"Now the account," she said. "You said you would sign all of it. I have ruled off a sheet. Do it in your own hand, and do it in this room, and do not go away and do it later, because a thing written later is a thing written by a man who has thought about it, and I want the one that was written by the man who had just come down a stair and could not get up the second one."

He wrote it on the sheet she ruled off.

It is four years since he wrote anything longer than a load table, and his hand went over the first line and had to be brought back.

*Sworn before Lysa Fenn, magistrate of Merefen, on the thirtieth of Mudmonth, YR 312, by Aren Kest, road warden of this town, formerly a captain in the Lantern Guard by brevet.*

*That on the nineteenth of Mudmonth, YR 301, I was sent to hold the Alder Vale crossing with an order that was not complete. That the order named a bridge, a district, and forty men. That it did not tell me the purpose of the operation, and that I did not ask, and that I have told myself for eleven years that I did not ask because nobody told me, and both of those are true and only one of them is a defence.*

*That I did not reach the inner city. That I held the outer crossing until the afternoon of the twentieth and was not relieved until the twenty-second. That a fever was in the city and I have learned this year that the fever was real and that the crown's account of the night is a true thing used to carry a false one.*

*That in Embermonth of that year, and in three years of debriefs after it, I gave the account that the fever had taken Bellweather, in those words or in words that were not different enough to matter. That I knew within a year that my own account did not account for the thing I had been sent to do, and that I had field notes, and that I kept the field notes in a coat, and that I did not hand them over. That I did not hand them over because challenging the inquiry would have cost me the only work I had after I was discharged, and I have given that reason to two men since and it is a true reason and I have never once offered it as the whole of it.*

*That I have named no man in this account. I do not know who gave the order. I have known for eleven years that there was an order, and I have not produced the knowledge, and there are men in this province who have known it longer and have said less, and I do not know their names and I am not going to guess at a page.*

*That two facts are here set down because they are mine and not anybody else's: that I gave a promise on the twelfth of this month with no date upon it, having already chosen the nineteenth; and that on the eleventh of this month I refused six marks and signed a bond instead, so that the bell would stand in my own name before the guild, and I have not told that reason to any living soul until this hour.*

*That everything above is true to the best of my knowledge and that where I am uncertain I have said so, and that if any part of it is proved false I will come back and say so in this court myself.*

He read it, and it was the worst thing he had ever put his name to, and it was the only thing he had ever written down that was for him.

He signed it, and Mara Vey witnessed it, and Lysa Fenn stamped it, and then the magistrate did a thing he did not expect, which was to take the sheet, and read it again, and put it at the bottom of the exhibit rather than the top.

"Your account goes under the document," she said. "Not over it. If it goes over it, the first thing a reader sees is a man explaining himself, and then a reader stops. If it goes under, the first thing a reader sees is a seal and a date from the year you were twenty-one, and then a reader has to find out why."

The number was on the table by then, because Mara Vey had put it there before he came in and he had not looked at it, and he looked at it now.

It was a single column, in a fair copy, and it came from the trust's own account of the third week of Mudmonth, and every line in it was a thing that had happened to somebody.

*The market of the twenty-fourth, lost; thirty-one stalls, pitch and stall-fee by custom* — 4 marks 2s.

*The market of the twenty-seventh, held late and thin; loss by custom* — 1 mark 1s 6d.

*Tolls not taken on the shut road, nine days* — 1 mark 6s.

*The Tuesday barge, held off two days; glass unsold; carriage on the wharf road* — 1 mark 2s.

*Cartage, stone, lime, oak and nails, for the scour repair* — 2 marks 3s 6d.

*Four hands, nine days, of which eleven days' pay fell to the repairer in the yard and not to the trust* — 1 shilling 4d.

And at the bottom, in the trust clerk's hand, a line with no figure against it at all: *eleven tons standing on the western bank, loss claimed by a factor, not yet assessed.*

"Eleven marks," said Mara.

"Eleven marks and four pence," said Lysa Fenn. "I had it copied this morning, because I thought a man who had been gone nine days ought to see what nine days had cost, and I am a magistrate and it is the only medicine I have. Eleven marks and four pence. It is a good deal of money in this town. I have had men transported for less."

"That is what nine days cost Merefen," said Aren.

"That is what nine days cost Merefen," said Mara, "and it is written in a column, and it is the most honest page in this office." She put her hand flat on it, the way he had put his hand flat on the stone, and he noticed her doing it and did not know what to do with the fact that she did it. "And you are not going to put that page beside that exhibit."

"No."

"Because the moment this office puts eleven marks and four pence on the same page as a seal from YR 301, it has made a place called the casualties of the Alder Vale, and everything in that place becomes one number, and the one number is the number the crown has been quoting for eleven years." She squared the sheet. "The city is on one page. The villages are on another page. Eleven marks and four pence is on a third page, in a trust's account book, where it belongs, and I will not have it in my file as an argument, because the day I use a market's takings to argue about the dead is the day this office stops being a record and becomes an argument, and an argument can be beaten, and a record cannot."

"Miss Vey," said Lysa Fenn, "that is the first sensible thing anybody has said in this room this morning, and I include myself, and I shall want it in a file of its own if you will allow me."

"You may have it in a file," said Mara. "It is not mine. Anybody may have it."

Aren Kest stood up, and found that the floor was where he thought it was, and that his legs were arguing with the arrangement but losing.

He was nearly at the door when the magistrate said, "Mr Kest."

"Ma'am."

"You have the look of a man who has written down that he can go somewhere," she said. "I have seen it on surveyors and on bailiffs. I will ask you one question, and I do not want an answer, and I will tell you why I am asking anyway. On the thirtieth of Mudmonth, YR 312, is there a road to Bellweather?"

"There is," said Aren Kest.

"I did not want an answer," said Lysa Fenn. "Thank you. It is now in the room and not in the book, and if I have to have it later I will ask you again in front of a clerk, and you will have to say it twice."

He went down the half-landing stair into a street that smelled of salt and river, and the wind was off the west channel and it was the third cold day of Mudmonth, and the whole of it was very clear to him: he had a mark in his hand, three days of fever behind him, a bridge he could not pay for, a brother who had told him no in a room with a door, and eleven marks and four pence in a column in a trust's account, and one thing that had come back in his fingers out of a slip in a city that was not in the world.

The Old Toll crossing was a mile up the road, and he walked it, because he had nine days of walking to make up and no other errand.

The cradle was empty. There was a clean rectangle in the moss on the third course of the abutment, paler than the stone round it, and there was a sheet of paper nailed to the post under a hood, and on the paper there was a disc of grey wax with a hand laid flat on a book.

He did not touch the seal.

He stood on the near abutment for a while in the cold and looked at the stone instead, the cut sill, the three lengths he had repacked in the autumn and again in the week before the storm, and the pale dry warm place in the middle of them where two lines crossed, the size of two hands, where the packing had been lifted and set down out of line by something that had struck the stone from the other side.

There was a message nailed inside the hut on the road, a slip of a foundryman's hand, and he read it standing up, and it was a price, and it was not cheap, and the line at the bottom of it was the only line in the world that could tell him when he would be able to use a road again.

*Two discs, Steppe glass, cut and annealed to a bridge pin. Four marks the pair, ready in a fortnight. Money before cutting.*

Four marks. He had a shilling a day, a bond for six against a chest worth three and a half, a weekly wage he was behind on, and a brother who had four marks and a bridge to finish, and a road that would not carry anything twice until that sill was mended with something no road warden in Merefen could afford.

He put the slip back in the hut and came away down the road, and the pale cross stayed in the stone where it was, and the bell stayed under a grey wax seal in a storehouse by the fish weir, and the weather came on cold out of the fen, and there was not one thing in the world he could do about any of it that afternoon except be in Merefen when it was needed, which is what he had always done, and which was going to have to be enough for a great many days.
