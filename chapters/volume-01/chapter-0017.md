# Chapter 0017

The fifth of Greenmonth was for cleaning up.

There is a market in Merefen on the fourth and a market on the ninth and the days between are for taking the pitch off the cobbles and getting the crates back to whoever brought them, and the fifth of Greenmonth was a Monday and it did that job and nothing else. The stalls came down at the fourth hour and the wet pitch came up in slabs and went into the bin by the fish weir, and nobody came to Weir Street all day who was not already coming, and no letter came up the road, and no new paper appeared on any board in this town, and the bell was in a storehouse by the weir under a grey wax seal with a hand laid flat on a book on it, and the town got on with the river.

That was the whole of the fifth. Tovan Kest noted it in his own way, which was to say nothing to anybody for a whole day and to think about it at the sixth hour while he was oiling a spindle, and what he thought about was this: nine days of being the wrong brother had cost the town eleven marks and four pence and had cost his own yard something he had not yet been able to put a figure on, and then on the fourth of Greenmonth a magistrate had come down from the provincial town and told six hundred people that the law had been on the town's side, and the town had gone home and shut up shop and carried on exactly as before, with one exception, which was that a great many people had begun to talk about his brother.

Not about the bell. About the brother.

"You'd be the one who'd know."

"Well, he was nine days gone and there's no market since."

"Your brother was the one that went."

That was the shape of it on Weir Street, and by the fifth of Greenmonth it had a sentence in it, and the sentence was: *he's the one that talks, and he's the one that was away.* Tovan heard the two halves joined up about nine times in a day and he got used to it the way a man gets used to a roof that leaks, which is to say badly and without any expectation that it will improve.

---

The work finished on the fifth, which was the thing a bridge carpenter dreads more than a flood.

A flood is a job. A scour hole is a job, and a job has a beginning and a middle and a man who can be paid at the end of it, and every man in a yard in Mudmonth would rather have the hole. What there is not, in Mudmonth, in any yard on the Sable, is *work finished*, because a finished repair is a repair that will be looked at for a long time, and the next one is a thing that happens in Longlight when the river drops and the stone comes off the western bank at summer prices and every man in the vale wants a bridgewright standing on his bank saying yes.

Tovan's yard, on the evening of the fifth, had four men and nothing for them to do.

That is not unemployment. Unemployment in Mudmonth is a hungry thing. It is a yard with men in it and no oak on the trestles, and the men go home at the fourth hour and come back at the sixth and are paid on Friday, and everything about it is fine for a week and then is not fine at all, because a man who is idle for nine days has spent his wages and a man who is idle for nine days has also decided something about the yard he is standing in.

He had the shear pin to fit. That was a day and a half, and it was his brother's, and the brother had done it for eight pence and a shilling, and Tovan had taken the iron. He had the wharf road's kerb to re-cut, which was the trust's and paid, and small. He had the Sedge Reach handrail on the town side, which was a month's-old complaint from a woman with a cart and which the town had not paid for because the town had nothing, and which he would do for nothing because his own crew worked under it every day and a handrail that comes off in a frost kills somebody.

That was the whole of the week. He was idle for four hours out of nine and he was aware of it in his shoulders.

His brother came in on the sixth at the seventh hour with a chalk stub and no work, and Tovan put him on the handrail, and said so.

"You'll take the town side. Eleven feet of it, two new stanchions, and you'll not touch the deck."

"I can do better than a handrail."

"You can do the handrail," said Tovan, "and if anybody in this yard says to you that you ought to be somewhere else, you'll tell them you're doing the town side handrail for nothing, and if they ask why, you'll tell them because my crew works under it. That's the whole of it. Don't write anything down."

"That isn't—"

"I know what it isn't." Tovan did not look up from the iron he was drawing. "Brother. I have said one thing to you in a room with a door and I am not going to say it again in a yard, because a yard is where it would be useful to you, and you have had enough useful things out of me. You're on the handrail."

They worked the handrail for two days, and they did not speak about anything except the handrail, and it was finished on the seventh at the second hour, and it was a good piece of work, and it was the last work in the yard for the week that Tovan did not have somebody else's name on.

---

The factor came on the sixth, at the eleventh hour, and came correctly.

He came to the yard, and not in front of the crew, and he said he had said he would do that and he had done it, and Tovan gave him a stool and did not apologise for the stool.

"I have taken your advice," the factor said, "and I have hired a hull, and I have taken two trips off the shoulder's south end while the shoulder is still a shoulder, and I have nine of my eleven tons on the towpath below the mill and the other two tons are a cartload of face stone that I am going to have to move in June, and I have lost a fortnight of margin that I will not get again this year." He put his hat on his knee. "So I am not here to be pleasant and I am not here to be a fool either. I am here because you have a crew of four and no oak on the trestles, and I have a fortnight's worth of face stone sitting in a field that nobody wants, and I would like it cut by the end of Greenmonth at a price that keeps both of us in our trades."

"Cutting is a month's work."

"It is for you with four men. It is a month's work for nobody else in this county, which is why I am sitting on a stool in a yard with a man who has nothing on his trestles." He took a purse out of his coat. "Four marks into your hands on the day, and the cutting to begin inside the month, and the balance on delivery at the mill. Four marks is not a fortune and it is a start, and I am putting it in now because the man who came to me with a true price and no date is a man I can put money in front of, and there is not a second one of you in this county."

Tovan Kest looked at the purse for a while.

He was aware, with a clarity that arrived about four seconds too late to be of any use, that he had four men on the payroll for the week and no work for them, that the trust owed his brother eleven days, that his brother had a bond standing for six marks against a tool-chest that the guild's clerk had priced at three and a half and half of which was rust, and that there were, in this room, four marks.

"Right," he said. "I'll have it in the chest and I'll have your name against the cutting and I'll start on the eighth, and the eighth is a Thursday and the trestles will be up by the fourth hour."

"I'll want it in writing."

"I'll have it in writing before you leave and Sarn at the foundry can witness it, she does my ink." He took the purse. "And I'll tell you the same thing I told your first man, because you are the second man to come to this yard and ask after my brother and the first one asked as though I were the wrong end of a thing, and I'd rather you heard it from me than worked it out. My brother is not in this yard's business. He is a road warden and he is a bridge repairer and he is a man with a bond on him and a shilling a day, and whatever he has said to you or anybody else about a bell is his and it is not in this yard and it does not get built here. If you want face stone cut, you are buying my crew. That's the whole of the transaction and I'd like it said out loud once so there's no misunderstanding in June."

The factor looked at him for a moment.

"Your brother," he said, "came down my bank on the first and told me the price of my own mistake and did not offer to carry a stone in his hand. And you have just told me that anything he says about a bell is not in your yard." He put his hat on. "Cut the stone, Master Kest. I will send a man to look at the first ten."

He left with the paper in his coat and Tovan stood in the middle of the yard with four marks in his chest and the smell of cold iron and oak shavings in his throat, and understood exactly what he had done, and could not have done it differently, and would think about the moment when he handed the purse back for about as long as he lived.

Because the purse was the crew's. Not in law — the crew were paid on Fridays and this was Tuesday, and the money was in the yard's chest and the yard's chest was the yard's. In fact, the money was the wages of four men for a week that was going to be paid on the Friday of the following week, and if the face-stone cutting went well and was paid on delivery, then the wages came out of the cutting and not out of anything else, and the balance of it was a fortnight of a young man's life spent on not being idle, and the man who cut it would be paid for it in June out of a factor's margin and not out of a bell.

And there would be nothing in the chest on Thursday morning except the other three and a quarter.

He went back to the iron and drew a line and did not think about the fourth hour of Thursday for two days, which was the longest he managed.

---

The clerk came on the sixth as well, and the clerk was the worst of it, because the clerk was polite.

The guild's clerk had been twice since the twenty-eighth and had stopped coming after the twenty-eighth, and Tovan had put that down to a clerk with other work. It was not that. On the sixth of Greenmonth the clerk came at the fourth hour, when the crew were packing up, and asked whether he could have two words in the office, and there is no office, so they stood in the doorway with the door open.

"Two things," said the clerk. "The first is that the bond of the eleventh is still on the roll, six marks, surety a tool-chest which the roll has at three and a half, and the bench sits on the ninth, and the bench is going to ask what is proposed."

"Tell the bench there is nothing on the bridge and there's a Crown seal on the post, and the bench can ask the Crown."

"The bench is going to ask a road warden, Master Kest, not a Crown. There is a difference and the bench knows it." The clerk put his ledger under his arm. "The second thing is that I have been asked, at the guild, to ask you, as the brother and a man on this roll, what your brother means to do."

"About what?"

"About the bell," said the clerk, in a voice that had clearly been told to be gentle, and was not managing it, and which was the most honest thing in the yard all week. "There is a bond on the roll with a surety worth three and a half against six, and the object the bond was given over is in a Crown storehouse, and there is a man in this town who is the only one anybody has ever heard of saying where it came from. I am not asking you to answer for him. I am asking you, as a man on this roll, to tell the bench whether he is a man who intends to keep doing this, so that the bench can decide what it is voting on in three days' time."

And Tovan Kest stood in the doorway of his own yard with a guild clerk in front of him and four men walking off up Weir Street behind him, and felt the whole of the last four weeks come to a head in a place where it could not be got at.

"Two answers and then you can write whichever of them you like," he said. "One. I do not know what my brother means to do. I have not asked him and I am not going to ask him, and if the bench wants to know what a road warden means to do it can put the question in a form that is a question and not an opinion, and he can answer it in his own hand, and I will witness it if he asks me in front of a person, which he will not, because he will not ask, because asking is the whole of what he has never once done in his life."

"And the second?"

"The second is that I will tell you what I know, because you have been decent about this and because you have a ledger and a bench behind you and neither of them is a person, and a person ought to be told. I know that a bell went out of this town on the nineteenth at first light on a Crown paper, and I know the paper was dated three days before and came into Rell's hands on the night before, and I know that on the fourth of Greenmonth a magistrate from the provincial town sat in a room over a salt merchant and said out loud that it was three days, and I know the whole of Weir Street heard it, and I know that the town has been told in chalk that the law was on its side, and I know that my brother was on the twenty-seventh on a bridge in a fever telling four hundred people that a city is standing where this kingdom's finding says there is a fever pit, and I know that he was wet through and grey and had nine days of nothing in him and he said it twice, because the watch sergeant had a book."

He stopped.

"Does the guild," he said, "want to know whether my brother is a fool?"

The clerk closed his book.

"No," he said. "The guild wants to know whether the bond is a risk to the roll. That is all the guild wants. Everything else you have just said to me is nothing to do with the guild and I have heard it and I am not going to write it down, because I am a clerk and I have a roll and the roll has one column on it and it is money."

"Then write this in the one column," said Tovan. "Four marks in the chest on the seventh, a cutting contract for face stone to start on the eighth, and a crew of four who will be idle between now and Longlight. And the bond is a risk to the roll, and it is a risk I would stand, and you can put that down as the brother and a man on the roll, and let the bench vote on it on the ninth."

---

The man who came to the yard on the seventh of Greenmonth came at the ninth hour and did not come to a door.

He came down Weir Street with a pole over his shoulder and a rope round his neck, and he was fifty-two and had the shoulders of a man who has poled a flat-bottomed hull for a working life, and he was stopping at every yard on that street, which is what a man does when he is looking for work and has decided to be seen looking.

"Master Kest."

"Tovan."

"I know who you are and I know who your brother is, and I have come from the north end and I have walked the length of this street to be seen walking it, and I would rather tell you why I have come than have you ask me." He put the pole down against the wall, which nobody does in a yard. "My name is Pei Tarrow. I work freight on the Old Toll channel and I have worked it eleven years and I will work it next year if the channel holds. My mother was of Hushwater. She was a Tarrow and her mother was a Tarrow and she came down the channel in her own boat as a girl and she is alive and she is sixty-eight and she is four miles from this town at this moment and she has asked me to stop coming to Merefen because coming to Merefen is what got her noticed in a town that had not noticed her in eleven years."

"I didn't send for you."

"You did not, and I would have come anyway, and here is the whole of it in three parts and then I will let you go back to your work." He took his hat off, and put it back on. "One. Everybody in this town is now told that there is a woman in Merefen called Rusk who is the widow of a crossing, and I have been in three rooms this month and twice I have been asked whether I am her husband's brother, and I am not, and I have said so, and it is not interesting enough for a room, so they have stopped asking, and they have not started asking the other question, and they will. One of these days a man in this town is going to work out that a Tarrow was a Hushwater woman, and he is going to be very pleased with himself about it, and he is going to come and find me, and I have decided in advance that the answer is no and that I would rather give it standing in your yard than be asked for it in a taproom by a man with a good heart and a bad memory."

"Take the stool," said Tovan Kest.

"I'll stand, if it's the same, I'm not here to be comfortable."

"Take the stool, Pei Tarrow, because the man who has to stand up in a yard is the man who is about to be told no, and I have a stool and nobody's using it." He put it down. "Two. There is no freight on the Old Toll channel. There has been no freight on the channel since the nineteenth of Mudmonth, because everything that came up the road stopped coming, and the stuff that was on the far bank went down by water, and I have eleven years of a channel and one crossing season and no work, and I have had three-quarters of what I made last year, because the channel is a season's work and it is half over. Three parts, then, and this is the one that matters and I will say it once. I am not a symbol. I have been one for eleven years without being asked, which is worse. There is nothing in Merefen that I can do for anybody's case. I have no book and no list and no dead. I have a mother at four miles who was not in the village's book because she left it at nine years old, and eleven years of freight on a channel that is not my family's village, and I would like the freight."

"You are not going to be anybody's anything in this yard," said Tovan. "You will be a man in this yard on a Friday, and I will pay you a rate, and the rate is the rate and it is the same as the rate the other three are on and I am not telling you it and I am not hiding it."

"What's the rate?"

"Eight pence a day and cartage at the yard price, and the other three are on the same, and there is a Friday, and there is the ninth of Greenmonth market day and every man in this yard gets the same day off with half a day's pay, and I have never in my life paid a man out of the yard's money for a favour and I am not starting with you."

Pei Tarrow looked at the stool, and then at the trestles, which were empty, and then at the four men coming up Weir Street at the fourth hour for the afternoon, and he said, "I'll take it, then, and I understand what it is and I understand what it isn't, and I have been told what it isn't twice this week by men who were apologising and I would rather be told by a man who isn't."

"Two things and then you'll be on the trestles," said Tovan, and he said them because they were the terms and because he was his father's son. "One: you will not be asked about Hushwater in this yard, and if anybody in this yard asks you, you take it to me and I will put them on a piece of oak and they'll watch me do it. Two: you will not answer for my brother either, in either direction. He has not asked you for anything, he does not know you are here, and I would like it to be in a book somewhere that the two of you have not spoken, so that nobody in this town can say that the road warden's case has a Tarrow in it. If somebody comes to you about him, you send them to me. That's the whole of what I have to give you and it is not nothing."

"Right," said Pei Tarrow, and picked up the pole.

That was how it happened, and it took eleven minutes, and there is no version of it in which anybody said one word about a bell, or a fever, or a city, or a road, or a boy of twenty-one on a bridge in a storm, and afterwards Tovan Kest stood in his own yard in Weir Street and understood something about himself that he had not been carrying round before.

His whole life, since he was ten, had been organised around a single question: what is my brother going to do next, and how do I keep the yard out of it. He had built the yard as a place that was not that. He had refused the bond's asking, the road, the span, the secret, the statement, the cover, the little jobs after dark, the errand to the abbey. He had said no to a series of offers, most of which had been made in a fever and all of which had been made in love, and he had a great many ways of describing that refusal and none of them had ever got him any further than the refusal itself.

What he had found out, at the ninth hour on the seventh of Greenmonth, standing in a yard with a man from Hushwater at the barrow's end of the trestles, was that the yard was already that place. It had been that place since the sixth of Mudmonth, when he had finished a scour repair at a loss, and again on the twenty-seventh, and again for three weeks after that, whenever a man in a market said *you'd have to ask Tovan Kest* and meant it kindly. The refusal had not been the thing. The refusal had been a gate he put in front of his brother. The gate was a real gate and he would keep it and he was glad of it, and what it was for, as of this morning, was to be the only place in the whole of this town where a man could come and be paid for a day's work without anybody in the building asking him what he thought about a bell.

That is a smaller thing than a road and it is worth more than one, and it took him thirty years to be old enough to be told it by a man he had known for eleven minutes, and he was not going to be told anything else about it that day.

---

The boy came for him at the ninth hour on the seventh, which was odd, because the boy was a Sedge Reach deck boy and the deck had nothing in it that wanted Tovan Kest.

"There's a noise."

"Off the wharf?"

"Off the far side. Down toward the old crossing." The boy was thirteen and had been a tower boy and had come down to the deck because the tower job was only six days a week and this was the seventh. "Two of the fellers on the deck heard it and then I heard it, and the water was dead flat and there was no wind at all because it's the one day in a month with no wind in it, and it went over the far bank. Like a bell."

"Whose bell."

"Like *the* bell," said the boy, with the enormous confidence of a thirteen-year-old. "Like the old one off the Old Toll, the one they took. It wasn't loud. It was like when it's cracked and it's a bad note, about three seconds. And there was nobody on the road and I walked up to the weir and there was nobody by the storehouse and the watch was on the mill chimney and nobody else was out."

Tovan Kest put down the chisel.

There is no bell on this reach. That is not an opinion. There is a bell in a storehouse by the fish weir, under a seal, and it has been there since the nineteenth of Mudmonth, and nobody in this town has seen it since the morning a cart took it off the abutment, and the cradle on the near abutment has been empty for a fortnight and the moss has started. There has been no bell on this reach since the seventeenth of Mudmonth, when a carter's man went under off the reed beds and the ferrymen rang for him on the old crack, and that was the last time it was rung, and eleven people heard it from the slip and nobody has rung anything since because there is nothing left to ring.

"Two of the fellers," Tovan said. "Names."

"Rennick and the big one, the hired man for the week's cartage."

"Did they see a man?"

"They saw a bridge."

"There's three bridges in a mile," said Tovan. "Did they see which one."

The boy thought about it properly, which nobody gives a boy credit for.

"The old one," he said. "The far end of it. The bit with the thing bolted on the side, where the bell used to be. And it came off the bridge, not off the water. Off the bridge, going up the road to the north, the way you'd hear a thing that's still in the town." He shifted. "And then after, about a minute after, somebody at the mill said it was nothing, because the chimney's iron, and Rennick said it wasn't the chimney. And that's all. I came to tell you because you're the one people ask."

"Aye," said Tovan Kest. "I am."

He wrote it on the back of his hand where the tally lived, under the four marks and the stone and the lime, and then he did something he had not done for three days and did not know he was going to do, which was to go up the Old Toll road with the boy and stand on the near abutment of that crossing in the last of the light and look at a piece of iron with nothing on it.

The cradle was there. The iron was rusted into the stone where a fitter had welded it eleven years ago, and it had been an empty cradle for a fortnight, and under it the stone was swept and the moss was green and starting over the corner of the clean rectangle.

The seal on the post was grey and had been rained on and had not run.

Tovan Kest stood in the middle of the Old Toll crossing at the ninth hour of the seventh of Greenmonth, with a bar over his shoulder out of pure habit, and there was nothing on the bridge, and there was nothing in the air, and the river went under three arches in the cold going down and the town behind him was doing a Tuesday.

"Master Kest?" the boy said. "Are you going to do something about it?"

"No," said Tovan. "There's a Crown seal on that post and a magistrate's finding in the provincial town and I have four men out of work and a cutting to start on Tuesday, and there is nothing in this town that is anybody's to do anything about this week." He shouldered the bar. "I'll tell you what I'll do. If you hear it again, you go to the tower and you write down the hour and which way the wind was and whether there was a cloud over the sun, because the tower's got a line down the old raised road as far as the weir and a man can look down that line from the top of it, and if anybody ever wants to know whether it was a bell or a chimney, that is the only record that will be worth a farthing."

"That's not doing something about it."

"No," Tovan agreed. "That's a boy on a deck writing down the hour. It's a very small thing and it's the only thing in this town that I can stand behind, and I have had a very hard month about what it is that a man can stand behind."

He walked back down Weir Street with the boy and put four marks in the chest against the third man's name and told the three who were there that the cutting started on the eighth at the fourth hour, and got a cheer out of the yard that was not entirely a cheer, because a yard that has work in it is a yard that has a reason to be there and a yard without is not.

Down at the north end of the town, on the last of the light, a man of fifty-two with a pole over his shoulder and a rope round his neck stopped on a stair outside a chandler's and looked at a town that had decided, in four weeks, that everything about him was a thing somebody else had already decided.

He had been asked twice whether he was a Rusk's brother and he had said no twice, and it was going to be a longer sentence than that by the end of the month, and the reason he had walked the length of Weir Street that morning instead of coming to the north end first was that he had wanted to be seen doing it, and the reason he had wanted to be seen doing it was that a man who is seen choosing where to stand is a man who has decided something.

He had a job. It was eight pence a day and the same day off as the other three and there was no sentence in it about anything, and on Friday he would be paid, and on Friday he would take the money and go four miles up the channel to a house where a woman of sixty-eight was sitting by a window who had spent eleven years being not noticed.

That was the whole of what Pei Tarrow wanted out of the year, and he had asked for it in a yard in the plainest words a man could use, and had been told what it was not, twice, in front of witnesses, and had been paid for his first day on the Thursday, in coin, on a rail, which was three days earlier than Friday and which he was not going to forget and was not going to mention.
