# Chapter 0592

**About nine hundred drying cloths on about nine hundred feet of line, the ninth hour of the bar sitting, a barrow run of flat ground, fruit about six weeks off being picked, and two women who disagree about a cord and a line and are both right.**

The only flat ground within four hundred paces of the fourth hundred pace was the strip at the middle of the Long Set, about nine hundred feet long, and it carried about nine hundred drying cloths on lines, and the barrows needed it to run on, and the cloths had been down once before and about two in three had come out dry and nobody had counted the two in three, and they were up again because fruit about six weeks off being picked waits for nobody.

The woman of about fifty-eight who turns those cloths stood at the near end with her hands on a line, and the woman of about sixty-four who ties about nine hundred picking cords in a sitting stood at the far end with a bundle under her arm, and between them the lines sagged with damp weight, and about nine men waited forty paces off with empty barrows, and nobody was angry.

“**They come down,**” the woman of about fifty-eight said, “**or the barrows go round by the high side, which is half a day lost on every day.**”

“**They do not come down,**” the woman of about sixty-four said. “**The cords are tied to the same posts and the posts will not hold a barrow run shaking them twice a day. Move the run to the low edge and lose ten minutes and keep the lines.**”

“**The low edge is soft,**” the first said. “**A barrow goes in to the axle there after rain.**”

“**Then lay boards,**” the second said. “**There are boards at the press-house doing nothing.**”

“**Boards sink too,**” the first said, “**and then we have boards under mud and cloths in mud, which is worse than one of those.**”

It was ordinary on the surface, which was fruit and cords and cloths and a barrow and the ninth hour, and it was small and specific, and they were both right, and that was the point the valley had forgotten how to hold. The woman of about fifty-eight was right that the run needed the flat and the flat was under the cloths. The woman of about sixty-four was right that the posts would not stand it and the cords would go with them. About four people standing by took it that the cloths should come down because stone outranks cloth, and about five took it that the lines should stand because a mend that eats its own drying eats next year too, and the two sets did not overlap and neither woman was asked which of the two she thought she was.

The man of about sixty of the Standing stood at the edge of the forty paces and listened, and the rule he had made in about four minutes was the reason the two women could stand there and disagree at all, and he had said out loud that it was not a good one, and the valley itself was the rest of the resistance, because it had been built to prevent arguments and it did its work by making every disagreement into a thing nobody wrote down.

The woman of about thirty-eight who keeps a block between two head stones came down the middle with a basket and stopped, and she did not take either side, and the oldest voice in that valley did not come out of her, and that was noticed by everybody and said by nobody.

“**How many cloths,**” she asked, “**and how many cords on the same posts?**”

“**About nine hundred cloths,**” the woman of about fifty-eight said. “**And about nine hundred cords tied along the same run, of which she ties about a hundred and forty.**”

“**Then you are arguing about nine hundred feet of posts,**” the woman of about thirty-eight said, “**and you are both right about them, and the valley has no way to write down that two people are right about one thing and still have to choose.**”

“**We had a way,**” the woman of about sixty-four said. “**We put it in the ground twenty-two winters ago.**”

Nobody answered that, and nobody was angry, and that combination was the finding of the hour and not a softening of it. The people who argued hardest on that strip were not the people who had been wrong about the water. The two sets that had said no to the money were not on that ground at all. The argument was between two women who had both said yes in their own way and had both paid, and they argued about cords and lines because those were the things in front of them, and they argued well.

The sun stood over the lines, and the cloths shifted in the air coming up the slope, and the smell of damp cloth mixed with the smell of fruit coming ripe, and the barrow men sat on the grass and waited without complaint, because waiting is much of the work on a ground like that and they knew it. The woman of about thirty-eight set her basket down and walked the run from end to end, feeling the posts with her palm where the cords bit in, and the wood was sound, and the sag was honest wear, and she said so out loud so both women heard it, and neither took it as taking sides, because it was not.

In the end the cloths on the middle three lines came down and the cords stayed on the posts, and the barrows ran between, and about two in three of the cloths that came down were dry by evening and nobody counted them, and the woman of about fifty-eight wrote on her own slate that she had not got the number, and the woman of about sixty-four tied her cords round the shaking posts and lost three of them and tied them again, and the posts kept, and the lines kept, and the run kept, and all of that holding was ordinary work and nobody named it.

The man of about sixty was told at the ninth hour what had been decided, and he was asked whether he had been asked and by whom and whether it was right, and he answered about the asking and would not answer about the right, and said there was no room to put it in, and told them not to write it down, and walked back up the slope alone.

**What that hour cost is a column, and it is the woman's who turns the cloths, and cords and lines and a disagreement nobody wrote down are not in it and none of them has a price.**

**a hoof pick at fifteen, a cake of soap at two, a ladder rung at twenty-seven, a hank of yarn at seven, a water scoop at twenty-two, a rushlight at four, a cart rung at thirty, a jar of oil at ten, a hand file at eighteen, and a whetstone at thirteen.** Fifteen, two, twenty-seven, seven, twenty-two, four, thirty, ten, eighteen and thirteen is one hundred and forty-eight pence. **A mark is forty-eight pence and one hundred and forty-eight pence is three marks and four over**, and about nine hundred cloths and about nine hundred feet of line and about nine hundred cords and about a hundred and forty of them hers and four hundred paces and forty paces and the middle three lines and three posts lost and about two in three come out dry are nine figures of distance and of count and not one of them is a price, and the ninth hour and the bar sitting and about six weeks and twenty-two winters and half a day lost and twice a day and ten minutes are seven figures of time and not one of them goes in at either end of it. **A woman of about fifty-eight who turns the cloths and a woman of about sixty-four who ties the cords and about nine men with barrows and a woman of about thirty-eight who keeps a block and a man of about sixty of the Standing and about four standing by and about five standing by are seven figures of persons and are not added to one another.** Nobody was thanked and nobody was angry.
