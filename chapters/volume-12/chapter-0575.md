# Chapter 0575

**The oldest tree in the Long Set, the eighth hour of the blossom sitting, about eleven people tying cords, a spade, a barrow, a plank about two hands long that has been in the ground for twenty-two winters, and a woman of about sixty-four who arrived at her usual hour and found nine people at her tree.**

They did it at the eighth hour because the eighth hour is the hour the cords are done, and it was not called a sitting, and there was no room, and nobody wrote anything who did not have to write something for a living.

The four of them who hold ground inside nine yards of that tree dug it, because the man of about sixty had said in a room that they would, and because a rule that nobody likes is still the rule that is there when the work gets done. The man of about fifty-one held the spade and did not dig, and the woman of about thirty-eight dug, and she has said out loud that she did not expect to and that she has not decided yet whether that is a thing she is glad about.

Nobody thanked anybody.

There is a flat stone about the size of a door mat under the oldest tree and it had been down long enough for about nine inches of loam to have grown over it, and when it came up it came up whole, and under it was a plank of green wood that had gone hard and black, about two hands long, and the marks were cut into it and not written on it and not painted.

Nobody spoke for about as long as a man takes to say nine.

**And then four people on that slope said four different things about it at once, and none of the four was right, and the two of them who could read it are the two people who are not going to be able to take it back.**

The woman of about thirty-eight could read the first half and the man of about fifty-one could read the second, and neither of them would read the other's half, and they read it out between them about four times before they stopped.

What the plank says, in the marks, is this. A strip of ground at the foot of the Long Set asked to be let go. About ninety people went out on it. Four sets of them argued about it for nine days. **They were right that it would have come to a quarrel that none of them could have stopped, and there is no mark in that plank that says they were right, and the man of about fifty-one said it out loud as his own finding afterwards and not as a reading.**

Then the second half, which is the remedy, and it is one mark repeated and then a line, and both of them had to agree what the marks mean before anybody could say it out loud, and they agreed and they were not certain.

Nobody thanked them for agreeing.

“**No argument about a set would ever be written down again,**” the man of about fifty-one said.

And the man of about sixty of the Standing said the sentence that the whole of this sitting turned on, and he said it standing about nine paces off with his hands behind him, and it was not angry and it was not clever and it is the truest thing anybody said on that slope.

“**Then there has been an argument about a set written down in this valley for twenty-two winters, and it has been under a tree, and there are about nine people standing round a hole looking at it.**”

Nobody argued with him. The man of about thirty-one of the Standing said that a record kept in the ground was a record nobody could be shown, which was the point, and the man of about sixty said yes, and that it was also the whole of the difficulty, and that those two things had been the same act for twenty-two winters and had never once been said out loud in the same breath.

Nobody thanked him.

Then a woman of about sixty-four came up under the tree at about the eighth hour, because the eighth hour is the eighth hour and about nine hundred cords in a sitting do not wait for a spade, and she came round the north end of the arc of stones where the bigger ones are, and she stopped.

**And she said the number out loud. That is what she has done at the eighth hour for thirty years, and she was not going to do it differently on the morning there were nine people and a hole at her tree, and the number she said is the number cut into the top of the plank, and she was the first person in thirty years to say it correctly, because for thirty years she had been saying it out of a slate in her apron and there had been nothing at all to say it to.**

Nobody thanked her. Nobody told her that she had said it correctly. She stood at the north end of the arc with her hand on the stone she had been calling nine for three decades and she said the rest of it in the flattest voice anybody on that slope had ever heard.

“**The woman of about seventy cut that number into the wood twenty-two winters ago and she told me the same number in my first year and she told me I was to say it and not let anybody count for themselves, and she told me she was given it and she was copying it. That is all either of us ever did. There was never a count. I have been a copy walking round a place for thirty years and I have been good at it.**”

Nobody thanked her and nobody argued and nobody asked her a question about it, and about nine people on that slope heard the whole of it and about nine were carrying cords and did not, and nobody in that valley has asked her since to say it again.

Then the man of about fifty-one read the other half of the plank, which is the half neither of the first two readers wanted to read, and it is not in the same hand and it is not a list and it has no reason attached to it anywhere on the wood.

“**Water. Out at the foot of a wall and in again at the head of it, on the day the strip was let go. Whoever cut this did not write why, and there is no mark on this plank that says the two things were done together, and I am not going to say they were done together unless somebody puts it in front of me.**”

Nobody put anything in front of him.

**And that is the whole of what a man of thirty-two with a limp could have said about it if he had been there, and he was not on that slope and was somewhere between a lane and a made road with nobody able to say which, and nobody in nine counties has been told where he is, and the man of about fifty-one has not been asked him anything because he is not here.**

Then the two halves were said out loud one more time, and this time it was not read.

The man of about fifty-one said the first half and the woman of about thirty-eight said the second, and neither of them said the other's, and nobody in that valley has said since which of the two was right.

“**They were right,**” the man of about fifty-one said, about the ninety people and the nine days. “**Four sets of people arguing about a strip of ground with a river at the bottom of it comes to a quarrel in about nine days and nobody stops it. They were right about us.**”

“**They were right about us,**” the woman of about thirty-eight said, “**and they were wrong about the ground. The remedy was not to stop being able to have the argument. The remedy was to put the argument in the ground and take the paper away, and a thing in the ground is the same as a thing that never happened, and they knew that, and they did it anyway, and I was about sixteen and I said yes.**”

Nobody argued with her. Nobody told her she had been about sixteen and had said yes and had not gone, because everybody on that slope already knew it and it had been said out loud in a market town four days' walk off to about four people near a fish board and it is on nobody's sheet.

Then she said the last thing of the morning and it was the whole of what the morning was for.

“**I would like somebody to say which of those two things is the mistake.**”

Nobody said it. About nine people were on that slope. Nobody thanked anybody.

And the man of about fifty-one put the plank down on the grass, flat side up, in the light, where anybody could see it, and said out loud that it was not going back in the ground, and he said it to the woman of about thirty-eight and not to the room, and she did not argue with him about that and neither of them pretended it was a decision they had made together.

**And the cost of that sitting is a column and it is the man of about fifty-one's, and a plank about two hands long that has been in the ground for twenty-two winters is not in it and it cannot go back and it has no price.**

**a lantern at four, a mattock at nine, a rope at thirteen, a tarpaulin at sixteen, a barrow at twenty, a bottle at twenty-four, a spade at twenty-seven, a second barrow at twenty-eight, a pick at thirty-one, and a candle-end at thirty-three.** Four, nine, thirteen, sixteen, twenty, twenty-four, twenty-seven, twenty-eight, thirty-one and thirty-three is two hundred and five pence. **A mark is forty-eight pence and two hundred and five pence is four marks and thirteen over**, and ninety people who went out on a strip of ground twenty-two winters ago, nine days of arguing, nine people on a slope, the fourth hundred pace of four miles of stone, and nine yards are five figures of five different things, not one of which is money, and every one of them is at neither end of this column. **A plank with marks cut into it is not a form and is not a head and is not a division and is not a leaf about nine inches by six, and the fourth head of the Crown's form is four counties off and ruled and empty and about six months empty, and no office and no person has filled it and nothing in this valley can.**

The hole was filled in at about the ninth hour with the loam that came out of it and the flat stone went back on top of the loam and not on top of the plank, and the plank stayed on the grass in the light where anybody could walk round it and read it and nobody read it back out loud, and it is still there, and nobody in that valley has said a word about where it ought to be kept, and the record is open and cannot be shut.
