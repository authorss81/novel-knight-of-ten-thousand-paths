# Chapter 0591

**Fallowmonth the twenty-first, YR 320, a Friday, a working morning on the fourth hundred pace, the second hour of the bar sitting, about nine men on a bank, a slate with three names on it, a boy of about fifteen with chalk, and a question nobody has put in about nineteen years.**

The bar sitting had come in with the turn of the five and nothing had been tidied, and four miles of stone went down that slope with the fourth hundred pace lying under a thin mist with about four hundred paces of good new bed holding at the far end of it, and the top three hundred paces wrong above, and about a hundred paces between the three hundred and the four hundred untouched and growing green at the edges.

About nine men stood on the low side with barrows, and the man of about fifty-one who keeps the water stood on the high side with the slate under his arm, and the woman of about thirty-eight who keeps a block between two head stones stood at the far end of about nine yards of lip, and the boy of about fifteen stood between them with the chalk in his hand, and Aren Kest stood four paces below with a barrow he had been given at the second hour and no place yet to empty it.

The finding lay on a stone where the three had left it, with three names on it in chalk, and nobody had been asked to receive it, and the four sets had found the money between them and the money was spent, and a ditch about two paces wide on the top of the fourth terrace was being cut by one person while the rest cut stone, and about nine hundred drying cloths hung on the middle ground of which about two in three had come out dry and nobody had counted the two in three, and the bar of wood lay in the grass at the foot of the Low Lane and had never been lifted and nobody had asked about it. Nine days and a morning stood counted on a slate in the valley, put down at the water's pace by a woman with a stick and about nine stones, and the morning is on the slate because she asked that it be written.

The man of about fifty-one looked down the bank at the work and then at the slate, and the line of rubble about two hands thick under the lip was covered now under about four inches of new course, and the chalk line across the lip could not be seen from the top, and he said what the morning was for, which was stone and not talk.

“**We lay from where we stand,**” he said. “**The lip carries and the bed holds, and the three hundred above will wait for a year nobody has arranged for.**”

Nobody answered that, because there was nothing in it to argue with, and the boy of about fifteen turned the slate round in his hands, and the three names on it were his and the man's and the woman's, and he looked at them the way a person looks at a thing he has agreed to carry.

Then the boy asked the question, and he asked it out loud, and he asked it of nobody in particular, and that was the turn the whole morning took.

“**Who here is allowed to say no,**” he said.

It was not a question about water. It was not about the lip or the bed or the three hundred paces or the hundred. It was a question about the valley, and nobody in that valley had put it in about nineteen years, and the bank went quiet in a way that had nothing to do with work stopping, because the work did not stop.

The man of about forty-four who holds the second hoe rested his hands on his barrow and looked at the boy, and about nine men on that bank looked at each other and then looked away, and the man of about fifty-one did not answer at once, which was itself an answer of a kind, and Aren Kest set his barrow down and said nothing, because the question had not been put to him and he had not earned a place in it.

The woman of about thirty-eight heard it differently from the rest, and that was the thing only she could do, because she had asked twice to be told she was wrong and had been told nothing, and she knew the shape of a question being asked and the shape of a question being allowed, and they are not the same shape.

“**Say it again,**” she said to the boy, “**and say who you are asking.**”

“**I am asking everybody,**” the boy said. “**The slate has three names and I am one of them, and I was told the bell is a penny and this is nothing, and I said I would do it for nothing. If one of the three says no tomorrow, does the finding fall, or does it stand with two?**”

“**It stands with three or it does not stand,**” the man of about fifty-one said. “**That is what a finding is.**”

“**That is what it is,**” the boy said. “**It is not what I asked. I asked who is allowed to say no to the asking, before there is anything to stand or fall. Four holders were asked about a hole and all four said she would rather not and it made no difference. Four sets were asked about money and two said no and the money was found without them. So who is allowed to say no here?**”

Nobody thanked him for asking. Nobody told him he should not have asked. About four of the men on that bank took the view that a boy who carries chalk has asked above his work, and about five took it that he had asked the only thing worth asking on a morning like this, and the two sets did not overlap and he was not asked which of the two he thought he was.

The man of about sixty of the Standing had come up the bank halfway and stopped, and he heard the question from where he stood, and he was the man who had made the rule in about four minutes that let four holders refuse without stopping anything, and he had said out loud that it was not a good rule, and he said nothing now, because to answer would have been to allow the question, and to refuse to answer would have been to allow it too.

The woman of about thirty-eight stepped down off the lip and stood beside the boy, and she did not touch him, and her voice when it came was her own and not the oldest voice in that valley, and everybody on that bank could hear the difference, because they had all heard the other one at tables since they were small.

“**You asked it,**” she said. “**That is the whole of it and it is not small. Whether anybody answers is a different thing, and nobody here is going to give you the right to have asked. A right to refuse is not a thing anybody in this valley has been given, and until somebody is given it, every yes here is only the sound a place makes when it cannot say no.**”

The boy looked at her, and then at the slate, and then at the three names, and he did not rub anything out, and he did not add anything, and the chalk stayed in his hand.

“**Then I will keep carrying it,**” he said, “**and I will remember that I asked.**”

The man of about fifty-one picked up the slate and put it back on the stone, and the morning went back to stone, and the barrows moved, and Aren Kest lifted his barrow and carried it down the low side to the fan and emptied it where he was shown, and the man who had shown him did not watch him and did not tell him he had done it well.

At the ninth hour the bank broke for bread, and the question was still standing where the boy had left it, and nobody had tidied it, and the valley had been asked a thing it had not been asked in about nineteen years, and the asking had been heard by the one person who could hear what it was.

The mist had burned off by then, and the new bed lay pale at the far end, and the untouched green between showed where hands had not been, and the boy sat on the grass with the slate across his knees and ran his thumb along its edge without marking it. He was not proud of the question and not afraid of it, and that steadiness in him was what kept the morning together after the words were out. The woman stayed near him without speaking, shelling a fallen fruit with her nail and eating it in small bites, and the taste was sour because the pick was still weeks off, and she ate it all the same because it was off her own ground.

Aren ate with the men on the low side, sitting with his bad leg out straight, and the bread was coarse and filling, and nobody made room for him and nobody moved him along, and that plain treatment was easier to carry than kindness would have been. He thought about the fan uncut further down and about the weight of spoil, and about how a bank holds until it does not, and he kept those thoughts where they belonged, which was inside his own head and behind his teeth. The man who keeps the water ate standing up, looking at the sky for rain that was not coming, and the man with the second hoe mended a strap on his barrow with a strip of cloth, and the work waited for them all without impatience, the way work does when it knows it will be done.

**What that morning cost is a column, and it is the boy's, and a slate and three names and a question nobody answered are not in it and none of them has a price.**

**a bar of soap at eight, a coil of twine at four, a pail at twenty-four, a jar of oil at thirteen, a tallow dip at six, a shoulder strap at twenty-six, a horseshoe nail at nineteen, a cake of ink at eleven, a riddle at twenty-one, and a curry brush at sixteen.** Eight, four, twenty-four, thirteen, six, twenty-six, nineteen, eleven, twenty-one and sixteen is one hundred and forty-eight pence. **A mark is forty-eight pence and one hundred and forty-eight pence is three marks and four over**, and four miles of stone and the fourth hundred pace and about nine yards of lip and about two hands of rubble and about four inches of new course and four paces of bank and a ditch about two paces wide and the fourth terrace and three hundred paces wrong and about a hundred paces untouched and about four hundred paces of good new bed and about nine hundred cloths and about two in three come out dry and about nine stones with a stick are fourteen figures of distance and of count and not one of them is a price, and the second hour and the ninth hour and the bar sitting and about four minutes of a rule and about six weeks to the pick and about nineteen years and nine days and a morning are seven figures of time and not one of them goes in at either end of this column. **About nine men on a bank and about four of them and about five of them and three names on a slate and a boy of about fifteen and a woman of about thirty-eight who keeps a block and a man of about fifty-one who keeps the water and a man of about forty-four who holds the second hoe and a man of about sixty of the Standing and four holders of ground and four sets of people and two sets that said no and Aren Kest are thirteen figures of persons and are not added to one another, and the penny for the bell was paid and is not in this column.** Nobody thanked anybody.
