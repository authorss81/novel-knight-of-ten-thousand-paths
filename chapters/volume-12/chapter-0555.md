# Chapter 0555

**An old crossing over a channel at Merefen, a town of about four thousand people on the eighth of Greenmonth, YR 320, a Friday and not a market day, between the second hour and the eighth, a man of thirty-two with a limp stood under a bell about the height of his chest that had not made a sound since the year he was born, and he did not ring it, and he was asked about a sill and gave an opinion, and he got up a bank in about four seconds longer than he sat down, and nobody in that town thanked him for either.**

The sill is at the third bay from the east and it had gone soft at the underside where a cart wheel had found the same place for about eleven years, and the man who keeps the yard had wanted it looked at in Hearthmonth and had been told that Hearthmonth is the copying season and that nobody goes under a span in Hearthmonth.

He went under it at about the third hour, on his forearms, with a lamp behind him and his stick against the outside of the pier, and he counted four load points before he went in and counted them again coming out, and he told the man who keeps the yard that the sill wanted mending and that the cart had wanted it mended for about eleven years and that both of those were true at once and that neither of them was anybody's fault, and that he would want four days' notice and the use of a stall.

**And then he said the thing that made the man who keeps the yard stop arguing about the price, which is that if the sill goes in Thawmonth there will be nine hundred carts a week going round by the reed road, and that a reed road takes about four days longer and about four times the straw, and that a figure of about nine hundred carts belongs to a man with a handcart four days from here and is not his figure and is not this crossing's figure, and that a crossing does not count carts and in about eleven years has never counted anything except the water.**

The man who keeps the yard said *noted* and wrote it down, which nobody in that town expected, and did not thank him, because a man who writes a thing down does not thank a man who said it.

**There is a woman of about sixty-two behind a bar four streets off and she was about sixty-two about eleven years ago as well and nobody has ever checked, and she put a pot of tea on the counter in front of him without being asked and he did not pay for the first one and did pay for the second.**

Outside the bar there is a yard of carts and a locked room over it with a board over the stair, and the board carries thirty-four in a hand that is not his, and he did not go up and did not look at the board and has not looked at the board in about four years and nobody in that town has asked him to.

Then he went down to the water, because the bell is over the channel and a man who has come to look at a bell does not need a reason to stand under it.

It is green-black bronze about the height of his chest with a hairline split running from the lip to the shoulder, and it hangs in a frame of oak with a bar through the headstock loop and two men took the ends of that bar when it was lifted and nobody lifted it by hand. **And it has not made a sound since the year he was born, and it is not cracked in a way that would let it ring badly, and it is cracked in a way that makes it a thing nobody would put a rope on.**

He put his left hand flat on the lip of it and the bronze was cold and then was not cold, and his palm did the thing it does at about the fourth hour on a day when he has not said anything about it and has not said anything about it for eleven years.

Then he took his hand off it and stood there, and what he said out loud, to nobody, under a bell that nobody could hear, was this.

“**A crack does not make a bell unsafe. A crack makes a bell unusable. And a thing nobody can ring is a thing nobody has to answer for, and that has been the whole of the case for this bell for eleven years and about four hundred people in a town four days off have printed a sheet about it and not one of them has ever noticed that the reason it is safe is that it is broken.**”

Nobody heard him say it. Nobody was asked about it. A boy of about seventeen with a rod was on the bank at the time and heard about two words of it and neither word was one of them.

**And the boy came over with his board under his arm, because the boy keeps a column on a board and has kept it for about two years, and the column is not a column of debts. It is eels, and it is line, and it is bread, and it is the price of the oil a man mixes for a bait that catches nothing, and it goes up every week on a Friday and the boy brings it to the bar and the woman of about sixty-two reads it and takes a penny for reading it, and nobody has ever offered him more than the penny.**

He asked the man one question about the water under the crossing that nobody had asked him, which was whether water goes where you put it.

“**I do not know,**” Aren Kest said. “**I know where it goes now and I know what it did last year and I know that what it did last year is not a promise about what it will do. I have been on a bridge for about eleven years and I have never once known where the water goes and I have been wrong about where it goes in about two directions at the same time on more than one occasion.**”

“**That is not an answer,**” the boy said, and he was about seventeen and he had said it the way the woman with the basket had said it four days and about six miles away, and neither of them knew the other existed.

“**No,**” Aren Kest said. “**It is a figure about what I do not know, and it is on your board, and it is the only line on that board that is not money, and I would like you to leave it there and not to rub it out, and I am not going to pay you for the line and you are not going to ask me to.**”

The boy wrote it on the board and did not rub it out and did not pay him for it.

Then he asked the second question, which was whether a man could make a channel go where you wanted it to go, and he said it in the voice of somebody who has been thinking about it for a week, and Aren Kest looked at the boy for about as long as a man takes to say four and then told him the truth about his own trade in one sentence, and it was that about four miles of stone with a lip on the high side is a day's work for four men who have done it before and a season's work for four men who have not, and that whether it holds for nine years or thirty-one years is not a question a man answers by looking at it and it is a question a man answers by finding out who built it and what it was built to do.

“**I have never found out who built that,**” the boy said.

“**Then you cannot mend it,**” Aren Kest said, “**and you will not be the first man who has found that out about something his own family has used for a hundred years, and it is a worse thing to find out than to guess, and I would rather you found out at fifteen than at forty.**”

The boy wrote none of that on the board. He has still not written it on the board and he is about seventeen and there is a line on his board that is not money and there is nothing about a channel.

**And the cost of that Friday is a column and it is the boy of about seventeen with a rod's, and it is the first one he has ever kept, and a bell that cannot be rung is not in it and has no price.**

**a hook at two, a line of twine at three, a plug of iron at five, a wedge of bread at seven, a second wedge of bread at eight, a pot of tar at eleven, a hank of yarn at fourteen, a pound of candles at eighteen, a night's straw at twenty-one, and a coil of tarred rope at thirty.** Two, three, five, seven, eight, eleven, fourteen, eighteen, twenty-one and thirty is one hundred and nineteen pence. **A mark is forty-eight pence and one hundred and nineteen pence is two marks and twenty-three over**, and eleven years of a cart wheel finding the same place is not in it and is not a figure of persons, and four days' notice and the use of a stall are not in it and are not prices, and a reed road taking four days longer and four times the straw is not in it, and thirty-four on a board over a stair is a figure of carts in a different town and is not in this column and is not this board.

He came back up the bank at about the sixth hour in about four seconds longer than it takes to sit down, and the bell did not ring behind him, and it has not rung since the year he was born, and the man of about thirty-eight with a satchel was not in that town and has not been in it for about nine years and nobody in Merefen said anything about either of those two facts.
