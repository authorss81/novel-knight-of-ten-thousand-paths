# Chapter 0325

**The fifth day of Goatmonth, YR 316, a Monday, and not a market day in a county with a market, and the second hour and the fourth hour and the sixth hour, at a steading up the water in a valley four hundred miles from any county that is on a map, with a mill and a glass works at the low end and a made road about four hundred yards long with a standing finish on the end of it that goes nowhere, and about four hundred people in that valley, and a man of thirty-two with a limp who came up that road on his second morning in that valley and was in the ninth generation's part of it by the fourth hour, and nobody in that valley has been told what he said at a door.**

The road a man of thirty-four dug in a wet summer is four hundred yards long and has a standing finish on the end of it and two stones that hold the finish, and about nine feet of grass stands between the stones and the grass has not been cut and nobody in that valley has ever asked him to cut it, and a boy of about nine goes up past the end of it four days out of seven with a barrow and does not stop. The steadings are up the water past the last of the oven and there are eleven of them and four hamlets, and the water is the size of a man's two hands and it is not deep enough to drown a dog in, and about two hundred of the ninth generation have never once been down at the low end of that valley.

Marek Ollen entered him at a gate on the second morning and put a mark in the third column and said nothing about it, and the roll is folded in four and has about four hundred people on it in about nine hundred lines, and there is a gap of nine ruled days in it in a season of about four in a year, and that gap was ruled by Marek Ollen's own hand and is older than most of the page and has nine names in it in a hand that is not his.

**And for three days he had been nothing in that valley, which is a thing nine men in a shed, a man at a wheel, a woman with a slate and a room with twenty-nine benches bolted down in it can all do at once and none of them can do for anybody else.**

He had slept in a barn at the low end of the water on the first night because somebody offered it and had not asked him who he was, and he had gone up the water on the second morning on his own and had eaten at an oven and had been asked his trade by four people and had given it four times, and about two hundred of a generation that has never once been up the low end of that valley went past him on that lane and did not look at him, and he had walked four hundred yards of made road and stopped at the standing finish and looked at nine feet of grass and come back.

**And nobody in that valley has asked him what he is for, and nobody there is going to, and a man who cannot get up a bank and does not need to has been going up a hill for three days and has been on his own the whole of it, and four people in four hundred miles of country have worked out since that the only place a man can be nobody for three days running is a valley of about four hundred people that has no register in it, and that every other place a man of thirty-two can stand has a paper on him somewhere.**

---

**And a woman of about thirty came down off a steading at about the fourth hour with a pail and stopped when she saw him, because a man had not come up that road in about a month and had not come up it at all twice in the time anybody in that steading could remember.**

She had a pail in one hand and she set it down on the ground when she stopped, which is the ordinary thing a person does when they are going to be more than a second, and she stood about four feet off him and looked at him for as long as it takes to look at a stick.

“**You are not from the low end,**” she said.

“**No,**” he said.

“**Then you are from somewhere that has a road,**” she said, and did not ask where, and about two people in that steading have said since that a woman of about thirty asked a stranger one question and did not ask the second one, and that the second one is the one anybody in that valley asks.

“**I wanted to say a thing out loud,**” she said. “**I have been waiting about a year to say it and there is nobody in this valley to say it to, and you are the first person who has come up that road that I do not know, and that is the only reason I have stopped.**”

“**Say it,**” he said.

---

**And she said it, and it was about her mother, and it took about nine minutes, and he was in the middle of it in about a minute and a half and he was in the middle of it for the rest of it.**

She sat down on the wall at the steading gate with the pail beside her and she told it the way a person tells a thing they have said in their own head about nine hundred times, which is without stopping for anybody. Her mother had died in the winter before last. There had been a fire at the low end of the water that had not been a fire. Her mother had known every person in that valley by what they did and not by what they were called, and she had been asked about a name four days before she died by a woman with a slate and had not had it, and had it back an hour later, and had said, in her own words, that a person who forgets a thing and gets it back has been given the same thing twice and not the first time, and that the second time is not the first time and does not count.

**And at about a minute and a half he was not in a valley four hundred miles from any county that is on a map, and he did not know when it had happened, and there is a page with eleven lines on it in the inside pocket of that coat and the eleventh of them is a man on a shift nineteen years old and about four people in four counties have heard about the eleventh line and it is not the thing he was in the middle of, and the thing he was in the middle of is a woman on a wall telling him about a woman who did not want a name twice.**

He had a road-marked left palm and it was warm at about the fourth hour in that county every day of a year and nobody in that valley knows it is warm, and it was warm, and he put it flat on the top rail of a gate while she was talking and he did not know he had done it.

“**I do not know whose that is,**” he said, at about the ninth minute.

She stopped. She had not expected him to speak at all and he had spoken in the middle of the ninth minute, and about two people in that steading, who were not there and were four hundred yards down the water, have worked out since that a stranger interrupted a woman of about thirty in the ninth minute of the only thing she has said out loud in a year.

“**You do not know whose that is,**” she said.

“**I have been in the middle of it for about a minute and a half and it is not mine,**” he said, “**and I cannot tell you when it stopped being mine, and I have been carrying a thing of my own for about two years that I did not put down, and it came up in the middle of your mother's last month and I do not know what part of what I was feeling was yours and I would rather say that to you than say the other thing.**”

---

**And she did not believe him, and she said so in about nine seconds, and nobody improved on it in about a year.**

“**You are a man who has been in this valley before,**” she said. “**You have not been in this house and you have not been in this room, and my mother is not yours, and if you are telling me that you do not know whose grief you are carrying then you are telling me a thing I have no way of checking in about nine hundred years of this valley, and I have just said out loud the one thing I have said out loud about her since she died, and I am not going to be a woman who said it to a man who was somewhere else at the time.**”

“**I do not know that you do not believe me,**” he said.

“**Then it does not matter what you know,**” she said, “**and you have not said why it came up in the middle of my mother's last month, and that is the part I would have believed.**”

**And that was the end of it. He did not say why, and there was a page with eleven lines on it in his coat and the eleventh of them is a man on a shift nineteen years old and he did not take it out, and a separate leaf with a seventh line on it that says *a person* is in the other pocket and he did not take that out either, and nobody in that valley has ever seen either of them and nobody in that valley was asked.**

She picked up the pail. She did not tell him not to come up that road again and she did not tell him to. She said the one thing she said after, and she said it to a woman coming down the water with nothing, and he was nine feet off and he heard all of it.

“**I told it to a stranger who was somewhere else at the time,**” she said. “**That is the whole of what I got out of it, and I am not going to do it again.**”

---

Nobody walked a part of that day with him. The mill went on and the man at that wheel stayed on it, the market stood down, the oven went through two batches, about nine men came down off the hill behind him and went back up it, a boy of about nine went up the made road past the standing finish with a barrow, and about four hundred people in that valley went through their day not knowing that a man of thirty-two with a limp sat on a wall at a steading gate for about nine minutes and then told a woman of about thirty that he did not know whose grief he had been in the middle of, and that she told him she did not believe him, and that neither of them said one word to anybody else about either half of it, and there is no line in that valley on which a thing anybody said at a door in a steading can be put down and cannot be got back out of, and the woman with the slate is at the top of the lane and was not told and was not asked and has not been asked, and about two people in that steading have said since that a man has come up a road four hundred yards long that does not go anywhere and told the truth about himself to the wrong person on the wrong afternoon, and that the woman was right, and that being right about that is the cheapest thing available to a person in a valley.

**And at about the sixth hour a man of thirty-four with sixteen men and no work came up that lane from the yard at the top of it, and he had not been in that yard since the second hour, and he had been up the water on his own business and had come down the lane at the sixth, and he did not ask what had happened and he did not ask who the woman was.**

He took up a place across the boards from the man with the limp and looked at him across about nine feet of yard for about as long as it takes to look at nothing, and he said nothing at all, and then he said a thing that was not about the thing.

“**She will tell it again,**” he said. “**She told her father-in-law's people the same thing in a kitchen in the autumn and she stopped halfway and did not start again, and she has been carrying the half she got through ever since, and you have just had the whole of it, and I am not saying that to be unkind. I am saying that I have been waiting four days to say something to a man with a limp and this is what I have got, and it is about a woman up the water and not about you, and you can do what you like with it.**”

**And he went back down the lane to his yard, and the man with the limp did not follow him and did not ask him what he had meant by the four days, and about two people in that valley have said since that a man with sixteen men and no work has been waiting four days to say one sentence to a stranger and did not say it and then said a different one, and that the different one cost him something and was not the one, and that nobody in that valley is going to find out which of the two sentences was the one he had been holding.**
