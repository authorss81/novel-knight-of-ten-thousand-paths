# Chapter 0341

**The sixth day of Mudmonth, YR 317, a Saturday, and a market day, and the second hour and the fourth hour and the seventh hour, in a county that has four market towns in a district and about nine hundred people, in a low stone building on the north side of the biggest of those four markets with a shop counter in the front room and a press and a frame of pigeonholes in the room behind it, and a bench of nine across the same street in a room over a byre that sits on Saturdays and on nothing else in the week, and about nine hundred people in that county do not know that a man with a satchel came into the yard of that building on that morning and asked the first question anybody has asked in it in about four years.**

The biggest of those four markets is about two hundred yards long and it is shut on a Wednesday and a Friday and it is open on a Saturday and on a Thursday, and about four hundred people stand in it on a market morning out of nine hundred in the whole county, and the other three markets are a row of stalls and a rope. A waggon of lime came up that street on the Friday before and was at the end of it an hour with nobody under the tailboard and went back down it, and a man who keeps a book of that street's comings and goings wrote one line about it, and there is no figure anywhere in what he keeps and no form in that county that would let anybody produce it.

**And the building is a shop and not an office, and it has been a shop since before anybody in that county can remember, and the back room has a press in it and a pigeonhole of about four hundred pigeonholes in a frame on the wall, and about four hundred sheets of that office's own printed series are in those pigeonholes this morning, and there are about nine hundred of that series in that county in a year and they go round in two rounds, and the first round went out in the last month of the year before last and the second goes out in about four days.**

A woman of about fifty does the collation. She has done it in that room for nineteen years and she takes the sheets out in the order the pigeonholes are in, which is not the order they went in, and she counts them into nine piles on a board, and a round is about four hundred and fifty sheets and takes her a day and a half, and about two hundred sheets of the second round of this year are on that board already and the rest of them are in the frame on the wall.

A man of about forty keeps that building. He has kept it for four years and he has never once been asked what is in the pigeonholes, and the shop sells nails, string, twine, sealing wax, a sheet of clean waste paper and a quill, and nine people come in on a market morning and four of them buy something.

**And a bench of nine sits in a room over a byre across that street on Saturdays and on nothing else in the week, and it sat at the seventh hour on that Saturday in a market morning, and nothing was put in front of it.**

It has been asked nine things in nineteen years. Four were yes and three were no with a reason and one was brought up two flights and not put, and the ninth was refused on the last day of the year before last in eleven words with a reason in it, and the clerk of about sixty who keeps that minute has no line in it for how many times anything has happened in that room and has not gone looking for one, and has an answer ready that nobody in that county has ever wanted.

**And under the fourth step of the outside stair of that room there is a slip of paper about nine inches by six, and the figure at the head of it is in a hand that does not write figures very well, and there are nine marks under that figure, and it said nine before that Saturday and it said nine after it, and what that figure counts is askings, and an asking is neither money nor a person and goes into nobody's arithmetic, and no form in that hall has ever been the place one went.**

**And a hundred and forty is cut into the end of one of twenty-nine benches in a room over a cross four hundred miles off, and what that one counts is how often anybody has ever been in an aisle to be asked, and those two nines and that hundred and forty and the nine piles on a board in a shop are four different figures and no person in this kingdom has ever added any of them to any of the others and is not going to.**

Nobody walked a part of that day with anybody. Two hundred yards of market carried about four hundred people and about five hundred of that county did not come out of their houses, the mill two days up the water turned as a mill turns, nine men stood at a bench-board four feet off the ground in a yard, and a man of about fifty-five worked three bars at a barrier nine hundred yards further up that same road that anybody at all can go round at either end in nine seconds.

---

**And at the fourth hour a man with a satchel came into the yard of that building and put the satchel on the bench against the wall and asked the man of about forty a question, and it was the first question anybody in that building has been asked in about four years, and it went the other way from the way a question goes in a shop.**

He is about thirty. He has a satchel and a firm is not in it and he carries for a trade that has a name and the name is not printed on anything he carries. He had been in that room once before, in the last month of the year before last, and was in it for nine minutes with a bundle against his chest while a woman of about fifty counted four hundred and fifty sheets into nine piles, and nobody spoke to him then and nobody has spoken to him in four years and he has not said that he had been there before.

“**What is in the pigeonholes,**” he said.

“**About four hundred sheets,**” the man of about forty said.

“**That is a number of things. I am asking what they are.**”

“**They are sheets of that office's own printed series and they are the county's own returns and there are four hundred of them in there this morning and there have been about four hundred in there twice a year since before I came,**” the man of about forty said, and stopped, and about two people in that county have worked out since that a man who keeps a shop for four years gave a stranger three figures in one answer and that all three of them are true and that he cannot say which of the three the stranger was asking for.

“**And where do they go,**” the man with the satchel said.

“**Round. Twice a year. Nine counties.**”

“**And who keeps a list of them.**”

And the man of about forty was in his own shop and found out that he did not know, and it took him four seconds, and the finding was not a shock and was not a discovery of a lost thing. It was that there has never been one. The pigeonhole is not a list. A pigeonhole is a hole in a frame with a number cut over it and a number cut over it is a place a sheet goes and not a record that it went, and about nine hundred sheets a year go out of that room in two rounds and there is no paper in that county that says which of them went in the first and which of them went in the second, and two hundred of them are on a board in nine piles in that room this morning and the rest of them are in a frame on a wall.

“**There is no list,**” the man of about forty said.

“**No,**” the man with the satchel said. “**I did not think there was. I have carried two of the rounds out of that room and I did not know there was not one either, and I have been doing it six years, and about four people in four counties have been saying since that a man asked a shopkeeper a question and got the true thing about a pigeonhole and that neither of them was surprised and that the whole of what was said between them is eleven words and a figure.**”

**And the discovery is not the pigeonhole. The discovery is that about four hundred sheets in a frame, about nine hundred sheets in a year, and about nine sets of copies that do not exist yet are three different counts of three different things, and that a man who carries paper for a living has never once been given the first of them against the second of them, and that nobody in that county has ever worked out how many of that series have passed through that room in four years and nobody is going to, and that the three of those are not added to each other in this building or in any other and must not be.**

**And a man with a satchel had one of that office's own printed series in the satchel that morning, and he had carried it nine days, and he read none of it then and reads none of anything, and one of every nine of that series has something standing in the space in front of the name of the place and this one was not that one and he has never once asked which of them was and nobody in that county has asked him and nobody is going to.**

---

**And at the sixth hour the second round was made up on the board, and the woman of about fifty counted the last of the piles with her own figure against the corner of it, and the man of about forty was at the end of the board and did not help, because he has never helped, because helping is a thing a person is asked to do and nobody has ever asked him in four years.**

“**Four hundred and forty-eight,**” the woman of about fifty said.

“**Round it,**” the man of about forty said.

“**I do not round it. Four hundred and forty-eight is what is on the board and it is two less than the first round and there is nowhere in this county to enter the two.**”

**And about two hundred of that county have said since that a woman of about fifty who has counted four hundred and fifty sheets into nine piles for nineteen years has said out loud that the two sheets that are missing are not a thing anybody can enter, and that the two of them were probably never there, and that she has said neither of those things and that nobody asked her and that the number four hundred and forty-eight is the only figure in this county that has ever been in a room in this building and is not on a form.**

And a man of about forty went to the counter and sold a sheet of clean waste paper and a quill to four people over that whole morning and nobody in that shop said one word to anybody else in it, and about four people in that county have worked out since that it took a building burning for nine hundred people in that county to find out whether a shop that sells string and a quill had always been an office, and a bench of nine sat across the street for about an hour and was asked nothing, and a man with a satchel was in the yard with his back against a wall for a quarter of an hour looking at a frame of holes in a wall, and about two hundred people in that county have been saying since that a man who carries paper stopped in a yard he has stopped in six times and looked at a wall the whole time, and that he was not waiting for anybody and that nobody came.

---

**And that Saturday is set out here in pennies, item by item, and the total is on the line after the last of them and not before it.**

A candle, a penny. A wick, a penny. A quill, a penny. A sheet of clean waste paper, a penny. A leather apron for the collation, twopence. A pot of oil for the lamp in the back room, twopence. A fathom of string, threepence. The charge for entering one line in a book, fourpence.

*Four pennies is fourpence and the two twopences are fourpence and the string is threepence and the charge is fourpence, and that is four and four and three and four, and four and four is eight, and eight and three is eleven, and eleven and four is fifteen, and fifteen pence is the whole of that Saturday.*

Total: fifteen pence. **A sheet of that office's own printed series is a penny a sheet at that counter and nobody in that building sells them by the hundred, because a hundred sheets that go round nine counties are a hundred separate things and a price for a hundred of them is a figure about a hundred and not about a room. The bag a man carries them in is his own and not that office's and nobody has ever put the two in one sum. A woman of about fifty who has counted about four hundred and fifty sheets into nine piles for nineteen years is paid out of a shop and not out of a chest, and a wage is in no column of that office and may not become one.**

---

**And a bench of nine in a room over a byre across that street rose at about the eighth hour and went down a stair and out into a market that was shutting, and a clerk of about sixty shut a fair copy and put it in a press he has used for nineteen years, and under the fourth step of the outside stair of that room there is a slip of paper about nine inches by six and it said nine before that Saturday and it said nine after it, and about four thousand people in a town two hundred miles off did not know that nine men had sat in a room for an hour or that nine marks were on a piece of paper under a step or that a man had asked a shopkeeper a question and got a figure, and about nine hundred people in that county did not know any of it either, and the pigeonhole in the back room of a shop held about four hundred sheets of other people's business overnight and will hold them again tomorrow, and there is no list of them in that county and there never has been, and the second round goes out in about four days.**
