# Chapter 0314

**The twenty-first day of Frostmonth, YR 316, a Saturday, and a market day, and the second hour and the fourth hour and the fifth hour and the seventh hour, and a market day in a town of about four thousand people on a river, with a street four hundred yards long, about four hundred people in it, a reach four hundred yards down a lane off that street with about forty men on it, and a yard on a street four hundred yards up the county's own road with about nine men in it, and a hut at a barrier of three bars three hundred yards down that road, and a long room over a wharf at the north end of that market with its outside stair standing open and nobody on it, and nobody in that market knows that about nine people went up that outside stair on the Wednesday before last and looked at a thing under it and came down again.**

It is on from the second hour and it is not finished till about the seventh, and in the middle of it a waggon of salt stood at the mouth of the channel road with nobody standing under the tailboard for the better part of an hour, and the salt on it belongs to a salt merchant in his own waggon and the guild has no part of it and no claim on any of it. One line went into the book of that road's up and down for the waggon, and there are nine lines in that book this year, and nobody in this town knows there are nine.

**A long room over a wharf at the north end of that market stood open from the second hour, and a bench of nine sits in it on Saturdays and on nothing else in the week, and it sits at about the seventh hour, and up to about the fifth hour of that day nobody had gone up that stair, and the reason nobody had is that the last person up it had come on the Wednesday before last and had not come back since.**

The last person up those steps was a man of thirty-two with a limp, on the Wednesday before last, and he climbed them by himself and stood on four bare feet of floor between the table and the wall for about as long as nine things take to count twice, and he did not go a step further. Under that room's window is a chest with two marks in it that have sat where they are since the ninth of a month in the year before last. Nine people have noticed that floor. Not one of the nine has ever asked anybody what it is there for.

**And under that stair there is a page about nine inches by six, with a figure at the top of it in a hand that does not write figures very well, and nine marks under that figure that are not even, five of them sitting low on the paper and four of them sitting high, and nobody has ever entered that count on a form, and about four hundred people in that market have walked past that stair without once knowing that it was there.**

A count of askings is nothing that can be priced and nothing that counts people and it goes into no sum with anything, and a bench of nine in that county has been asked nine things in nineteen years, and the count is nine, and it is nine this morning, and nobody in that county has been asked to move it.

---

**Nobody in that county owns a market and a reach and takes a day off for either of them, and so the road stayed open over the water and the salt went over the first span of it, and about forty men were out on the timber by the second hour on a morning when four hundred people were standing in a street four hundred yards away.**

Bay four of that reach was finished in a summer and its pointing ran into a third day, and since then not one bill raised in this county has carried a complaint about it. There is a short joint in the parapet above it. Nine men work in that yard and not one of them has put a rule on it.

“**Load points,**” a man on the first bay said.

“**Nine since the second hour,**” the man with the limp said.

“**You have been on that book a fortnight,**” the man of fifty and a half said.

“**I have been on the deck of it,**” the man with the limp said.

“**It is the same thing to me and it is not the same thing to you and I have not worked out which of us is right,**” the man of fifty and a half said, and laid the bar down flat on the parapet and picked it up again without using it.

He is fifty and a half and the arch work and the pointing are his trade and he has been spreading a second coat of finish over a first one for about a fortnight, and the laying of the timber is not his and nobody in that yard has put him in a position to ask him about it. Four people in this town have it worked out that a man paid to point a thing who does not point it has been told to leave off and has not been told that he is permitted to.

A carter of thirty came over the reach with a barrow and a waggon of his own, and another man's salt went over the first bay in it, and four hundred people in that market stood forty yards away and watched a waggon that was not the guild's cross a bridge that belongs to the county, and about nine of the four hundred worked out whose salt it was and the rest did not.

**The whole of what that man is for in this county is a tally he keeps on the timber, and it has no number on it, it belongs to no office, nobody alive can produce it, and no form anywhere has ever heard of it. He has been at it about two years and about forty mornings out of every four hundred.**

What he writes on the deck of a bridge in a town of about four thousand people is a count of loads, and a load is a cart, a barrow or a handbarrow, and a load point is where a load was set down and not where it went. There are nine months in the year in which he does it and three in which the weather stops him, and he has never been asked for any of that by anybody, and nobody in this county knows that a man stands out on that timber at all.

**Nobody in that town has ever noticed that the inside of that man's left hand is warm at about the fourth hour and has been for two years, and four people have watched him lay it flat on cold timber about forty mornings a month and have never once put a question to it, and the one person in this kingdom who would have felt it is two hundred miles off in a building that has stopped doing its ordinary work, sitting beside a stove that has not been lit since the weather turned, and nobody in that building has asked after a man with a warm hand and nobody is going to.**

By the fifth hour he had shut the tally and was up the lane and standing in the street with the rest of them, four hundred strong in four hundred yards. The salt waggon outlasted the market by about an hour and then turned back down the channel road, and the man who keeps the account of what that road carries in both directions wrote nothing more that day, because a waggon that comes up a road and goes back down it is one line and not two, and that line is the ninth in his book for the year, and this town does not know that a book exists.

---

**Four hundred yards up the county's own road from that market there are nine men in a yard, and a board stands on legs four feet off the ground and is about nine feet from end to end, and behind them, in a room of their own, they keep a press, and it is not an office press and nothing can be taken out of it by law or by anybody else.**

The boy at that board is fifteen and what he copies is not only the numbers on a line but the gaps underneath them as well, and of the four spaces on that board the gap is the widest, and he has copied it eleven times in one week, and nobody has ever explained to him what a boy is supposed to put in a gap. Nothing is. A gap is what it is for.

“**He will not finish that,**” said the man of twenty-five whose yard it is.

“**He is doing the gap under it,**” said the man of about nineteen with an empty hod, from the end of the board.

“**I can see which part of it he is doing. I have watched you watch him do it four times this week.**”

“**It is under the number,**” the boy said.

“**That is where it is.**”

“**Then stop saying where it is,**” the boy said.

“**I will say it once more and then not again,**” the man of twenty-five said.

He went back to the gap. Two of the men in that yard have worked out that a boy has been set to copy an emptiness for about two years and has not once been told what an emptiness is for, and one of the two has also worked out which of the four on that board is the widest, and both of them have worked out that about nine feet of board on a street four hundred yards up the county's own road is the only place in this county where a man is told on purpose to put nothing down on purpose.

Behind them a man of about sixty keeps a lime heap tight against a wall under a lid, and he works with his back to the yard the whole morning, and he is the one man there that the yard does not pay. He said one thing over his shoulder that day and he did not turn round for it and nobody was meant to have it.

“**Somebody has to know where the lime is.**”

**Three hundred yards further down that same road there are three bars across it and a hut beside them, and a man of about fifty-five has lived in that hut nineteen years, and he took the bars down and put them up nine times before the seventh hour, and four words went out of him to each thing that came through. Those four words are on no list in this county and are not going on one, and about four hundred people in this town have gone through that barrier and none of them has ever wanted to know where four words of that kind come from.**

---

**A woman with a pail went up the lane at the north end of that street at about the fifth hour and said one thing at each of nine doors, and at all nine of them she was gone again before the noise of the knuckle had finished in the frame, and nine is the number of doors in that lane and not the number anybody told her to do, and about nine of the four hundred people in that market that afternoon were somebody else's business.**

At the door forty feet from a wagon arch she said that the salt would be gone off the first bay by the second hour on Tuesday. At the next door she said that the waggon had gone back down the channel road. At the third she said that the room over the wharf had been open all morning and that nobody had gone up the stair.

Nobody in that lane has ever put a question to her about where any of it comes from. Two people in the lane have worked out since that there is nobody else in this county who takes one morning and says one sentence about it at nine houses, and that the town does not know the nine sentences are one sentence, and that on that Saturday the three things in front of it were salt, a waggon and a stair.

---

Nobody walked any part of that Saturday with anybody. Water that had been standing under the wharf since the previous evening's tenth hour went back at about the third hour. Forty men off the timber, nine men out of the yard, a man of about fifty-five back to three bars and a hut, and a stranger going up a road that belongs to the county on a stick he has never once put down and does not need to.

**And about the seventh hour a bench of nine sat in a long room over a wharf at the north end of that market, and there was nothing to be put in front of it, and the clerk of about sixty opened a minute he has kept for nineteen years and it is the second kind of minute he keeps and he keeps the first kind in a field at a parish boundary once in nineteen years and is not going to be asked about that, and he did not enter anything on the sheet under that stair, and nothing is going to be a tenth, and if a tenth came it would not be a present. About four thousand people live in this town and about four hundred of them were in that street this morning and not one of them knows what is under that stair, and nobody in this county has ever been asked to move it, and nobody in this county is going to be.**
