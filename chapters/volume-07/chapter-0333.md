# Chapter 0333

**The sixth day of Thawmonth, YR 317, a Thursday, and a market day, and the second hour and the fourth hour and the seventh hour, in a provincial town, in a building of four floors and seven presses with about forty people on the floors of it, and one floor up from a press room there is a room with a stove in it that has not been lit and a single window nine feet off a bare wall, and about four hundred sheets a month go across one table under that window and are read at waist height, and about nine hundred people in that town do not know that a man of thirty-one in that room has carried the same ruled line across four turns of the year without knowing what was standing against it.**

The market was standing at the second hour and about nine hundred people were on their feet in it, and about two hundred sheets went across that table before the fourth hour, and the woman of about thirty read every one of them at waist height because the light in that room comes up off the boards and not down off the sky.

**And the man who carries a district came down that stair and back up it twice, and the man who carries the line did not leave the room at all, and they are two men at one table and nobody in that building has ever put their two jobs in one sentence.**

A district in that office is a set of market towns. A man of about thirty keeps that room's books, has been in the building nine years, and at the turn of a quarter he carries a district forward: he writes the district at the head of the new sheet in the ordinary way, and it takes him about four seconds, and about four people on that floor have said since that a man who can do that in four seconds has done it about nine times and has never once been asked to do it a tenth time in front of anybody.

**A line is a different thing from a district and it is the same office that has both.** A line is a rule ruled on a sheet with a figure at one end of it, and it is a line's business to be straight from one sheet to the next, so that a figure in the second column stays against the thing it was put there against and not against the thing that happens to be under it four weeks later. The man who carries the line copies the ruled line of a district off the old sheet onto the new one and he does about nine of them a quarter and he has been doing it since he came into that building, and his name is Osten Varel and he is thirty-one.

---

**And the turn of a quarter is not a thing anybody in that room is told about, and it is done in about nine days, and the ninth day is a Thursday, and about nine hundred people in that town were on their feet that morning and about two hundred of them went past a building they have gone past four times a week for years and not one of them knows what was being carried across a table inside it.**

A district is carried by one man and a line is carried by another, and the two of them are in the same room, and the difference between the two jobs is about nine seconds and it is the whole of the difference between this office and every other room in it. The man of about thirty writes a place at the head of a sheet. The man of thirty-one rules a line with a figure at the end of it. A place is a name and a line is a promise that the figure at the end of it belongs to the same thing it belonged to last quarter.

“**Do you do all nine,**” Osten Varel said, at the second hour, to the man of about thirty.

“**I do all nine,**” the man of about thirty said.

“**I have only ever done the ninth.**”

“**You have done the ninth for four turns and there are eight of them that are places, and I have never once asked you which of the nine you took and you have never once asked me,**” the man of about thirty said, and went up that stair and back down it in about nine minutes, and about four people on that floor have said since that two men in one room have carried a place and a line across four turns of a year and have never once put the two in one sentence, and that neither of them could tell the other what the other did, and that this is not a mystery to either of them and has never once come up.

---

**And nobody has ever asked anybody how often, and the fifth column is filled in about nine times a week out of a rule of the trade, and nobody in that building has ever checked it, and the head over it is the office's own and not a clerk's, and about two people on that floor have worked out since that a column a man is told to fill in from memory is a column nobody has to be right about.**

“**How often is that again,**” a woman said, at about the fourth hour, to Osten Varel, with a sheet in her hand.

“**It is what it was last quarter,**” he said.

“**I know what it was last quarter. I am asking you whether you have looked at it.**”

“**No,**” he said, and about two people on that floor have said since that a woman asked a man whether he had looked at a figure he was copying and he said no in one word, and that the whole of that office's working on the fifth column is a number a man says he has not looked at being carried forward for a year, and that nobody in that building has ever asked a second question about the fifth of six columns on a form that is going round nine counties.

---

**And he was in that room at the fourth hour on that Thursday with nine sheets of the old quarter open in front of him and a straightedge and a quill, and he copied the ninth one in about nine minutes, and then he read the line up to himself, which is what a man does to a line he has just ruled, and nobody in that room has ever heard him do it and nobody has ever asked him why he does it.**

The line went across the sheet and stopped four inches short of the right margin, because the fifth column is the same width as the others and the rule stops where the rule stops. At the left-hand end of the line there was a place in the first column, written the way that office writes a place, which is a set of market towns. **And it was not a set of market towns. It was a room, over something, in a town of about four thousand people, and about four people in that building have said since that a man of thirty-one ruled a line on a sheet four hundred miles from the room the line was about, and the line is the correct length and the rule is the correct width and the place at the end of it is not a place.**

“**That is the same again,**” a woman said at the next table, in the voice of a person who has said the same thing about forty sheets that week.

“**It is the same again,**” Osten Varel said.

“**Is it the same as the last quarter.**”

“**It is the same as the last quarter and the one before that and the one before that,**” he said, and two people on that floor have said since that a man of thirty-one said the same sentence three times in nine seconds to a woman who was not asking him anything, and that the three times were true and that the woman was not listening and that neither of those two things has changed in about nine years.

---

**And the second column had a figure in it, and the head over the second column says *Date*, and the fifth column had a figure in it too, and the head over the fifth column says *How often*, and about four people in that building have said since that a man who copies a line for a living has never once been told where the figures at either end of it come from, and that the figures on the ninth line had changed twice in nine months and the line had not moved an eighth of an inch.**

He has been in that building since he was twenty-four. He has carried about nine lines a quarter for seven years, and he has carried the ninth of them four times, and the ninth of them is the one with a room at the end of it, and he did not know that until the fourth hour on that Thursday, and about two people in that building have said since that a man of thirty-one found out on a market Thursday what a man of thirty had known for nine years and neither of them has told the other one.

**And the discovery is not that the line is wrong. The discovery is that the line is the only thing on that sheet that has been right every single time anybody has looked at it, and that the line is the part that gets carried.**

---

**And a man of thirty-two with a limp was at that window at about the seventh hour with his back to a wall nine feet off it, and he has come to that building twice and he has asked one question in a room in three years and nobody in that building has ever answered it, and the question was asked in a press room at the end of a passage and not in this room, and about two people in that building have said since that a stranger stands at that window the way a man stands at a window he has decided to stand at.**

Osten Varel ruled the ninth line and put the straightedge down and said the thing he had been carrying in his mouth for about four weeks, and he said it once, and he said it to the man at the window because the man at the window is the only person in that building who has ever asked a question and got nothing, and about two people on that floor have said since that a man of thirty-one chose the one man in a building of four floors as the person to say it to and that the choosing took him about nine minutes and that the man he chose could not have answered it.

“**If the figure is wrong then the line is wrong and the line is mine and nobody has asked me which of the two I would rather have,**” Osten Varel said.

It is twenty-eight words and it foots at twenty-eight, and the man with the limp did not answer it, and he did not say that he had not heard it, and about four people in that building have worked out since that a man of thirty-two was told the true thing about a column by a man who carries lines and did not answer it and has not answered it since and is not going to.

“**I am sorry,**” the man with the limp said.

“**You have not asked me a question,**” Osten Varel said, and about two people in that building have worked out since that a man who carries a line was told sorry by a stranger for a thing nobody had asked him about, and that the man who said it had asked one question in that building in three years and it was not that one, and that the whole of what he came two hundred miles for on that afternoon went up the stairs of that building with him.

**And then Osten Varel carried the ninth line onto the new sheet, the same four inches short of the right margin, at about the seventh hour, and the figure went against it, and the figure was not the figure from the old sheet, and nobody in that room asked him where the new one came from, and the room did not ask him anything else either, and about four hundred sheets a month go across that table under that window and are read at waist height by one woman in a room with a cold stove in it.**

A market was on outside that building for the second time in three weeks and about nine hundred people went into it. Not one of them came up the stairs. Inside, a tray came back into a passage at the fourth hour with two sheets on it that nothing was written in, and a man of about sixty carried it out again and said the thing he says. At the bottom of the stair a man of about fifty set a page without lifting his eyes, and a man of thirty-two with a limp went up past him and past everybody else and was not stopped by anybody and asked nobody anything, and he has asked one question in that building in three years and it was not asked on that day.

**And a ruled line about four inches short of a right margin has been carried across four turns of a year in a room one floor up from a press room, and a figure of persons has gone against it in the second column twice and something else has gone against it in the fifth column twice, and there is no name of a person anywhere on the whole of the sheet in either of them, and a man of thirty-one carried the line four times without knowing what was at the end of it and knows now and has told one man who came two hundred miles, and that man is four hundred miles from being the person who can do anything about it, and he is going home on Monday.**
