# Chapter 0462

**The nineteenth day of Hearthmonth, YR 318, a Friday and not a market day, and the first hour and the fourth hour and the seventh hour, thirty miles south of a fen town of four thousand people, in a fold of ground with a byre in it and a stream nine feet wide and nine stones in a boundary that two hundred people live inside, where one man of about forty-one has walked that boundary since he was eleven years old and a boy who was nine does not walk it and is still nine, and a man of about twenty-nine has come thirty-one miles to ask him one question and has got there at the wrong hour of the day on purpose.**

## One

**A boundary is nine stones and a walk and two hundred people who have never argued about where their own boundary is, and that has been true for four generations, and the reason it has been true is that there was never anything to argue about.**

The man of about forty-one was at the third stone with a bar of iron and a bucket when the man of about twenty-nine came up the lane, and he did not stop and he did not put the bucket down and he said good day and went on baring the third stone, which is the one his grandfather cut and which leans four inches out of true and has leaned that way since before his father was born.

There is no house on that fold nearer than two hundred yards and the boy was not there and the boy is not there on a Friday and has not been on a Friday since he was nine.

**The byre was open at the near end and there were nine beasts in it and a boy of fourteen who works there came out with a bucket and looked at a stranger standing in the rain and went back in without a word, and that is the whole of what a fold of two hundred people does with a stranger.**

## Two

The man of about twenty-nine asked the ordinary question a person asks when they have come a long way with a question, which is whether the man of about forty-one had ever thought of handing it on.

**He said that he had thought about it and that his father had walked it and his grandfather had cut three of the nine and his great-grandfather had done nothing to it at all, and that handing it on is a thing you do to a house and not to a line in the ground.**

They walked it together because the man of about forty-one was going to the fourth stone anyway and the man of about twenty-nine could not have found it in nine counties. **It was not a walk anybody had arranged and it was not a walk for pleasure, and the man of about forty-one set the pace he has set since he was eleven and did not once slow down for a man in a wet coat, and the man of about twenty-nine kept up on a boundary that has a slope in it the whole of its length and did not say one word about the slope.**

At the fourth stone the man of about forty-one turned round and looked back the way they had come without saying anything, and four generations have done that at the fourth stone and none of them has ever written down why, and on the way back they stopped at the second stone and he said that his father cut that one, and at the third he said nothing at all, because that was the one he had been baring when a stranger came up the lane.

Then he said the thing that the man of about twenty-nine came thirty-one miles for.

“**I am not asking you about a line,**” he said. “**I am asking you what holds a thing together when nobody's father did it and nobody's son is going to.**”

**And that is twenty-eight words in one turn of one speech printed in two spans, and it is the second thing the man of about forty-one was asked that day and he answered the first one in one long sentence and the second one in twenty-eight words.**

## Three

And the man of about forty-one put the bar down in the mud and asked what the thing was, and the man of about twenty-nine told him, standing in the rain at the third stone, in nine minutes.

**He told him two miles and about nine hundred paces of hard sand and broken shell where a river runs into the sea, and eleven houses, and about nine hundred people, and about four hours out of every twelve when it is there, and about twice a day.**

And the man of about forty-one listened to all of it and nine-tenths of what he heard was about the tide and none of it was about a line, and when it was finished he picked the bar up again.

“**That has no edge,**” he said.

## Four

“**I will tell you what the nine stones do and I will tell you what they do not do,**” he said. “**They say where my ground stops. Your thing has no edge to it. Your sand turns in nine places and not one of those nine is a place anybody keeps, and the ninth of them is three black stones a storm put there in a month I was not born in and could not name if I were.**”

“**And if you want a thing that can be inherited then you would have to cut nine stones into your own sand,**” he said, “**and put them in on a low tide, and you would have nine stones and four hours, and then the water would have the lot of them back and you would be back where you started with a hole.**”

“**A boundary is inherited because it is not touched. I have never once in thirty years gone over a stone to see whether it was where it was. That is the whole of what my grandfather gave me and it was not a stone.**”

**And that is forty-four words in one turn of one speech, and the man of about twenty-nine counted them on the back of a sheet folded in four because he counts things, and did not write the number down.**

**And that is the finding, and it took four minutes and nobody in that fold improved on it, and the man of about twenty-nine stood in the rain at the third stone and understood that he had come thirty-one miles to be told that a thing you inherit is a thing that is not being moved, and a road is the one thing in nine counties that cannot be stopped from being moved, because the water moves it twice a day.**

He asked the man of about forty-one one further question on the way back to the byre, which was whether a boundary ever went wrong, and the man of about forty-one said that it had gone wrong three times in thirty years and that two hundred people had walked to the ninth stone on the third occasion because they could not agree, and that the third occasion took four hours and settled nothing.

And then he said the thing that undid the whole of the walk, and he said it without being asked and looking at the third stone. **That the fourth generation of men who have walked those nine stones is him. That he is forty-one. And that when he is gone the walking stops, and that two hundred people in that fold have never once asked him what happens then, and that he has never once told them, and that the boy of nine who is still nine is the only person in that fold who has ever asked him anything and what the boy asked was whether the third stone was the crooked one.**

## Five

The cost of that day is a column and it is the man of about twenty-nine's and he is keeping two of them now on the backs of two folded sheets and has not joined them up.

**a loaf at one penny, a second loaf at one, a pound of cheese at four, a pound of wood at three, a tallow candle at two, two quarts of small beer at two, a room at six, a bed at four, a sheet of paper at one.** One, one, four, three, two, two, two, six, four and one is twenty-six pence. **A mark is forty-eight pence and twenty-six pence is over half of one by two**, and nine stones and four generations are not in it and are not a figure of men, and thirty-one miles of walking in the rain is not in it and is not in anybody's column.

**And a man of about forty-one picked a bar of iron up out of the mud at the third stone and said that a boundary is inherited because it is not touched, and a man of about twenty-nine wrote the sentence down on the back of a sheet folded in four and did not put a name against it and did not put a date against it, and two hundred people in that fold got an ordinary Friday done and four of them knew a stranger had stood at the third stone in the rain and did not improve on it, and a boy who was nine and is still nine did not walk it and was not asked to and would not have come if he had been.**
