# Chapter 0484

**The second ebb after the one where a boy of about seventeen with a rod asked what a chain was for, and the third hour after the water went out, at the boat land at the landward end of a foreshore two miles and about nine hundred paces long where a river runs into the sea, where a boat of glass has come in on the flood and put itself on the shingle about forty yards below the slipway, and there is no month on that shore and no day of the week on it and there has been neither for about nine years, and eleven houses stand along that sand and about nine hundred people use it and four of the eleven have a person in them who answers for it and seven do not, and a man of about thirty-three with a barrow and a load of glass in it is standing on the shingle nine feet off a chain that has been laid out flat on the wet sand by a boy of about seventeen with a rod and is doing nothing with it at all.**

## One

**A boat of glass is not a thing that can be chained, and the reason is not that glass is hard or that a boat moves. The reason is that a chain answers one question and a boat is not on the line the chain was laid along.**

The boat is a flat-bottomed punt about two miles off and it has been on the shingle about an hour, and the man of about thirty-three with a barrow has taken nine loads out of it and has four more in the barrow and about eleven more on the shingle, and he has been going up and down a slipway for about an hour and has not once looked at the chain.

**A cart-load of shell is six pence in the only book in the trade and nine pence in the mouths of three ports and a kiln four days off that coast pays six pence out of a book and has never once paid a different one, and the glass on that shingle is worth neither of those figures to anybody and is worth a great deal more than both of them to the man with the barrow, and he is not going to be asked and has not been asked.**

## Two

**And the boy of about seventeen with a rod is standing at the end of the chain with the rod in his two hands and has not moved for four minutes, and the chain is laid out flat on the wet sand in a straight line about forty yards long with a ring at each end and a handle at one of them, and it is going slack in the middle because the sand under the middle of it is soft.**

“**It will not go straight!**” the boy said. “**It goes slack. I have had it out four times now and it goes slack in the same place every time and the man of about forty-four says that is the ninth place the sand turns but he says it like he is reading it off something.**”

“**It is slack because the middle of that is soft,**” the man of about thirty-three with a barrow said, without stopping, “**and if you pull it hard it will come in two, and if you pull it hard and it does not come in two then you have found something and it will not be a turn in the sand, it will be a stone, and there is no stone out here that anybody has ever wanted.**”

“**Then what is it for,**” the boy said, and he had asked that once before, a fortnight back, and had been told, and he asked it again because the man who had told him was not standing there.

**And that is a hundred and twenty-three words in three turns of one exchange, and the first two of the three turns are given here as their two printed spans each and not as totals, because each of the two totals is one of four numerals that are not set down anywhere in nine counties; the first is the sum of five and forty-seven, the second is the rest of the hundred and twenty-three and is the sum of ten and fifty-six, and the third is five, and the man of about thirty-three with a barrow did not stop walking up the slipway at any point in it, and the boy of about seventeen with the rod said the last five words to his back, and the man with the barrow answered the second turn without turning round and has said, since then, that he answered it to keep the boy from pulling the chain, and not for any other reason, and neither of those is a lie.**

## Three

**And what a chain is for, said out loud on a shingle by a boy of about seventeen who has been asking people for four days, is that it finds out how far one thing is from another thing, and it does not find out which of the two things is the right one, and it does not find out whether either of them ought to be there.**

He said it to the man of about thirty-three with a barrow and the man of about thirty-three with a barrow said that he had been wrong the first time and had been right the second time and would rather the boy did not ask him a third.

“**I said it was for the hundred paces. It is not only for the hundred paces. It is for anything you want to know the distance of, and the reason you want to know the distance of a thing is so that you can go and be near it, and that is all it is for, and I have been thinking about it for a fortnight and that is as far as I have got.**”

“**Then what is it not for?**”

“**It is not for deciding where anything goes. I have watched four men put eleven paces of stone a hundred paces down that sand and I could put a chain round the lot of it and call it measured and it would still not be anywhere, because nobody made it anywhere. A chain is a stick you lay on the ground to see how big the ground is. The ground is there either way.**”

**And that is a hundred and fifty-five words in three turns of one speech, of which the first is seventy-five, the second is six, and the third is seventy-four, and the man of about thirty-three with a barrow has carried glass for about nine years and has been in and out of four counties with it and has never in his life put a chain round a thing, and the boy of about seventeen with a rod wrote none of it down because he had not been told to and had nothing to write on and did not want to lose the putting down of it by writing.**

## Four

**And what came up the slipway after that was a barrow-load of glass and a boat that was going to be afloat again inside about four hours, and the chain was still lying flat on the wet sand with its slack in the middle, and nobody rolled it up, and nobody said anything about it, and nine people came and went over the top of it between the fourth hour and the sixth and stepped over the slack and not over the handles.**

A man of about fifty-three with a wagon came down to the boat land at about the fifth hour to load nine sacks and had to step over it twice, and Aren Kest was the only other person on that shingle who was not carrying anything, and the two of them did not speak, and neither of them was the reason the other one was there, and a man of about fifty-three with a wagon has been on that sand four times a week for nineteen years and has never once walked over a chain and has never once said anything about one.

“**That will be under the water at about the ninth hour,**” he said, to nobody, and four people heard it, and the boy of about seventeen with the rod looked up from where he was coiling a second chain that was not slack, and neither of them said anything else and the first chain stayed where it was.

**And on that shingle the fourth house's woman of sixty-three was not and the ninth house's man with the lease was not and the widow at the eleventh house was not, and none of the three has been told that a chain lay flat on a shingle at the landward end and went slack in the middle, and the driftwood in a post at the seaward end has twenty-two notches cut in the edge of it with the twenty-second not cut and the water did not go off its mark that day as it has not gone off it on the first of a month in this year.**

## Five

The cost of that tide is a column and it is the boy of about seventeen's, and it is the first one he has ever kept and he kept it on the back of a rod-measuring sheet with a stub of pencil he found in a shed, and a rod and a chain are not in it and are not instruments anybody rents for money on that shore.

**a loaf at one penny, a second loaf at one, a third loaf at one, a pound of cheese at four, a pound of wood at three, a tallow candle at two, a quart of small beer at two, a room at six, a bed at four, a sheet of paper at one, a second sheet of paper at one, a quill and ink at three, a brush at two, a knife at two, a pound of rope at six, a pound of nails at three, a handcart for the day at four, a broom at five, a basket at five, a pound of candles at four, a pair of gloves at two, a second pair of gloves at two, a waxed cord at four, an inkwell at three, a bushel of oats at four, a second bushel of oats at four, a second brush at two, a second room with a fire in it at eight, a second night's bed at four, a third night's bed at four, a bucket of tar at eight, a second bucket of tar at eight, a second knife at two, a second handcart for the day at four, a second broom at five, a second pound of candles at four, a second pound of cheese at four and a second tallow candle at two.** One, one, one, four, three, two, two, six, four, one, one, three, two, two, six, three, four, five, five, four, two, two, four, three, four, four, two, eight, four, four, eight, eight, two, four, five, four, four and two is a hundred and thirty-four pence. **A mark is forty-eight pence and a hundred and thirty-four pence is two marks and thirty-eight over**, and a boat of glass and nine loads and about eleven more on a shingle are not in it, and a chain is not in it and a rod is not in it, and nine cart-loads of shell a day off four boats into four pans are not in it, and about nine hundred people on two miles and about nine hundred paces are not in it and are not a figure of men.

**And a chain lay flat on the wet sand at the boat land with a slack in the middle of it that nobody rolled up and nobody wrote about, and a boy of about seventeen with a rod asked a man of about thirty-three with a barrow what a chain was for and was told that it is a stick you lay on the ground to see how big the ground is, and the glass went up the slipway in nine loads and four more and about eleven more stayed on the shingle, and a man of about fifty-three with a wagon said that the chain would be under the water at about the ninth hour to nobody and was right, and about the ninth hour the boat that had been on the shingle all afternoon was afloat again and riding at anchor two hundred paces out, and about two hundred paces of the seaward half was under, and it was out of it again before the light.**
