# Chapter 0179

The count was made by the four men and the woman, out of their own heads, over about two days, and there is no book in the world that says how it was made and there never will be, and that is not a fault in the count, it is the count.

Four men and a woman went to about nine hundred doors in eleven days. Nine hundred doors is four hundred households, and four hundred households is about four hundred people, and a household with one person in it is a household with one person in it, and a household with a family in it is a household with three or four, and nobody in that valley has ever counted its own people and the only figures anybody keeps are the number of stalls at a market and the number of beasts in a byre and the number of stones in a wall, and a man of thirty-four with a rope-belt on worked it out on the trestles of the room over the cross on the twentieth day of a cold in about nine minutes and got four hundred and eleven, and then got a hundred and ninety-four, and then stopped.

“About four hundred,” he said, “and the roll says about two hundred of the ninth, and the difference is the two hundred before them, which are all dead or somewhere else, and the roll has got a day in it and not a length of anything and I cannot get a year out of it, so I have got two numbers and they are both about right and I am not going to make them into one.”

“Nine hundred doors,” said Iven Sarr, from the third row.

“Nine hundred doors and about four hundred people and about a hundred and ninety-four of the ninth generation in this valley, and eleven of the ninth over a pass where I cannot ask them and cannot be asked for them, and the roll in your room over the byre has about two hundred lines in the ninth spread and every one of them is a birth, and the arithmetic is a hundred and ninety-four and I have got it by subtraction off a number you have never had out of that room.”

“Nine four in and about four hundred and not one of it is in a book.”

“None of it is in a book. That is the arrangement and I worked it out with a rope on and about four people in the room and I would like it said out loud that I worked it out in about nine minutes in front of a man of fifty-eight and a young man of twenty-two and that none of us wrote it down, and if you want to know how right it is then you cannot, and that is not because we have kept it from you. That is because there is nowhere in this valley to keep it and that has been the position for nine generations and everybody in this room has known it their whole lives.”

---

**And the three households said no, at three doors, in the same words by no agreement of any kind, and there were nine people in them, and nobody in the world will ever be able to tell you which nine.**

The first was the man of about fifty at the gate where the word was run out, and his reason was about four sentences long and it was said on a step in the fourth week.

“You have told me the six things and five of them are true and the sixth one is a question. My grandmother stood in that aisle when the benches were put in and it took nine minutes and it was the worst nine minutes of her life, and she is alive and she is sixty-one and she has told me about it four times, and I have never once said to her that I would not do it, because there was never any reason to, because it never came up. And you are asking me about a winter on the word of a man with a stick that nobody in this valley has ever asked the name of, and I have a bench with a hundred and forty cut into the end of it that my family has never been struck off, and you are asking me to agree to a thing that will not go in that room, and I have not got a better reason than that and it took me about four seconds.”

The second was a man of about forty in the ninth generation, at a door two steadings below the third generation's house, and his reason was one sentence and he said it before the six things were finished.

“I have been in that book since I was born and I have never been asked anything, and I am not going to be the first one in nine generations to be asked, and you can write me down as one of three and you cannot write down why, and if you write down why then I will change my mind, and I have thought about that for four days and I have decided that I do not want to be a man who changes his mind for a piece of paper.”

The third was the man who makes nails, and he was the last of the three that anybody found out about, and he gave his reason out loud at the market in front of eleven people in the fifth week, having said no at his own door two days before and told nobody, and he said it because he is a nail man and a nail man says things at markets.

“I have wanted to go out of this valley for nine years and I have said so out loud twice to a stranger and once to a woman with a slate, and I have a brother-in-law nine days off down the low country who has offered me twice and my wife has said no twice, and I have never said yes because there is no road and no way to carry anything.” He put the tally back the other way up on the back of the stall, which is a thing he does when he has said more than he meant to. “And the first family out of this valley is going to be a family that has a reason and a place to go, and I am the only man in nine miles who has said that out loud in a market and I would like it noticed that I have noticed it. And I am not standing to a thing that puts me at the front of that queue. I would rather be one of the three than be the first, and I have never in my life been the first of anything and I am not going to start in the fourteenth week of a cold in a valley that cannot leave.”

Nobody at that market argued with him, and about nine of the eleven people there agreed with him on the ground that he had said it out loud, which is the only way anybody in this valley has ever agreed with anybody about anything.

“And there is one more thing about the three and it is the part that is going to be in trouble in nine years,” said Wystan Roole, in the room over the cross on the twentieth day. “The count came out of four heads. I have got mine and I have got the woman of the Low Holm's and I have got the two men's, and we did it in about nine minutes and none of us wrote it down and none of us can be checked against the other three, and that is the same as the ninth spread, which has about two hundred of one mark in it and no name on any of them, and it is the same as the form, which has got a column for a figure of men going out and a column for a name and the valley filled in neither.

“Three households. And I have got a roll of people and not a roll of households, because that is what the keeper of it has kept for a hundred and forty years and he is not going to change it in a cold week, and he is right and I would not ask him to. So the three households have to be turned into a number of persons, and the three of them are a man of about fifty, a man of about forty, and the man who makes nails, and the man who makes nails has a wife and a mother-in-law who is sixty and is in the same household, and the man of forty lives with his mother and a brother of twenty-six and a sister of nineteen, and the man of fifty is on his own with a dog.

“That is three and that is three and that is five and I do not know what the ninth generation counts when it says *a household*, and I have not got a way of finding out that does not mean asking about ninety people what is in their house, and I have been in this valley nine weeks and I know what happens when four men ask ninety people anything.”

“Nine,” said Aren Kest.

“Nine. And I got it in about nine seconds and I am telling you that I did not get it right and I know I did not get it right, and that is the first figure anybody in this valley has ever worked out about the people in it, and it is wrong, and it is the first one, and there are going to be more of them and they are all going to be wrong, and there is nobody in four hundred miles who can come here and count us.”

**And then the man of thirty-two told the man of fifty-eight all of it in the room over the byre on the twenty-second day, and it is the longest single thing he has said in a room in this valley, and he said it because the man of fifty-eight is the only person in a valley of about four hundred people who can be told it.**

“There is a file,” he said. “In a county about two hundred miles off. It is a Crown office and it has never appeared and it is not going to and it is doing its job. That office has been printing one kind of paper for about two years, and the case the paper was printed in began in a county four hundred miles from here, and about two years ago the office sent it out asking every county for a count of what it owed in respect of people who had gone out of it, with a space for a figure and no space for a name, and it was answered in this county in nine minutes and the answer was a figure, and in the autumn that same office answered a different question with a different figure, in a woman's hand, with no seal and no number and no date on it and no addressee, and the figure was two thousand six hundred and forty, and there is no way on this earth to check that figure, because the only way to check a count is to count the district, and a district is a thing a Crown's hand is for, and this valley is not in one.”

“What does it owe,” said Iven Sarr, and he said it in the voice of a man reading a column heading off a form.

“It does not owe anything. The word on the paper is *continuing obligation* and the office's own hand at the foot of the paper says the obligation is held by a person who has gone out of a district and whose name is entered against it in any record of the district. That is nine words printed at the foot of a form in a small type, and it has been there since the first of a year before you were thirty, and nobody in that kingdom can say why, and I have not explained it to you now and I am not going to.”

“Two hundred years ago there was a form under my byre,” said Iven Sarr, “with the same nine words at the foot of it in the same small type, and you told me you had read that wording before and that you were not going to describe it to me in a room with no page in it, and it has taken me eleven days to work out what you meant, and I have not worked out whether you were right.”

“I was right and I would do it again and I would like it noticed that being right is the part I cannot account for.”

He put his hand flat on the shut book, which is what he does, and then took it off again.

“Now the thing I have come up these nine steps to tell you, and I am telling you and not telling the council, and there is a reason and the reason is that you are the only person in this valley anybody can serve a paper on and I would like to know what you would do if one came.

“That count is the only thing in the world that can be produced against anybody in this valley. Not the book. The book cannot be served, because nobody can be shown where it is and you have not let it out of that room in nineteen years and you are not going to and I have not asked you to. Not the form. The form is under a stone in a room with a squared stone on it and the stone is on crooked and no court in this kingdom would come four hundred miles to look at a floor. The count is the only paper, and a figure is the only kind of paper that cannot be refused, because you cannot refuse a figure. You can refuse a man. You can refuse a county. You cannot refuse two thousand six hundred and forty.

“And when the winter is taken out, that figure does not go anywhere. It is the figure of a valley that held a continuing obligation, and when the thing is taken out of the ground under this valley there is no obligation any more, and the figure will be the figure of a valley that acknowledged an obligation and did not discharge it, and it will be in a file in a county that has a door on it, and in nine years somebody in that county may produce it against anybody in this valley whose name happens to be in a column somewhere, and there is not one name in this valley in any Crown's hand and there has not been one in that county since a day at the end of a month it keeps, and this valley has never heard of that month, and I am telling you that as the best fact in this case and not as a small one.”

He stopped there.

“So do not tell them.”

“Do not tell them what.”

“Do not tell them what is in the file. Do not tell them that it is there, do not tell them the figure, and do not tell them the words at the foot of the form, and I have given you all three and I have not given you one of them to a room, and you are a man of fifty-eight who has wanted this valley to be told a thing for six years and I have just asked you to do less than you did nine days ago.” He put the stick down against the trestle. “And here is why, and I am going to say it in about four seconds and I have been in this valley thirteen weeks and I have not said a true thing in a room in nine weeks. A count is the only paper that survives. Everything else in this valley falls down. A bench is in a house. A book is in a room. A form is on a floor. A slate is round a woman's neck and a stall is at a market four times a year and a thing somebody said at a gate is gone in about nine years. And the one paper that cannot be got at is the one that is not on any of them, and there is no such paper, and I have looked, and the nearest thing to it in this valley is a sheet of rag paper with four lines on it about a keeper that was copied out by a man of twenty-two nine days ago on a scrap about the size of a hand, and it names nobody, and it is in a room, and a room is not nowhere.”

Iven Sarr stood at the trestles for a while and did not say anything, and the light in the room did not do what light does in a county that keeps its hours, and there was one window about the size of a man's hand and the fire was not lit because it had not been lit since the morning and neither of them had noticed.

“Nineteen years,” he said at last. “Nineteen years I have refused to let that book out of this room. I have refused a man with a cart, and a woman from four valleys off, and a stranger from a county that has a press in it, and a road warden with a limp, and my own wife, and I have never once given a reason for it to anybody except the man with the cart and I did not give him one then either.

“And the reason I was doing is that I did not trust them with it. That is what I have been telling myself for nineteen years in about four words, and it is not a very good reason and I have never once put it in ink. It was not that I did not trust them with it. It was that I did not want it to be anywhere that it was not under me, and I have wanted that since I was nineteen years old, and it is the first want I ever had in my life and I have never once said it out loud, and I did not know until this afternoon that it was the only thing standing between about two hundred people and a figure I have never seen.”

He put his hand flat on the book and left it there.

“That is not a want to be proud of at fifty-eight,” he said, “and it is not a want I am going to give up, because the reason it worked is the same reason it is a want, and if I let that book out of this room it is gone, and if it is gone then about two hundred people in this valley are standing in a market with nothing behind them at all. And what I want now is different and I have not had it before, and I am not going to say it in this room, and I am going to be a man of fifty-eight for a while yet.”
