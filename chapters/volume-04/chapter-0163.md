# Chapter 0163

There is a mark on the back of the last leaf of the ninth spread, the size of a thumbnail, low down and a little to the left, and it is not ink.

It is a depression. You can see it in daylight with the leaf held up and you can feel it with a thumbnail, and it is the mark of something pressed into a wet sheet of paper while the paper was still wet, and it came off a mould, and it is as old as the leaf.

“Show him the other side of it,” said Iven Sarr, and turned the trestle round, and the stranger came round it and looked at the back of the last leaf of the ninth spread of the only roll in a valley of four hundred people.

“Once a season,” said Sarr. “Since the fifth year of my keeping. I have not shown it to anybody in six years.”

“That is because you have shown it to three people in nineteen years and one of them wrote it down wrong.”

“That is because I have shown it to four people in six years and three of them are dead and one of them writes to me once a season about the weather and has never asked me a word about it, and the fourth is a man with a cart in a copy four hundred miles off.” He put his finger near it and did not touch it. “He called it a press-mark. He was in this room about two hours in the third year of my keeping and he had a good eye for a thing that was not a hand, which is more than I have ever had, and he said the book was printed at a house two valleys over and that whoever printed it had had nine generations of a valley's book on one press.”

“Is there a house two valleys over with a press in it.”

“There is a paper house about four valleys over and I have been down to it once in my life, in a warm week, with two of my wife's cousins, and it is the only place I have ever been where a thing that people use came out of a machine that made the same thing all day.” He took his hand off the leaf. “So the man with the cart was not far out on the paper. He was far out on everything else, and everything else is what people do with a mark once it is in a copy.”

**And a man of thirty-two who has read four hundred books in his life sat on a stool in a room over a byre and gave the only opinion he has given anybody in six weeks, and he gave it once, and he did not improve on it.**

“I have seen that mark a thousand times,” he said. “It proves a sheet. It proves a mould, and a house, and a season when somebody's vat was the right heat, and it does not prove a person, and there is not one thing on it that anybody can be made to account for.

“Your third column proves no person either. That is the difficulty and it is a different difficulty. A press-mark is a mark of a thing that was made, and you can hold it up and say what made it, and when the making stops nobody is anybody's business. A hand is a person. That is all a hand is. It is the one mark in the world that cannot be made by a machine, and that is exactly why you cannot get a person out of one.

“Those two marks are on the same leaf four inches apart, and the one that proves a house is in a copy in a county four hundred miles off being the reason a valley is somewhere it has never been, and the one that proves nothing is the one you have been looking at for six years.

“I am not going to guess what it is. I want that written on the page, and I am not going to write it on a page, so you will have to hold it. A guess in a copy is a name in a column with a different kind of face on it, and I have seen a thing that was wrong get copied eleven times in one case, and the wrongness was never the damage. The damage was that after the eleventh it was in eleven places and not one of them could be checked against the other ten. I am not going to be the eleventh.”

“Eleven,” said Iven Sarr, and looked at the stool.

“It is not a figure about anything. It is a number of copies, and I have not checked it, and I am not going to.”

**And the reason there is a bundle of about forty leaves tied with a piece of tarred hemp on the trestles of the room over the byre is that the man of fifty-eight has been down into the chest under the boards of the byre, which is where the winter book lives in the summer, and has brought one up, and has put it down without untying it.**

“That is what is left of the copy,” he said. “He tied it up himself in the yard before he went, and he tied it up well, and there are nine generations of this valley in it in somebody else's hand, and the fifth generation in it is wrong, and there is a name in it that is spelled two ways on two pages and I have never known which of the two is the right one and I have never asked him because he has been dead about nine years.

“He wrote to me twice after. The first letter asked whether I wanted the fifth generation corrected and I sat in this room with the letter in my hand for about four days and I did not answer it, because a corrected copy is a second copy, and the second copy is better. That is the truth of it and I have not dressed it up. A copy that is right about the fifth generation is a copy, and there is no way on this earth to get a wrong fact out of a book that is right about a hundred other things. I let a man have a false fact about my valley for about nine years of his life because the other option was that he had both.

“The second letter said he was sorry about the fifth generation, which was a thing nobody asked him to be sorry about, and then it said that a person who has got a thing wrong in a copy ought to say so in the copy, and I have never been able to decide whether that is the best sentence anybody has ever written about a book or the worst, and I have had sixteen years.”

He put his hand on the bundle without untying it.

“You may not take it out of this room either. I am aware that I am a man who says that about two things in one afternoon and has never said it about a person in his life.”

“You are the fourth tread,” said Aren Kest, getting up off the stool. “There are nine and four of them are soft.”

“There are nine and one of them is soft and it is not a good stair and it is the stair I put my wife's grandmother on and off that top room for eleven years and I have never had it out and I am not having it out now, and if you tell me it is a hazard I will put you down the outside of it.”

**And the fourth tread of the outside stair went under a boot before the light had properly gone, which was earlier than either of them expected, and the man who came up it had not been in the room over the byre in six weeks and had come anyway.**

“You are the man off the road,” said Wystan Roole.

“Yes.”

“That is not a question and you have not answered one, and I have not asked one, and we are going to keep that arrangement for another hour and then it is going to stop working, so I will say the thing I came for and then you can decide whether to be civil about it.” He had his hands in a belt that was not a belt, it was a piece of rope with a hook on it. “There is a byre at the Low Holm that has a bank behind it that has been going for about nine years, and I have sixteen men in the room over the cross and about thirty under it, and none of them has had a job to do in this valley since before I was twenty, and I have asked twice for a road and been told twice that there is no road to build. So we are going to build a wall.”

“You have not asked the woman whose byre it is.”

“I asked her in the middle of the week and she said she would think about it, and she has not thought about it.” He said it the way a man says a thing he has thought about. “That is not a no. And I am not going to wait a whole winter for a woman to think about a wall, and I will put it plainly so that you have it right the first time: I am not doing this because she asked. I am doing it because a wall is a thing I know how to do and there is a bank going at a byre, and those two facts have been in the same valley for nine years and nothing has come of them.”

“And you have brought this to a man who can read a floor.”

“I have brought this to the only man I have met who can read a floor in four seconds from a doorway.” He looked at the trestles and at the book on them. “And you read a book this morning, because the woman over the byre told me about it before I got to the yard, and I am not going to ask you what is in it and I do not know what a hand is in a book. I have never needed one. I have needed a floor about four times this winter and I have not had one.”

“About three faults in a floor in four seconds finishes the first day,” Aren Kest said. “You said that, and then you said you were not going to ask me to do anything, and you wanted it understood that you had not asked.”

“I did.”

“You are going to ask me to do something now.”

“I am going to ask you to do something now, and I have not asked anybody in this valley for anything in four years, and I would like you to notice that it took a stranger in a room with a book in it before I could do it, and I would like you to notice the room and not the stranger, because the room is what did it.” He put the rope down on the trestle end and squared it with his boot. “Come down to the Low Holm in the morning. Early, because the light is not worth anything on that bank until the ice has gone off it and that is about the fourth hour, and I am not having you standing about in the cold looking at a wall like a man at a fair.”

**And the best part of a wall nobody asked for is the morning you find out what it is made of, and this one was made of two walls.**

The stone came out of a field wall at the top of the bank that had been coming down for eleven years and was due to be taken down anyway, and eleven men took it up in a line in a single morning and stacked it, and it was field stone, which is to say it is not one stone, it is whatever the ground was, and about a fifth of what came out of that wall was not fit to go into anything and went down the bank, and nobody minded, because the wall had been standing on the wrong ground for eleven years and had a drain behind it and the whole of it was coming out anyway.

The timber came off a byre roof two steadings up that had a hole in it the size of a man's body and had had the hole since the summer, and eleven rafters came off that roof and went on a barrow, and one of them was rotten at the end and was cut off and the offcut is still in the yard at the Low Holm, and nobody has moved it, and it will be there in the spring.

The lime came up from the low country before the frost, in three loads, on the backs of four men who had gone over the pass in the warm weeks with nine bushels of barley each and had come back with the barley gone and the lime in its place, and the valley has no coin and has a price, and the price is a number a man from the low country says out loud, and the woman with the slate wrote it on the slate when he told it to her in the yard, and nobody in that yard argued with her about a number.

And the line.

That is the part a man off the road stood and looked at for a while, because he has pegged out nine hundred yards of approach and a mile of boundary and a causeway in four counties and he has never once pegged out anything that was not going to be a road.

Three pegs and a string. A peg at the corner of the byre, a peg at the far end of the line where it came back to the wall, and a third in the middle because he did not trust the two of them across that span, and the string four inches off the ground the whole length of it, because there was no other way to keep it up on a bank in a wet week, and it was a dead straight line fifty yards long from one end to the other across a piece of ground that had a curve in it of nine inches.

Nobody in that yard had seen anything like it. That is not because it was clever. It is because it is a straight line, and there is not one straight line in this valley, and the reason there is not one is that there has never been one use for one, and about a hundred and forty yards of hedgerow and a gate frame and a byre wall are the whole of the works of this valley in nine generations.

And then the string was up, and it was a bad bank, and an hour after the line went in he got down on it with both hands on the string and his face close to the ground to see where the water was going, and the water was going exactly where you would expect it to go, and there was a hole in the byre wall at the low end about a foot and a half above the bottom of his footing, with a stone lip on it and about four feet of square stone set into the wall on either side of it, and it was not a hole, it was a mouth, and a square one, and somebody had cut it and had dressed it.

“Where does that go,” he said.

“To the beck,” said the woman with the yoke, from the byre door, without coming out.

“And the footing of this wall is a foot below that.”

“It is.”

“So the water has to come out through the wall.”

“It has for sixty years and it has not come out through the wall, it has come out under the wall, and the wall has not been there long enough to know that, and you have not dug yet.” She put her arms in her sleeves. “You are going to ask me whether you can put a wall on top of my drain.”

“I was going to ask you where the water goes.”

“You were going to ask me whether I knew, and I have known since before you came and I have not said it to anybody, and I am saying it to you now because you have asked me one question and I would rather it was the right one. I have not said it to him.”

He looked down the line at the man with the rope at his belt.

“I have seen it,” said Wystan Roole. “I saw it before the string went in. I have known about it since the middle of the week. I have not worked out what I am going to do about it and I have not told her, because a man who is about to build a thing and has not worked out the bottom of it should not have asked her for it in the first place, and I did, and that is the whole of what has gone wrong here and it is mine and not hers.”

“And if it cannot be done.”

“It can be done. Every wall anybody has ever built that has water behind it has a way out of it, and the way out of this one is a hole in the bottom course with a lintel over it, and that is not a compromise, that is a job. A hole in a wall with a dressed lintel over it is stronger than the same wall without the hole, and I have built four of them.” He turned the rope over in his hands. “I have not built one in nine years, because there has not been one to build, and I am not going to have this one not get built because I have not had the practice in a while, and I will cut it myself in about ten days and I will not use a man's hands for it, and she can watch me do it.”

“You can cut that in a morning.”

“In an afternoon, and it will take me until the light goes, and I will do it in the wrong place first and put it in the right place after, and that is how a man does a thing he has not done for nine years.”

“You have never been wrong about anything you said in that room,” said the woman of the Low Holm, and went in, and the door was not shut behind her, because the door of a byre in a working yard is never shut behind a person in a working yard.

“You have never been wrong about anything you said in that room,” Wystan Roole said, to nobody, four seconds after the door.

“You have never wanted a straight line in your life,” said Aren Kest.

“We have never had anything where a straight line was the answer,” said Wystan Roole. “That is the whole of it and I have said it to a council twice and been told twice there is no road to build, and I am not going to say it a third time to anybody in a cold room. I am going to say it with my hands, starting tomorrow, and if somebody in this valley wants to argue with me about it they can come and stand on the other end of that string and hold it while I dig.”

Sixteen men. The string was up by the fourth hour and the frost was still on the top of the bank, and the first barrow-load of stone came off the field wall at about the fifth hour and went into the footing at the corner, and there is nothing remarkable about a footing.

That is a thing to notice, by the way. In four counties he has worked in, a footing is a thing people look at. In this yard at the fifth hour of a morning in the twelfth week of a cold in its ninth generation, sixteen men and a boy of twenty-four who had never had a job put a barrow-load of field stone into a footing at the corner of a byre, and not one of them looked at it, and the only person who looked at it was a stranger from four hundred miles off with a stick he had no use for and a leg that is worse on a bank than on a floor.
