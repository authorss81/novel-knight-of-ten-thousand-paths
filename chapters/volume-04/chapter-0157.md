# Chapter 0157

The eighth of Goatmonth, YR 313, was a Friday and it was not a market day, and the fourth hour is the hour the river is in its bed, and there is no market in this town today and there has not been one since the thirtieth of Hearthmonth and there will not be another one until the twenty-first.

The Old Toll crossing is four hundred yards up the road. It is in municipal trust, and the trust has been in abeyance since before the bench existed, and the tolls on it are suspended by an instrument a court has found lawful and has said it has nothing further to say about at any time, and so the crossing has no owner and no money and no seat on that bench and no glass in the sill and no third seat, and there is a dressed block in the abutment and forty yards of tarred hemp put there on the fourth of Hearthmonth of the year before by four men and a boy.

He was on the sill at about the fourth hour with a bag over his shoulder and a stick in his hand and a notebook in the bag, and Bram Ottery was on the near abutment with his hat in his hands and a great deal of daylight on.

“You are early,” said Bram Ottery.

“I am at the hour I said I would be at.”

“I am early too and I have been here twenty minutes and there is nobody here to ask whether I am a fool, and I have decided that if I am a fool then I am a fool on a bridge at the fourth hour and not in a room, because a room would remember it.”

Nobody was on the crossing. There is a cart track up the north side that goes to a farm and about forty yards of it is on the crossing, and the cart had gone over at the second hour and would not come back until the eighth.

Bram Ottery said, “Do you want to say anything to me now, in case.”

“No.”

“Good. I want to be able to say that you did not say anything to me, because I have been asked before now by a man about to do a thing whether I would want to know, and I did not know the answer then and I have thought about it for a year and a half, and the answer is that I would want to know afterwards, and that I do not want to know before.”

He wrote the nine up on the sill with a pencil stub, on the flat stone, in about nine minutes, and seven of them took him about four seconds each and two of them took him the rest of the time and neither of the two got a figure, and he wrote the hour against each one because the trade says to and because in four years and a half he has never been able to find a reason not to.

Then he put the book away and put his hand flat on the dressed block in the abutment, which is the one thing on that crossing that is not going anywhere, and which is a block of stone that came out of a quarry about nine miles off and that a man paid for on a day, and he said the four things out loud, and they take about eleven seconds, and the man on the abutment heard all four of them.

“A line, and two hands, and a date, and a witness at this end who is not the person keeping it. That is the whole of it and I have said it in four counties and it has never once been improved on and it is the only instrument in this world that has ever opened a road anywhere.

“The witness is a man of forty-seven on a bench because he was elected and he is paid fourpence a week out of a box and he has said out loud that he can confirm that I was here and cannot confirm what I did, and he said that before he did it and I would like that in whatever he tells you.

“The road is not made to carry a person. That is the first of six lines on a board in a county four hundred miles off and it is not my line and it is not my condition and I am going to a place where nobody has written a condition at all, and I have thought about that on a step over a weigh-house for a whole afternoon and I am telling you about it because you are the witness and the witness is the only person in this arrangement who is entitled to know the worst of it.

“And I do not know what I am going for, and I am going, and both of those are true at the same time, and if a man asks you in nine years what I said on this bridge you are to tell him those eleven seconds and not the rest of it.”

Bram Ottery said, “Right,” and then, after a moment, “That is the first honest thing anybody has ever put on this crossing and it is on it at the fourth hour on a Friday, and I want to say one thing back and then I am going to shut up about it for a year.”

“Say it.”

“I am on that bench because I was elected and I have been on it four years and in four years I have not once known what any of the four other men on it were doing, and I could not tell you one thing about that yard four hundred yards down the street, and I voted for money for this crossing four times and I did not know what I was voting for. So I am going to be able to say that I stood here with a man I know at the fourth hour and he said eleven seconds, and I will not be able to say what any of it was for, and I would like that on my side as well as yours.”

He put his hat on.

The bell went at the fourth hour and a half.

It is a bell with a band of forty-one rivets round it and a dull red seam in the metal where the band was closed, and it is nine stone of green-black bronze, chest-high, split lip to shoulder, and there is a boy of seventeen on the near abutment holding one end of a line and a carpenter beside him who is a hand and not a keeper and has a bar he is not using. They rang it because that is the arrangement. The arrangement has been a boy and a carpenter since the ninth of Hearthmonth and it was a boy and a ferrywright before that, and a Monday is the day the far end of that line keeps, and today is a Friday, and nothing was struck four hundred miles off on a coping on a river this county does not have, and the two of those facts are in a day-book separately and are not in one sentence, because a book is a place where things get joined whether they were joined or not.

He was already gone when it went, and he heard about a third of it, and the coping on each side of that sill has nothing at all on it. No mark of a maker and no device and no rope in two loops and no yoke and no headstock and no striker, and a man of sixty-four in a county four hundred miles off swept round a tile on a coping for forty-one years and wanted it lifted, and this is not that coping and this is not that county and the man who swept it is not going to be sent for.

**And there is no month here and there is no weekday here and there has never been a month here, and the word for the year in this valley is a winter, and a generation is a winter, and there have been nine of them, and the ninth of them is arguing and has not been asked what it argues about.**

The four hundred miles in the letter is a figure in a room over a weigh-house two hundred yards from where he was standing, and it is the only number in this that anybody has ever put on paper, and he came down four flights of steps and he is not going to be able to say what it was.

It is not a door and it is not a light and there was nothing about it to write in a notebook, because there is nothing to write in a notebook about a thing you have to do with your hands. He put his right hand flat on the dressed block in the abutment of a crossing that has no owner, and there was the stone under the hand, and there was a man on a bank of wet stones with a leg that had gone four flights of steps, and then there was a girl with her hand on a gate bar.

**The first thing he saw was the light, and the second thing was the water.**

The light is not the light of a fen in the last week of a month this calendar uses. It comes off snow that is not white and off a sky the colour of the inside of a shell, and it does not get dark at the hour it gets dark in a county four hundred miles off, because there is no hour here that anybody keeps.

The water is not the colour of the Water in the Hearth Marches and it is not the colour of the Sable at Merefen. It runs brown and fast over a bed of stones, and there is ice on the edges of it, and the ice is old ice with a great deal of water in it and it is not a foot thick anywhere, and the water under it is doing about four feet a second in the middle of it.

He has seen a river in spate. He has seen a river in a night flood nine hours long with thirty men on a bank and a footbridge taking the whole of it because it was a lid. He has never in his life seen a river that is not doing anything at all except going, and that is what the Burn is doing, four feet a second, in the ninth week of a winter in its ninth generation, and it is the most ordinary thing he has ever stood next to, and it took him about four minutes before he could say anything about it at all.

**And then the first sound was a chain.**

A chain on a gate, four yards off, and a girl of about nine behind it with her hand on the bar. She was not pulling it and she was not holding it up. She was standing at a gate with her hand on the bar the way a child stands at a gate in a yard in a country where the gate is the child's job and has been since she could reach the bar.

There is no gate on a road here. The gate opens into a fold of ground between a byre and a wall, and through it there is a yard, and a stone room standing on the end of the byre with a stair up the outside of it, and over the room door there is a lintel.

It is a dressed stone about four feet long and it is the only dressed stone in that end of the valley he can see, and there is nothing on it. No mark. No inscription. No lettering worn almost away. No device, and no kiln mark on the back of it, and no rope, and nothing hung on it at all, and the only thing on the face of it is the row of chisel marks from when it was cut. A man in a county four hundred miles off has swept round a tile on a coping for forty-one years and wanted it lifted, and this is not that tile and this is not that coping, and there is nothing on this one to lift.

He looked at it for a while. Every bridge he has ever worked on or stood on has a bell on it, and a bell is a thing you can hear from four hundred yards, and there is no bell in this valley, and he has been here about six minutes.

The girl said, "You are not from the Burn."

"No."

"You are from the far side of everything," she said, "because there is nothing on the Burn that would let a person come in the way you came in, and I have been at that gate since first light and there has been nobody on the road all winter and there is not going to be anybody on the road, and that is not a thing I have to be told, it is a thing I am saying out loud because you are standing in our gate and I am nine and I have got a barrow to get out at the ford."

That is a better answer than he expected to get in his first six minutes in a place he cannot get out of in an afternoon, and he has not told her anything, and she has told him the whole of the valley's position in nine words and a sentence.

He wanted to ask her what the lintel was and why there was nothing on it, and he did not, and there are going to be a great many more of those in this valley, and the two that cost anybody anything both happen in doorways, and neither of them happens to him first.

He said, "What is your name."

"What is yours," she said.

So he said his.

She thought about it for about as long as a person thinks about a thing like that.

"You can have the name of the house," she said. "The house is the Low Holm and that is a place and you can write it down and it will not be me. And I am not going to be the one who says whether you are somebody, because I have not seen you before and nobody in this valley was sent for anybody, and if somebody in the market says they were sent for you I would tell them to go and ask the woman over the byre, and she would say the same as what I am saying."

Then she lifted the bar and went through, and he went through after her because the gate was up, and a man who stands outside a gate somebody has opened for him is a man who has been told where he is, and she had not told him anything, and the gate was only open.

The yard is about half the size of the yard on Weir Street. It is clean. There is a barrow against the byre wall with the handles worn shiny in the middle where hands go, and a stack of withies, and a ladder, and a churn, and a bench-board with a roof over it, and there is no crown's mould of grey wax nailed over any doorway in it and no seal of any kind on anything and no bell.

Under the stair of the stone room there was a woman of about forty with a yoke on her shoulders going out to the byre, and she put the yoke down in the middle of the yard, which is a thing a woman does with a yoke when she has decided to stop for a moment, and she looked at him for about as long as it takes to look at a wet man with a stick.

"You are wet through," she said, "and you have come a long way on that leg, and there is bread and there is a fire, and you will sleep in the room over the stair because there is nowhere else, and because that room is the room and not a room for a stranger, there is one room and it is not a room for anything.

"And I am not going to ask you anything and you are not going to tell me anything, and if you want to tell me anything you will tell me at the fire and not on the bank, because on the bank a thing is said to the river and at the fire a thing is said to a person, and we have not got the habit of saying things to the river in this valley and I would like it kept that way for a stranger for about a week."

Then she picked the yoke up and went to the byre.

The girl came back with bread and a piece of cheese and a cup of something hot, and there was a fire lit in a stone room over a byre in a valley four hundred miles from a county that is not on any map, and nobody in it knew his name and nobody in it had asked him for it and nobody in it was hungry, and the Burn went over its stones at four feet a second with old ice on the edges of it and a lot of water in the ice, and the lintel over the door had nothing on it at all.
