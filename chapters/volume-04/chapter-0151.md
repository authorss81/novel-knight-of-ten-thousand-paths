# Chapter 0151

The twenty-fifth of Hearthmonth, YR 313, was a Saturday and it was a market day, and the fourth hour is the hour the river is in its bed and the deck is dry under the third bay, and about forty men are on the Sedge Reach with stone in four carts.

He got up onto the deck off the gravel in three moves instead of two.

That is the whole of what a stranger would have seen and nobody in that yard saw it, and it is worth saying why it is three. A man with two sound legs goes up a two-foot bank in a stride and a hop and nobody turns round. A man with a permanent limp in the right leg and a stick he does not lean on goes up it in a stride and a hop and a pause, and the pause is the third move, and it is about a third of a second, and it happens on the way up and not on the way down, and it has happened every working morning for a month.

A stick is in his hand. It is a ferrier's stick, ash, four feet nine, with a rubber ferrule, and it was cut out of a fence rail in a county that is not on any map, and he cannot put a bar in a joint with it, and he cannot lean on it, because leaning on it puts the weight through the wrong hip, and that is what the stick is for now, entire. It is for four hundred yards of road. There are no four hundred yards of road on the Sedge Reach and the man with the eels crosses forty feet of gravel to get to it and the stick is still in his hand the whole way, because putting it down in a working yard is a thing you do at home.

The stone went down on the twenty-first and the twenty-second and the twenty-third and was being pointed on the twenty-fourth, because pointing is the one job on a bridge that is never finished on the day it is begun. It has rained about four times in three weeks and the lime is standing in the courses after them, and the fourth of the eleven tons is in the toe of the north pier and the rest of it is spread along the cutwater in a line a man could set a plank against.

---

**And at about the fifth hour a water-barge hand of twenty-seven came up the length of Weir Street in the rain and turned in at the gate of the guild hall over the wharf, which is four hundred yards from the water, and went up two flights of a stair, and stopped at the bottom of it and read a board.**

He was from the Hearth Marches and he had been in Merefen eleven weeks on a crew's account, and he was paid, and the pay was not much and it was every week and it was the reason he was still here. He poled and he steered and he got under the lee of a bank for four minutes and spent the rest of a night on a wheel, and the county he came from is a county that is not on any map in this kingdom.

The board is under the bench at the bottom of that stair and it is four feet by two and a half, and it is not lath and lime, and the thing nailed to it is a fair copy of a fair copy. It went there on the twenty-second of this month, three days after the original came down the line, and it is nailed on four corners with the nail heads left proud because a nail head you can get a thumb under is a nail head a man can take his thumb off, and the person who sent it to that hall was the keeper of records of the Bellweather Claims Archive, a body corporate with a door and a number at the north end of the town, and she sent it because a document addressed to a shelf is a document that ought to be where any person coming up two flights can put his hand flat on it.

The hand is not a clerk's hand. It is the hand of the man who keeps the only book in a county of four hundred people that nobody can be served with, and he has been keeping it for twenty-two years, and the man who wrote it is not the man who put it where Rhys could read it and there is nobody in that hall who will ever be able to say which of the two men it was that made the road.

Rhys read it standing, with his wet coat open and the rain coming off him onto the flags, and he read it the way a man reads a thing he has been told is not about him, which is slowly, and twice.

The first thing on it is a struck figure with a line through it and four sentences in the margin saying why the figure is wrong, and the figure is not a number of anybody and the margin says so in the keeper's own hand.

The second thing is six lines and a working on a second sheet, and the working is in pence and there is an addition at the foot of it and the addition is right.

The third thing is a line with nothing in a money column, and it is the only one of the six that has nothing in it, and the line is four men of a yard from a county four hundred miles off, on the council's account, at the guild's rate, with the word *none* against them, and the yard is named and the street is named because a name is a man and a street is a place, and the yard's first hand has the same surname as the man standing forty yards away on a bridge with a tool bag at his feet, and the guild's first hand objected to that on the board in the county it came from and would not have either name taken off.

Rhys got to the end of the page and stood there, and a man going up the stair with a coil of rope said *Move along, mate*, and Rhys moved along, and read the last third of it again.

It is four hundred and eight words and it is the first thing that has ever come north out of that county, and the whole of what it asks for is a jar of a wheat nobody there grows, with the carriage paid and the jar paid for, and a promise that the name of whoever keeps it will be entered in a book that cannot be served and not in a book that can.

Then Rhys said it, to nobody, in the voice of a man who has been thinking about a thing for eleven weeks:

“A man comes off a barge in a town and somebody asks him what his county gave away.”

---

**Nobody had to be asked. That is the whole of the road and it took about four minutes to open.**

He said it to the bench-clerk, who is a copyist in his own trade and has been a clerk for nineteen years of them, and who took it up the two flights himself because he is the only man in that hall who is allowed up into the press-room and he wanted to be in the room.

The keeper of records read the whole of it aloud.

Not a copy of it. The whole of it, in the voice she uses for facts, which is the only voice she has, and she read the fair copy and then she read the working on the second sheet with the addition, line by line, because the addition is the only part of a document anybody can check and it had never been checked by anybody but her.

She read it twice.

The first time was for the room, and there were four people in the room, and one of them was the copyist and two of them were her own clerks and the fourth was a man of about fifty who had come up two flights behind a carter who had brought nine crates of clay pipe to the wharf and who was waiting to ask about a boundary that was not a boundary and had been told to come back on a market day, and this was a market day.

The second time was for the clay man, and she did not say that she was doing it for him, and she slowed down about a third of the way through, and the clay man stood up out of the bench he was not sitting on and stood at the back of the room with his hat in his hands for the whole of it.

He is not the person the book is about. There is a kiln two hundred miles up the road that he has a brother at, and there is a file in a provincial town that has a figure in it about that kiln's district, and neither he nor anybody else in this room is in any of the four hundred and eight words, and the keeper of records read the whole of it a second time in front of him anyway, and that is a thing she will not be able to explain to anybody and did not try to.

Then she copied it.

That is the second half of the road and it is the half that has a figure in it. A fair copy of four hundred and eight words and a working folded with it, in this office's hand, in about two hours, on two sheets, with the addition left standing exactly as it stood in the county that did the addition, because the addition is the only part of that document that is evidence of anything.

She will not foot it. The addition on that sheet is not hers to foot and she has said so out loud in the room, and the reason she has said so out loud is that a keeper who checks a figure she did not compute and then says nothing about it has made the figure hers.

“There is one number on that sheet I have not looked at and am not going to,” she said, “and there is one number on it that anybody in this room can check in about nine seconds with a rule, and both of those are the right arrangement, and I would like it minuted that the keeper of this office has refused to add up a figure in a document from a county that is not on any map, and that the reason is four words long and the four words are *it is not ours*.”

Alish Renn wrote that down, and wrote down that she had said the room was not full, which is what the second copyist of the Bellweather Claims Archive has written down four times in four years and a half and will write down again.

---

**One copy went down the stair to the guild hall, and one copy went four hundred miles, and the one that went four hundred miles went on the same barge the man who read the fair copy had come in on, which is how a man who has spent eleven weeks on a wheel ends up carrying a county's account back to it on a Tuesday.**

The reason he wanted it, when the keeper of records asked him why in the doorway, which she did not, and which the bench-clerk did.

“I have been asked eleven times,” he said, “by men on a wharf and a man in a tavern and a woman at a fair who wanted to know what it was my county had given away. I cannot tell them. I was not in that room. I poled a boat. And a man who cannot tell you what a thing is gets told he is lying, and after the fourth time he stops saying he was not in the room and starts saying nothing, and then it is true that he has said nothing.”

“That is a good reason to want a copy,” said the bench-clerk, “and it is not the reason this office is making it at this office's cost, and I have written the second one down.”

“Then what is.”

“The second one is that a document that has been read out loud twice in a room with a door on a lane is a document that somebody can be shown, and this one has been in a press in this town for eight days and there is no name in it and there is no way to get at whoever holds it, and that is a thing I have heard about in four years and a half and I have not seen it twice.”

---

**And the fifth asking was put on the table at about the seventh hour of a Saturday sitting, and the answer was no.**

The reeve brought it up two flights in her coat, as she had brought it up two flights on Friday, and on Friday she had not put it, and she had asked for it to be entered that she had not put it, and it was in the minute in her own words with the hour against it.

She put it on the table on the Saturday morning because on Friday morning there was nothing in her hand worth putting, and because a bench that has been asked the same thing four times and has said yes four times is a subscription, and there is a sentence about that in this county's own minute in the words of a carpenter, and she had read it over twice on the way up the stair.

The asking is money, for the Old Toll crossing, four hundred yards up the road, which is in municipal trust and in abeyance since before the bench existed and has no owner and no money and no seat on that bench and no glass in the sill and no third seat. It has been asked four times and has been answered four times, and the four answers are in a subscription book with four names in it and two of them are the same man twice, and the chest has two marks in it and has had two since the ninth of Fallowmonth.

She put it down.

“Before anybody answers it,” she said, “I want the board read.”

The board is under the bench. It is six lines and they are the county's own words and they are in the hand of the man who keeps the minute, and they were written in chalk on lath and lime in a market town four hundred miles off in a room nobody in this room has ever stood in, and they are held in this office under a resolution of the twentieth of this month, and the resolution says they are held here and that the delegation is not to be sent for.

The bench-clerk read them, in the county's words, and did not translate them, and nobody in the room would have wanted him to.

*First. The road is not to be made to carry a person.*
*Second. No name is to be read out on the far side of the line by anybody from the north, and no name is to be written in the line's book at the north end.*
*Third. The north is not to send anybody who can be asked to come and look.*
*Fourth. The stone is not to be renewed in the north's presence.*
*Fifth. The road is kept by a hand at each end and a witness at each end, and the witness at the Marches' end is chosen by the Marches.*
*Sixth. The line is not to be opened on a day the Marches have not named.*

The room was quiet for about four seconds, which in that hall is a long time.

“The first of them is why a delegation cannot arrive at the ninth of Rainmonth,” said the reeve, “and the hearing stands anyway, and that is a different thing and I am not mixing them. The second of them is why the man who built that road is not the man who may write anything at this end of it, and it is the second of them and not the fifth, because I have had this out with a clerk in this room twice this week and I got it wrong both times.

“And the sixth of them is the door. It is not a lock. It is a door, and it opens on a day the other side has named, and a door is a thing you can be killed in, and I would like the minute to say that I said that and that I am the reeve of this county and I have said a great many things in this hall.”

The bench said no.

Not in four minutes. In about a minute and a half, and then the clerk wrote for about twenty minutes, and the reason given was the board and the first of the six lines and the second of them, and the second of them is the reason, and here is what is on the page:

*That the crossing this bench has been asked to fund is four hundred yards from this hall and is in abeyance and has no power in it to give, and that the asking is therefore a subscription and not a bench, of which this minute already holds a sentence in the words of a carpenter, word for word, of which there is no second version in this county or in any other. That the same bench has answered the same asking four times and has said yes four times, and that a body which cannot refuse is not a body and has never been a body. And that a fair copy of a county four hundred miles off is nailed to a board under this stair where any person coming up may read it, and that the first of its six terms is that the road is not to be made to carry a person, and that a road built to that term carries nobody, and that a bench of nine that has found money for a road that carries nobody has not been asked a hard question and has answered a soft one, and that the reeve is asked to enter that this is the fifth asking and that the number of yeses is four and that the number of times this bench has been asked the same thing is five and that those two numbers are both in this minute.*

Aldis Fenn read that back herself and did not improve on it, and one line of it is hers and the clerk knows which and did not put his own name against it.

The chest has two marks in it and has had two since the ninth of Fallowmonth.

---

**He was on the roll. He has a vote. He did not use it and nobody asked him to and nobody has asked him anything, and a man on a roll of nine with a vote and not an office is everything this county has ever given him, and it is not nothing, and it is not enough to build anything on, and he has never once said so out loud in that hall and he is not going to start.**

He came in about the ninth hour, after the minute was read back, and the reason he came in was that he had put forty yards of tarred hemp into the abutment of that crossing on the fourth of Hearthmonth of the year before with three other men and a boy, and a man who has done work on a thing is allowed to hear what a room has decided about it.

The road is four hundred yards long and it is there.

He walked it afterwards, in the rain, on the left, with the stick, at the pace of a man who is not late for anything. It is four hundred yards of gravel and puddle and a dressed block in the abutment and a sill with no glass in it and no third seat and forty yards of tarred hemp under the stone, and a coping on each side with nothing at all on it, no mark of a maker and no device and no rope, and it is a hundred yards from the market and four hundred from the bridge he works on, and it carries nobody at all today, because the thing it is for is a thing that has to be agreed, and the man who put the road in is the one man in this county who may not write a name at the north end of it.

And a boat came up the channel at about the ninth hour with a copy of a fair copy under a tarpaulin on it, going four hundred miles on Tuesday, in a man's hand because a man poled it here, and it carried a sheet of paper and a bag of salt and a boy of fifteen who copies the empty space under a number as well as the number, and it carried nobody at all, and the four hundred yards of gravel behind him carried nobody either, and the two of them went in opposite directions, and neither of them was going anywhere the other one was not.
