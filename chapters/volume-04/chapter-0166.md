# Chapter 0166

The market is on a day that is a market day, and that is the only date this valley has that everybody knows, and about two hundred people can tell you when the last one was and about eleven of them can tell you which day of what, and it is not a day of anything, because there are no days here, only the cold and the week of the cold and the market.

It is a wide place in the middle of the valley with a cross in it and a smithy and four trestles, and on a market day there is a barrow and three sledges on hard ground under the cross and one handcart, and a great deal of one kind of cheese, and barley, and a salt, and about nine pairs of gloves, and a man who makes nails and cannot sell them who has been at that stall four market days running and whom nobody has told to stop.

He put the tally up on the back of the stall, the other way up, which is a thing a man does when he has said more than he meant to, and he said the number out loud to anybody who wanted it, and it was nine the winter before and ten the one before that and eleven on the fourth day of the cold, and about four of them come back in the first warm week and the rest are somewhere else, and nobody in this valley can say where, and the four who come back will not say.

**And a man of thirty-two with a limp and a stick he had no use for got a young man of twenty-two of the ninth generation to stop, in a doorway off the wide place, at about the fourth hour, in front of about nine people, and he asked him the question in the plainest words anybody has ever used in this valley, and it took him four seconds to ask.**

“You have never been in that room,” he said. “The one over the cross with the benches in it. Have you.”

“No.”

“Would you like to be asked what you swore.”

It took the young man about four seconds. He had a hurdle over his shoulder and he put it down on the step, which is a thing a man does when he is about to say something he has thought about for a long time, and he did not raise his voice, and he did not say it to the market, he said it to the man, and he said it once.

“No,” he said. “And not in that room.”

**And that is the whole of the refusal and it took four seconds, and it was the correct answer, and it is the engine of this volume and not a defeat, and about eleven people in a wide place in a valley four hundred miles from any county that is on a map heard it inside a minute and nine of them agreed with it before they had worked out what it was about.**

The man off the road did not argue with him, and he had an argument ready and it was about two hundred and eleven names in a column, and the reason he did not use it is that the young man did not know there was a column and the argument would have been about a book and not about himself.

“Why not in that room,” said Wystan Roole, from about four feet away, in the voice of a man who has a wall to go back to.

“Because of the benches,” said the young man. “Have you been in there.”

“No.”

“Then you have not seen the numbers cut into the ends of them. And when a person stands in that aisle and says something, a man puts a stroke on a bench, and that is how it is known, and that is the only way it is known, and it has been that way since before the bolts were in and it is the only reason anybody in this valley has ever believed anybody about anything.”

“There is a woman of sixty-six at the upper water who would stand in that aisle tomorrow,” said Aren Kest. “She was in it forty years ago. She has said she will do it in front of the whole valley, and she has said it in front of me, and I am not able to say she will not.”

“She is sixty-six and it is her bench,” said the young man, “and she is not twenty-two.”

And that was the end of that, and it was the end of it in nine seconds, and the man of thirty-four who had been counting the rooms in his head for nine years did not get another word in before somebody at the back of the wide place said out loud, to nobody, that the ninth generation was not going to be asked anything, and about six people agreed with that too, and the market went on.

**And about nine of the people in that wide place had not come for it, and one of them said so out loud, which is the ordinary way a thing like that goes in a place with no writ in it.**

"I have no interest in that," said Wystan Roole. "I have been interested in a road for nine years and I have been interested in it in every room in this valley and I did not come to a market this morning to be a good man in it. I came because a wall needs sixteen men and I need to know whether there is going to be a valley here in ten years, and now I know there is, and that is worth a morning."

"You have known there was a valley here in ten years for nine years."

"I have known there was a valley here next week. There is a difference and I have never been able to make anybody in this valley hear it and I have stopped trying at it, and if you are the first person who has heard it then you may have it for free." He put his hand on the hurdle and took it off the boy without asking, which is a thing a man of thirty-four does to a man of twenty-two. "Get that up, you are letting the cold in."

The man who makes nails had turned his tally back the right way up and had not said anything at all, and about an hour later, when the barrow had been and gone, he said one thing to the man off the road that was about the market and was not about anything else.

"Nine is a good number for going over," he said. "Eleven is a bad one and it has been bad for three years and I have written it down on the back of a stall where anybody can see it. And I will tell you the thing I have never told anybody, and it is that I would go tomorrow, and I have a brother-in-law in a town about nine days off down the low country who has offered me twice and my wife has said no twice, and I have not said yes, and if this valley stops being a valley in a week then I am the first one out of it and I have known that for two years and I have never said it in a market."

"That is a thing to say in a market."

"It is a thing to say anywhere and I have said it here because you are a stranger and a stranger cannot be at the next market."

**And about an hour afterwards, when the wide place had emptied, the man off the road stood at the cross with about four people and worked out something that had been standing in front of him since the ninth week of a cold, and that he had never once said out loud to anybody, in any country, in four years and a half of doing this.**

There are four words for a person in this valley and they are not four versions of one thing, and about nine generations of people have gone on treating them as though they were.

A carrier is a person who has gone out of this valley and been named for it. That is a fact about a road and it is nothing else.

The hand is a person who keeps a thing that is not made of words. That is a fact about a wall and it is nothing else.

A keeper is a man who maintains a place or its records. That is employment. It is not authority over anybody, and Iven Sarr has not been able to tell anybody what to do in nineteen years, and the reason he can keep a book is that he is good at it and not because anything in this valley gave him the right.

And there is a fourth word in the county I came from. It is for a person a community appoints to speak to one other person, and it carries consent inside it, and it is the only one of the four that anybody can be sorry for.

A man in a market in this valley had been called a carrier to his face on the ninth week of a cold by a woman at a gate, and no person in the valley had ever appointed him to do anything and no person had ever asked his leave, and the word got to four people by the sixth hour of that morning and to nine people by the next one, and there is not one form in this valley on which to enter it, and that is not a gap in the form. That is nine generations of a valley using one word for four different jobs and being careful, in the way a valley is careful, never to look at all four of them at once.

**And then a stranger of thirty-two with a limp said a sentence that came from a county four hundred miles off, and it was the only thing said in this valley all winter that nobody in the valley could check, and that is exactly why it is on the page.**

“A name in a column is a thing you cannot get out of,” Aren Kest said. “There is one man in this kingdom who ever got out of one, and he did it by writing on the back of a leaf, two years before anybody came for him, that he did not put his name in the column. He wrote it before he knew anybody was ever going to look, and by the time anybody looked it was already written, and that is the whole of how he got out of it.”

The wide place was quiet for a moment.

“What leaf,” said Nev Treen.

“A leaf of a return. In a county that has a stone in a field in it. He filled in a column he did not have to fill in, and the office relied on it, and it was four years before anybody came about it, and when they came he had already written on the back of the leaf that he had not put his name there, and the office had a whole leaf of returns with that column in it and had never once turned a leaf over.”

“That is the story of a man in a book,” said the young man of twenty-two.

“That is the story of a man in a book, and the reason I have said it in a market is that it is the reason I am asking you, and I have not got a better one, and I have had about a week and I have not thought of a better one.”

**And then the young man of the ninth generation put the second half of it on the step between them, and the second half is the whole of what this volume has to say, and nobody in that valley has ever worked out how to want a thing and not have a page, and neither has the man who said the sentence about the leaf.**

“I am not refusing the question,” he said. “I have never once said I did not want to be asked what I swore. I want to be asked what I swore. I have been in that book since the day I was born and there is not one thing in it about me that I did not choose, and I have never been asked anything at all.”

“Then say it here,” said Aren Kest. “Now. In this doorway. And I will not write it down and there is nobody here with a slate.”

“You have got a book in a bag at the Low Holm.”

“I have not got it. It is in a room over a byre and the man who keeps it will not have it out of that room, and I have not asked him for it, and if you and I say a thing in this doorway then in nine years there is no way anybody is ever going to know that you and I said it.”

“Then it happened and you did not have it,” said the young man.

**And the man of fifty-eight was standing at the edge of the wide place and had been standing there for about two minutes, and he had come down off the hill on purpose, and he had a slate under his arm, and he had brought it because there is no other way anybody in this valley brings anything anywhere.**

“That is right,” said Iven Sarr. “Both halves of that are right and I have been on the wrong side of the second one for nineteen years and I did not know that until a man of twenty-two said it in a doorway, and I have been asking this valley for things in a room with a bench in it since the day I was nineteen years old.”

“You could keep it and not read it,” said Nev Treen.

“I could keep it and not read it,” said Iven Sarr, “and then I would be the man who kept a book of what this valley said and never looked at it, and there is one of those already in a copy four hundred miles off and it has been wrong about a whole generation for about sixteen years, and I am not going to be the second one of that. So no.”

He put the slate down on the trestle by the nail man and left his hand on it.

“Right,” he said. “Then here is what I have, and I am going to say it once, and it is not a want any more, it is a job, and the two of you can do with it what you like.

“I have wanted that book read for six years, and I have said so in this valley about nine times and I have never once been asked what for. And what it is for is not a reading. It is a room. A cold room, in the cold, with about forty people in it, and a man of about my age at the front with the book in his hands, reading all of it out, and not the parts that have held. All of it. I have never once wanted a person to be asked anything and I have wanted that for six years, and I have not known which of the two things I was asking for, and it has turned out that they are not the same thing and I have had them confused for nineteen years.”

Nobody in the wide place said anything, and about nine of them were still standing there, and the sledges under the cross had their chocks put under them, and the barrow went out at the ford.

“You are not going to be asked anything,” Aren Kest said to the young man. “That stands. I have heard it and I am not going to come back with it in a week in a different coat, and that is the first time in four years and a half that I have heard a no that I was not going to argue with and I have not got a better reason for that either.”

“You have got one,” said the young man. “You have not written it down.”

“That is the first honest thing anybody has said to me in a doorway in six weeks,” said Aren Kest, “and you are twenty-two and you have never been anywhere, and I have been told about three hundred times that I do not ask, and I have never once been told why by the person it was about.”

**And the young man of the ninth generation put the hurdle back on his shoulder and looked past him at the man of thirty-four and the man of fifty-eight and the woman of sixty-six, and then looked back at the stranger.**

“What is a page for,” he said.

Aren Kest stood in a wide place in a valley of about four hundred people with a stick he had carried four hundred miles for four hundred yards of road, and he had been asked a question by somebody who was owed an answer and could not have one, and he had been asked it in the open, in front of eleven people, at the fourth hour, on a day this valley could name.

“I do not know,” he said.

He said it in about a second and a half, and it was the second true thing he had said in a room in that valley in about two weeks, and by the following morning about nine people in four steadings and the town had said some version of it to somebody else, and not one of them knew where it had come from, and there is no form in that valley on which to enter a thing somebody said at a gate, and nobody entered it, and by the spring there will be a woman in that market who will remember that a stranger came up the bank in a winter and that somebody asked him something and that he did not have an answer, and she will not be able to say what the question was, and no instrument in this kingdom can go and look.
