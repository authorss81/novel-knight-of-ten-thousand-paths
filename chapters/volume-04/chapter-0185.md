# Chapter 0185

**The nine weeks were sixty-three days and they were the first sixty-three days of anything in this valley since the ninth spread of the only roll in it was opened, and nine people can give you a date for the start of them and four can give you a date for the end of them, and the man of thirty-four gave both figures out loud in a yard on the sixty-third day in front of sixteen men because he had been taught that in a cold room in the second month of this cold by a man who counted load points and had not been able to count them.**

Eight of the nine roofs nine steadings up the bank were still open. There is no money in this valley and there is no family in it that has asked the bridgekeepers for anything since before the man of thirty-four was born, and a roof is for a family, and eight roofs in a cold are not a tragedy, they are a hole and a floor nobody walks on and a door a family shuts behind them until there is somebody in the yard.

The mill could get stone out of its own valley for the first time since the ninth generation began. That is what the four hundred yards was for, and it worked, and four people in nine miles said so out loud, and one of them was the mill man of sixty-one, who said it once, in a yard, in about nine words, and then went back to a wheel he cannot leave.

And the second stand went in over the fifth and sixth weeks in about nine days, and the ninth generation asked about it more than the ninth generation has asked anything in nine years, and none of it was about a stand.

They asked about the sills again. They asked whether a sill wants a sill wall under it or a footing in front of it, and they had asked that in the second month of this valley and got the same answer, and a man of about twenty-six said he had asked it in the second month and got the same answer and wanted to be told what the difference was between asking twice and not knowing that he had already asked, and the man of thirty-four said that the difference was nine weeks and a ford and a wheel that had not turned, and that he was sorry and that the answer was the same and would be the same in nine years.

They asked what the water does in the warm week. And the man of thirty-four said he did not know and gave the two things he did know, which were that the water in that cutting is four feet now and about five in the warm week in an ordinary year, and that the four hundred yards of the Burn below a ford have gone quiet since the first cold and have not stopped above the ford, and that he had tried to find out what quiet was in the ninth week of the first cold and had got nothing, and that he was not going to manufacture an answer in a yard in front of nine people and that a man who does that is a man who is going to be quoted.

They asked whether a stand wants a rail. And the man of thirty-four said it did and that he had not put one on and would not, and the reason was that a rail on a four-foot stand over four feet of water in a valley with nine people who would use it was a rail that four people would be able to lean on and lean a barrow on and set a pail on, and that the only person in nine miles who needed a rail was somebody carrying a pail and she was better off with a yoke, and a woman of about thirty-five of the ninth generation said, from the top of the bank, that he was wrong, and he said he knew he was wrong and that he had left it off on purpose because he wanted to find out whether anybody would say so, and she said everybody on that bank had thought it and had said nothing because a man of thirty-four does not take a rail off a stand, and he said that was true and that it was the ninth generation's fault and not his, and about eleven people in that yard heard two sentences about a rail and none of them will be able to say in nine years which of them was the argument.

**And on the forty-first day a man of thirty-two laid a floor in a trough at the mill the way he has laid about four hundred floors in four counties, and it was wrong, and he was made to lift eleven stones in front of sixteen men, and the reason is the best thing anybody said in the nine weeks and it was said once.**

He laid a floor the way a floor is laid in four counties. Big flat stones, the biggest first, the joints between them buttered with earth out of the bottom of the trough, and the thing bedded on earth, and it went down in about a day and a half, and it looked, to a man who has been building things for nineteen years, like the best floor he had laid in a year.

They found it on the forty-third day. It is not that it failed. Nothing in a floor in this valley fails in nine days. The mill man put a barrow across it on the forty-third morning with a stone in it, and the middle of it went down about an inch, and the barrow stopped, and the mill man got off it and stood in the trough and looked at it for about a minute and a half and then said the two words that had ended about nine conversations in this valley since the man of thirty-four started asking for a road.

“Who laid that.”

And the sixteen of them lifted eleven stones in about an hour, and the eleventh was the one that had gone down, and a man of about fifty-one who had lost an argument about plates in the second month of this valley and got it back four minutes later said the thing about it, and the man of thirty-four let him, and said afterwards that he had wanted it said by that man for about nine days and had waited for it.

“A floor is not a floor,” said the man of fifty-one. “It is a pitching. And a pitching is not stones laid on earth, it is stones laid on stones, and every stone in it wants a stone under it and a stone beside it and nothing at all under that. That is the trade and it is about nine words and there is no way round it.

“You have given every stone in that trough earth on three sides. Earth is not a stone. Earth moves. When the water comes in the warm week and the earth under the middle of that trough goes soft, that stone has nothing sitting under it any more, and it goes down, and the two stones that were lying on that one have nothing sitting under them either, and they go down, and that is what your floor does. Not all at once. About four years of it, one stone at a time, and the first one is the one you would have pointed at.”

He put his hand flat on the eleventh stone.

**“Take the earth out from under all of it and lay it on stones, big at the bottom and small at the top, and no stone in it with a gap under it you could put a finger into, and no earth in a joint anywhere in that trough. And when you have done it, put the barrow across it with a stone in the barrow and not an empty one, and if it goes down an inch I will take it up myself with my own hands.”**

The man of thirty-two laid the second floor on a bed of stones. It took two days instead of a day and a half, and it is the floor of the mill at the bottom of this valley, and it is the only floor in nine miles that anybody has laid in nine generations that was laid by a man from a county that has a word for it and got the word wrong.

The barrow went across it on the forty-sixth day with a stone in it and it did not go down an inch, and the man of fifty-one was standing on the bank and did not say one word about that either, and the man of thirty-four said afterwards that the silence was worth more than the sentence and that he had waited nine days for both.

**And the wages of the nine weeks went on a page in a book he carries in a coat, and the addition was done on the page, because his hand does that, and the valley's own price of the nine weeks is not a figure and has not been a figure since the first of the cold and is not going to be one.**

*Sixteen men, sixty-three days, at three pence the day.* 3, sixteen times over, is 48. 48, sixty-three times over, is **three thousand and twenty-four**.

*Two barrows of salt at fourpence.* 4, twice over, is **eight**.

*Three thousand and twenty-four and eight is three thousand and thirty-two pence, which is sixty-three marks and eightpence.*

That is the wages of sixteen men for nine weeks in a county four hundred miles off where a day of an unskilled man is three pence, and it is in a county's money and there is no coin in this valley and there has not been any in nine generations, and the sixteen men are not on the list and neither is he, because there is no money in this valley to put either of them on a list with, and the sixteen men and a man off the road ate for nine weeks out of nine kitchens and one beest and a quantity of something that four of the people who provided it call a root and the rest of this valley calls a word they do not spell, and not one household was asked twice and not one household was told what it was for.

The two are not added. There is no way on this earth to add them and a person who tried would be doing arithmetic on a thing to make it go away.

He did not put the figure on the end of the trestle in the yard the way he put one there in the second month of this cold, and the man of thirty-four asked him about that on the sixty-first day and he was told the reason, and the reason was about nine words long.

“Because the trestle falls down and the chalk is off it inside a year, and I will be gone, and a figure that a man off a road leaves in a valley in chalk is a thing that outlives him and means nothing, and that is worse than not having it.”

“Then where does a figure of yours go.”

“Into a book that stays in a coat,” he said, “and that is the only room I have got, and I have said that to four people in this valley and I am saying it to you fifth and I am aware of the number.”

And the man who makes nails was on the ground work for nine weeks of the sixty-three days, and he asked for it, and the man of thirty-four put him on the barrow run and took him off it in about four days, and the reason is in the yard in about nine sentences and it is not about nails.

“You are the fastest man I have got on a barrow and I have had about thirty men under the cross and not one of them can throw a barrow like you, and I am not going to have you on it, and here is the reason and I would like it said in front of the sixteen.

**“In the second month of this cold four of us went to about nine hundred doors and asked this valley to agree to a thing, and there were three households that said no and you were one of them, and you said the reason at a market in front of eleven people because you are a nail man and a nail man says things at markets, and the reason was that you have wanted to go out of this valley for nine years and that the first family out is going to be a family with a reason and a place to go, and that you would rather be one of the three than be the first.**

“And I put you on a barrow because I did not think about it. I had sixteen men and no work and a barrow run, and you are the best man I have got at it, and that is all of why. And then on the second day I watched you go up and down that four hundred yards nine times and every single time you went over the second stand and came back and looked at the low country past the top of the bank, and you did not do that on the first day and you did it on the second and you have done it on every one of the fifty-nine days since.

“I am not taking you off the job because you cannot be trusted with it. I am taking you off it because I have just built the only ground in this valley that a man can stand on and look out of, and in four months there is going to be a queue for it, and I am not going to have the man at the front of the queue working it for me in a cold for nine weeks while I have sixteen men who want the work.

“Come back to it in the warm week. I will put you on it then and I will not ask you why you went and looked.”

He took him off it, and the man went and put a nail in a stand at the bottom of a cutting for fifty-nine days, and four people noticed that he had stopped looking out past the top of the bank, and nine did not.

And on the fifty-first day a man of eighty-one came down to the ford for the first time in about a year, and he stood at the top of the four hundred yards, and there were about four young people of the tenth generation on it with barrows, and he watched them the whole way down and did not go past the top.

They are the first of their family that has never stood in the aisle over the cross, and so is everybody their age, and there are forty of them, and the man of eighty-one had counted them out of his own head in about nine minutes in the third month of this cold and had not told anybody and had been asked by the woman with the slate how he had got the figure and had said *by the fires*.

He watched a barrow go down four hundred yards and across a stand and come back, four times, and then he said one thing to a man of thirty-four and it was about nine words and it was not a finding and it was not in the room over the cross and nobody repeated it.

“You have built the first ground in this valley that a child can be sent down on his own,” he said. “And I am eighty-one and I have never once done that and I have watched thirty of the eighth generation do it, and it took nine years before I noticed it was a thing I had never done.”

And on the sixty-third day, at about the seventh hour, a man of fifty-five came down the bank to the ford with no barrow and no yoke and about nine other people behind him who had come because he had come, and he stood on the new ground and looked down the four hundred yards of it and did not say anything about the four hundred yards.

He had been in this valley fifty-five years and had been in about nine miles before that, and he was of the ninth generation, and nine people in this valley had never heard him say a whole sentence in a room, and two of them had heard him say one in the fourth month of this cold.

And he is the last man in this valley who has ever stood in the aisle over the cross, and he stood in it thirty-five years ago, and there is a stroke cut into the end of the bench at the back of the right-hand row for every one of the people who has ever stood in it, and it says a hundred and forty, and the number has not moved in nine years, and nobody has ever been struck off one of the twenty-nine.

Torrin Hatch put his name in the room himself, because the man of thirty-four had not asked him and the man of thirty-two had not either, and he did it in about four seconds at the top of the bank, standing up, and nobody had asked him a question and he answered it anyway.

“Torrin Hatch,” he said. “Fifty-five. Ninth generation. I stood in that aisle thirty-five years ago and I was asked what I swore and I have not told anybody what I said, and I am not going to tell you, and that is not a mystery, it is a thing a man of twenty does with a question like that, and I have had thirty-five years since to work out that I would take it to the grave and I have.”

He looked at the man of thirty-four and then at the man with the stick against the wall behind him, and the stick had been against the wall of the room over the cross for nine weeks now and about four people had come in and looked at it and nobody had asked.

“I want the council asked something, and I have been trying to get it asked since the first of the cold, and I have not got anybody to ask it and I have not got standing to ask it and I have known that for thirty years, and I have been getting a road built instead, which is a strange thing for a man to have been doing for nine years and I have only just worked out that it is the same thing.

**“I want the council asked whether it is going to keep the winter. Not this cold, which is kept whatever the nine of us do with our faces. I want it asked what happens if it stops being kept. And I want it put in the minute that the council was asked it and that nobody in the room could answer, and I want that in the minute and not in a conversation, because I have had about nine years of conversation in this valley and every one of them went the way you would expect.**”

“You want a vote,” said Wystan Roole.

“I want a vote in a room with the benches in it and not in the aisle, and I am the one who has to say that out loud, and I would like somebody to write down that I said it and did not go and stand in the aisle, because I know exactly what that would look like to about nine people and I am not going to do it and I want it entered that I did not.”

Nobody wrote it down, because there is nowhere in this valley to write it down, and a man of fifty-five who has just been told by nine people that he is the last man to have stood in an aisle and has just refused to stand in one is not going to be in a book this evening. He knew that before he said it. The room over the cross has twenty-nine benches bolted to the floor of it with a number cut into the end of each one, and about four people were in it who had never heard the two sentences spoken, and a girl of about nine came up the outside stair during all of it and did not knock and stood on the fourth tread and listened through the door, and the fourth tread said so under her and under nobody else, and she counted the benches from the top of the stair through a doorway in about a minute and a half and got twenty-nine and did not go in.
