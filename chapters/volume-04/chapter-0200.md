# Chapter 0200

**The twenty-fifth of Fallowmonth, YR 314, a Monday, and not a market day, and the fourth hour, and there were about forty men on the Sedge Reach with the fourth bay finished and being pointed and the road open, and a man of thirty-two with a limp was on the gravel at the low end of it with a barrow, and a man of forty-seven on the bench of nine because he was elected was on the deck of the first bay with a hod, and neither of them held anything.**

Thirty-five days. The fever had broken on the seventh morning and had left him with a leg that does not get up a bank any better than it did in June and does get up one a good deal more slowly than it did in the year before, and a mason of fifty and a half, who has been cutting stone for thirty-one years and who laid a floor in a trough at a mill in a valley nine days over a pass in a winter in its ninth generation and told him to take it up and lay it again on stones, looked at him once, on the second morning after the fever broke, and said four words and went back to his stone, and the four words were *you are slower now*, and he has not said anything about it since and neither has anybody else.

The fourth bay of the reach was finished on the twenty-second of Harvestmonth and had been pointing ever since, and pointing is the one job on a bridge that is never finished on the day it is begun, and the man who began the pointing on the parapet on the twenty-third of Harvestmonth went home at the fourth hour and the joint was half a foot short at the end of it, and it is a foot and a half short now, and nobody in that yard has measured it either.

**And there is a line in the third course from the low end of the fourth bay of that reach, nine inches long, and it was pointed over on the twenty-third of Harvestmonth at the joint and not at the stone, and it is under about half an inch of lime, and it has not moved and has not grown and has not closed, and it is the only thing on that reach that nobody in this county can account for, and it is the second, because there is a line and a bar and a figure cut into the coping of the first bay at the height of a thumb that has been there since the sixth of Goatmonth, YR 313, and the figure in it cannot be read from thirty feet by anybody in this county and never has been.**

He looked at the seam once. That was on the Monday morning he came up, with his hand on the stone before the rest of him was out of the joint, and he had said on a bank in a valley that he was not going to measure it, and he did not measure it, and a mason of fifty and a half offered him a rule on the Thursday and said, in about nine words, that a rule on that stone is a figure and a figure is a survey and he had not got a survey.

He did not take the rule.

And a man of forty-seven on the bench of nine because he was elected and not because he is on a trade has not been asked about it, and has not been asked because he is not the person who keeps the reach, and the reach is kept by that bench, and he is paid fourpence a week out of a private box and not out of the chest, and he has not used his vote since the day he was elected and did not use it then and has said in that hall nothing about that and is not going to start.

**That is the only thing in this volume about the thing in the stone course and it is not a sentence and it is not a finding, and there is nobody in this county who has put a figure against it, and there is nobody in a county four hundred miles off who knows it is there, and neither of those is going to change inside nine years and one of them will not change at all.**

---

**And about forty men were on that reach in daylight with the road open and the salt coming over the lane,** and the stone came in four carts out of a quarry eleven miles up the channel and it came one cart behind another all morning, and the carts were unloaded with a crane and a gin and four men on a rope, and the work of a reach in a fen county is that a cart arrives and four men on a rope know what the cart is for before the man in the cart has got down off it.

A mason of fifty and a half. Two men of about twenty-six setting a course. A boy of fifteen at the bench-board with a lime tub and a rule and a bill, copying the empty space under a number as well as the number. A carter of thirty. A man of about nineteen carrying a hod badly. And a man of forty-seven on the deck of the first bay with a hod, who is one of the nine of that bench because a man in a market asked who was going and he put his hand up and not because he is on a trade, who does not hold this reach or the Old Toll or the crossing, and who has not voted since the day he was elected and did not use it then.

**And on the second morning of this month he carried a hod for four hours and a half and he did it well, and about four men in that yard have said since that they did not know he could, and nine have said that they did not know it was him, and the bench of nine does not know he was there and has not been told and is not going to be, and he has not used his vote about that either.**

**And on the same morning the bench of nine sat over a wharf with the tide under it and read a minute, and the minute is the fifth thing in it that is not about a road,** and four people in that hall have said that the fifth of them is the best entry in twenty-two years, and the man who wrote it has been a clerk of about sixty for twenty-two years and is not a member and has never been asked to be.

The bench has been asked five times for the crossing four hundred yards up this town's own road and it has said yes four times and it is not a bench and has known that longer than anybody in the room has been alive, and the first asking is the first of the year before last and the fifth asking was brought up two flights of a stair on the last day of that year by the reeve of this town, who is a magistrate's elder sister and does not vote on the guild's own matter and has not for nine years, and who did not put it on the table.

**That is in the minute in about eleven words, in a clerk's hand, and the clerk read it back twice and did not change it, and the reeve asked for it to be entered that she did not put it, and the number of times this bench has been asked the same thing is five and the number of yeses is four and both of those figures are on a page under a stair in that hall where anybody coming up may read them.**

The sixth thing that was asked of that bench is a road to a valley nine days over a pass and a figure of forty marks in a waggon-book with the addition done out at every line, and the bench said no in about a minute and a half in eleven words, and the reeve asked for the reason to be entered twice because a bench that says no for money has refused a thing it could not afford and a bench that says no for anything else has refused a thing it does not want, and this county has never in the whole time of the bench got the first, and is not going to put the second down in a book under a stair and have it read in a generation as the first.

And a magistrate of thirty, who is the reeve's sister and has been at that bench for nine years and has beaten two counts of persons with a form's own construction and is frightened of her own answer, put the reason for the no in the minute, and the reason is that a road that is not made to carry a person does not get built in a county where nobody has asked for it, and this one has got no person at the end of it to be carried, and there is nobody at the far end of that pass to be hurt if we do not and nobody to be helped if we do.

**And a document road opened out of a county of about four hundred people four hundred miles off is made of paper and carried nobody, and it went up two flights of a stair on a market morning in the first year of this case because a man of twenty-seven stood in the rain and read a fair copy of four hundred and eight words and a working on a second sheet off a board under a bench, and nobody had to be asked, and it went back four hundred miles to that board, and about four people in this hall believed the valley part of it and one wrote it down word for word, and the man who wrote it down is not a clerk and has never been one.**

---

**And a carrier of about fifty with a waggon of his own and a firm of two came down two flights of a stair into that hall at about the ninth hour and said four things and none of them was about money.**

“I was up the low country again in the second week of the twelfth and I was at the bottom of the pass on the cart road and I did not go up it.

“There is a road at the top of that pass now. It is nine hundred yards of it and it is barrow ground and there is a turning place about forty feet across and it is not finished, and the man who keeps the top of the pass came down it on a Tuesday with a barrow and told me about a wall at the low end of a barrow run that is going to want a wall in a warm week, and I have written that down because it is the only thing anybody has said to me in nine days that was about a trade.

“**And I did not go up it, and nobody has asked me to, and I have been up that pass twice in eleven weeks and I have never once gone up it to ask anybody anything, and there is a valley at the top of it with about four hundred people in it and about two hundred of them have not been asked anything in nine generations, and I went down the low country in the eleventh week of this case and I could have walked up a road in a day and I did not.**”

A magistrate of thirty asked him why, and he gave an answer in about four sentences that went into the minute of that bench in a clerk's hand, and it is the reason the sixth asking is in a minute and the seventh is not.

“Because a man of about fifty with a waggon of his own who has been up a pass twice goes up it to carry things and to be paid for carrying them, and the day he goes up it to ask is the day he has a reason to be listened to that has nothing to do with the asking. And I have not got a house on that side of the pass and I have not got a trade there and four people in this room have told me over the years that a carrier of fifty with his own waggon is not asked anything about anything, and they were not being unkind to me, they were telling me the arrangement.

“**And I would like it minuted that a man of fifty has said that out loud in a room, and that it took me nine days to work out that it was the reason and not the courtesy.**”

**And at the fourth hour and a half the bell went at the Old Toll four hundred yards up the road, and it has forty-one rivets in it and a dull red seam, and it is rung by a boy who has been the steward's apprentice for two years and three months and is still not good at it, and by a carpenter who is a hand and not a keeper, and the arrangement is not anybody's in this chapter and nothing in the sound of it belongs to anybody.**

A Monday is the day the far end of that line keeps. The far end has been shut in four lines of writing since before the man who cut the mark in the coping of the first bay was born, and the four lines are correct, and the four hundred miles between the two ends of that road is nine days over a pass and a county road and a low country, and the road does not go into the valley and does not spend a penny in it and does not ask anybody in it.

**The two facts about the Monday are in two books in two counties, and the bell is in this county and the far end is in the other, and neither of the two counties has ever been sent the other's book, and there is no instrument in this kingdom that can make either of them answer a question that is not one, and a book is a place where things get joined whether they were joined or not, and there is a fire in a room over a mill four hundred miles off where a clerk of about sixty has kept a minute for twenty-two years of a valley that has no writ and no bell and no office, and that minute is not the day-book of a records office and cannot be served and is not going to be sent here.**

He was twenty-five yards from it and he had not rung it in four years and he did not ring it and he stood on the bank above the wharf and let a boy of seventeen and a carpenter do it, and about four people in that town have said since that the man on the bank watched every stroke of it, and nine have said that he went down to the wharf before the second one.

And the tile is still on a coping at a crossing four hundred miles off, a hand's width, with a rope on it in two loops and no yoke and no headstock and no striker, and a man of sixty-four there swept round it for forty-one years and wanted it lifted because a lifting is a job and a taking away is a throwing away, and it has not been looked at by anybody in this county and the county that could look at it cannot be reached, and that is the third thing in this volume that nobody in this county can account for and it is the oldest of the three.

---

And the refusal went into the ordinary post on the fifth of the seven days and it went in a bag, and a copy went onto a shelf in a room over a weigh-house with a number against the office against it and nothing against a person, and the word eastern is in that copy and in a bag that has been on the road for thirty-five days and a clerk who takes the post out of that room every morning has never read it and is not going to, and the office that made the offer has not sent a second one, and about four hundred and eleven words in a strip of cut paper in the same press came back from a provincial town two hundred miles off two years ago cut along the left edge with a knife and the number of persons that offer was made out to is still not on the sheet and has been asked for in a return and has not come, and those three are in three counties and the nearest two are two hundred miles apart and neither of the people who put any of them there has ever heard of the other two.

And there is a bundle on a second shelf with a piece of tarred hemp through it, and there are nine things in it, and the tenth was opened on a Tuesday in the third week of Harvestmonth by a woman of twenty-nine in a room of two and the reading of it was slower than the writing and not better, which has been the practice of that office since the first year.

And a room with a fire in it has twenty-nine benches bolted to the floor in four rows with a number cut into the end of each one and a hundred and forty cut into the end of the one at the back of the right-hand row, and the aisle is four feet wide, and about nine bushels of barley were put in the aisle of it in the eighth week of eleven weeks and nobody stood in it and there has not been a person in it for three generations.

And the road up from the water is four hundred yards long and it is there, and the pointing on the parapet at the top of it is a foot and a half short, and the salt is coming over the lane, and the tide is at the wharf.
