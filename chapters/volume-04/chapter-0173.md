# Chapter 0173

He was on the fourth tread with a hurdle over his shoulder when the two of them came up out of the yard, and he had been there long enough to have put the hurdle down, and he had not knocked and he had not called out, and there were two women of the ninth generation at the mill and about nine people in the yard and not one of them had said anything to him about where he was going, and that was not an accident and he had worked out that it was not an accident some weeks ago.

Nobody in this valley has ever asked the ninth generation anything and there are about two hundred of the ninth generation in a valley of about four hundred people, and the way that has been achieved is that nobody has ever had occasion to.

“You have not asked me anything since the market,” he said.

“No,” said Aren Kest.

“You have not asked me anything and I have not asked you anything, and it is thirteen weeks and it is not going to be the arrangement for ever, and I have come up here to say a thing and I would like to do it while the two of you are in the room and not on a bank, because I do not want to have said it on a bank.” He put the hurdle against the door frame the way you put a hurdle against a door frame. “I am Osgar Vell. I have been on that page since the day I was born and nobody in my family has ever said the word to my face and you have been in this valley thirteen weeks and you did not know what I was called until this morning, and that is not a complaint, because you are a stranger and it is the correct way round. I would like it said in a room. That is all I came for.”

“Nobody has said your name in this room in a hundred and forty years,” said Iven Sarr, “and there is a name in a copy of that book which is four hundred miles off and the name is yours, and it is spelled two ways on two pages, and I have kept the book nineteen years and I have never once known which of the two is right, and I have never asked the man who wrote both of them because he is dead, and he made the copy with a cart in the yard in the third year of my keeping and tied it up himself, and it is a bundle of about forty leaves under a piece of tarred hemp in this room and it does not leave this room, and I am telling you that in front of a man of thirty-two because it has been sitting on the end of a sentence in my head for sixteen years and I would like it out.”

“That is the second time somebody has said something like that about a piece of paper in a room,” said Aren Kest. “I have been in this valley thirteen weeks and I have heard it twice and neither time was to me.”

**And the young man of twenty-two had come up nine steps to say a name and got four other things out of the room while he was there, and the fourth of them was the worst thing that had been said in that building for a hundred and forty years, and he heard all of it and has not repeated one word of it and is not going to as long as he lives, which is a thing he has not told anybody and which nobody in that valley has asked him about.**

“Then it will be the v or it will not be the v,” said Osgar Vell, “and I am twenty-two and it is my name and I have not got anybody to stop me finding out, and that is a different kind of having nobody and I have only just worked out that it is a different kind.”

“You have not got anybody to stop you because there is nobody here to stop anybody,” said Iven Sarr, “and I have been on the wrong side of that for nineteen years and I have not been wrong about it, and a young man of twenty-two has found a thing out at the top of a stair in four seconds that I have been carrying about for nineteen years, and I would like it noted that the thing he found out is a spelling, and that it has taken me nineteen years and it has taken him four seconds, and that neither of those two figures is any use to anybody.”

---

**And then the man of fifty-eight took four lines off the head of the first spread and read them out in the voice of a man reading a rule, and he has read them about nine hundred times and had told a stranger three days before that he would not read them, and he read them because there is a form on the boards of the floor of his own back room.**

“I have not read you these because they are a rule and not the oath and I said so and you wrote it down in a book and there is no day in this valley to put against it. I am reading them now and I am telling you the reason and the reason is on the floor of my own house and there is nobody in that room but me and a stone and a lamp and I have been not going in there for six years, and you are the reason I went in there, and you did not ask me and I want that on the thing I am about to say.”

*This book is kept and the room is kept. The keeper of it is a man and not an office.*

*The keeper of it answers for the book and the room and the day and the mark and the hand.*

*The keeper of it answers for no person in this valley and speaks for no person in this valley and has been appointed by no person in this valley.*

*Where a man is named in a book of this valley it is because he did it himself and not because anybody asked him.*

He read it twice and then put the book down and squared it, and his hand went flat on the cover, and he left it there.

“That is about a hundred and forty years old and it is four lines and there is not a mark on any of it that is the mark of a person, and I have read it about nine hundred times in this room and I never once worked out what it was a rule *about*.”

“It is a rule about the fifth column,” said Aren Kest.

“It is the answer to the fifth column.”

**And he got up off the trestles and got the form off the boards and put it on the trestles on top of the first spread, and he put his finger on the fifth of the eight numbers without reading it out, because a man of fifty-eight has been in a room with it for an hour and can find a line on it with his finger in the dark.**

“That is what it asks. *The name of the person appointed in the quarter to answer for the men that go out.* Nine words, and every one of them is a decision somebody took a long time ago, and the decision is that a person is going to be appointed, and the person is going to have a name, and the name is going to be written down, and he is going to answer for the men who go out of this quarter.

“And my great-grandfather put a book on a trestle in this room when this valley was nine generations younger, and the form was on the floor of the back room, and he read the fifth column, and he wrote four lines at the head of his book saying that there is no such person in this valley and never will be, and that whoever keeps the book answers for the book and not for a soul, and he signed nothing and dated nothing and told nobody he had done it, and it is the only thing anybody in this valley has ever written down about anything it meant.”

“And nobody found out.”

“Nobody found out for about a hundred and forty years because there is nobody here who would be looking. I found out at the age of fifty-eight in the eleventh week of a cold because a man off the road stood in a doorway and did not go four feet into a room.” He put his hand on the trestle. “And that is where the thing this valley has been doing for nine generations was written down, and it was written by a man who knew what the form wanted and wrote the opposite of it on the first page of his own answer, and then he put the answer in a book and never sent it, and that is the entire arrangement and it has held for a hundred and forty years and it is the best thing anybody in this valley has ever done.”

“Best is a word.”

“It is a word and I have put it there and I would like it noticed, because nine generations of this valley have put a great many words in books and I have never once seen one of them put in a book because a man was proud of it.”

**And then the man of thirty-two asked the question that has been standing in that room since the seventh hour of the morning, and it is on the page because he asked it out loud and did not know whether anybody in the valley could answer it, and about four people alive could.**

“If nobody is appointed to answer for the men that go out,” he said, “then what happens to a boy of twenty-two in this valley when he is on the top of a pass in a week's time and somebody asks him what he is. Because the answer to that is not the eighth word or the seventh one. The answer is that there is nobody at home. There has never been anybody at home. There is a book in a room and about two hundred lines in it and not one appointed person in a hundred and forty years, and the people who are appointed in this kingdom are appointed by a body, and there has never been a body in this valley, and if there had been one it would have been a council and this is a market with a cross in it and about nine people who turn up.”

“That is what I am asking,” said Osgar Vell.

“You are asking whether a man can answer for a boy who has not been appointed anybody by anybody, and I have been in this valley thirteen weeks and I can tell you the answer is that he cannot, and I can tell you that a person who cannot answer for himself is not in a worse position in this valley than a person who can, and that is not a joke and it is not a comfort, because the reason is that nothing in this valley can be produced against him either. There is nobody to produce him to. That is not justice. It is the same hole in a different direction.”

“I have been the second of those since the day I was born,” said Osgar Vell, “and I had not got a word for it and you have just given me one, and I am twenty-two and I am not going to say thank you for it, and I am also not going to tell you that it is nothing, because you are the first person who has been in this valley in nine years and you have said it once, and it has taken me thirteen weeks and a printed sheet off a floor.”

**And then the fourth word, which is the one the man off the road came out of a county four hundred miles off to say, and he said it once, in that room, in a valley of about four hundred people, and he did not explain it and he was not going to.**

“There is a word in the county I came from for the fifth column,” he said. “It is for a person a community appoints to answer for one other named person. They appoint him, he answers to the people who appointed him, they can take it off him, and he is not allowed to answer for anybody else, not one, for as long as he has it.” He looked at the form and not at the room. “That is all of it and I am not going to say anything else about it, and I am not going to say where I read it first because I have not got the other one with me and because a man who describes a document to nine people is a man who has made nine copies of a thing nobody in this valley can see.”

“It is a word for a person and not for a number of people.”

“It is a word for a person. That is the only thing that makes it different from the third column, and it is the one thing this valley has not got.”

“A carrier is not that,” said Osgar Vell.

“No,” said Aren Kest. “A carrier is not that, and I have been in this valley thirteen weeks and I have watched about nine people use the word about me and not one of them has appointed me to do anything and not one of them has asked my leave, and there is not one sheet of paper in four hundred miles on which to enter it, and I have told you that in this room before and I am not going to say it again.”

“You have said it in a market,” said Iven Sarr. “You said it in a market in one breath and then you said a thing about a leaf in a book in a county that has a stone in a field in it, and I have been on the wrong side of the second half of that for nineteen years and I am not going to be told about it again by either of you.”

“There is one thing more and then I am going to do the only thing I have wanted to do for six years, which is nothing,” said Osgar Vell.

“Say it.”

**And it turned out that the valley has never once had a single word for him, and there are eight of them, and no two of them mean the same thing, and none of them is the word on the form.**

“My mother says it one way and my aunt says it another and they live eleven yards apart and have not spoken about it in nine years. The woman at the mill says a third thing and she has said it to me since I was about seven. I said a fourth version of it to myself at seventeen in a doorway because I had been told the third one and I wanted to be the kind of man it was for. The girl at the gate says a fifth and she is nine and she has the best of the lot of them and she will not give her name, so that is one of eight on a gatepost. The man who makes nails says a sixth and it is the only one of the eight that makes it sound like a job. The old man at the upper water says a seventh and it is the only one of the eight that makes it sound like a debt, and he is eighty-one and he has said it to me twice and he looked at me both times to see what I would do, and I did nothing, which he has not mentioned.”

“Eight,” said Iven Sarr.

“Eight by the count I have made since a man off the road asked me a question in a doorway in the ninth week of the first cold and I have thought about nothing else since, which you will believe or not.” He put his hands in his sleeves. “And the eighth one is on that sheet. And not one person alive in this valley uses the eighth one, and it has been in this valley longer than any of the seven, and the reason nobody uses it is the reason my great-grandfather wrote four lines at the head of that book, and I have worked that out in about a minute and a half and I would like somebody to tell me I have got it right.”

“You have got it right about as far as anybody in this room can go and not one step further,” said Aren Kest. “And I am not going to tell you the rest of it, and the rest of it is the only thing I came four hundred miles for and it is not a thing you can be told in a room with no page in it.”

“That is fair,” said Osgar Vell, “and I have not been told anything in this room, and I came up nine steps to say a name.”

**And then he asked for the lamp, and he asked for a scrap of the paper that comes up the pass a season, and nobody in that room stopped him, and the man of fifty-eight let it happen and it is the first time in nineteen years that he has let a page be made in this room, and the reason he gave is four words long and it is on the page because it is the only thing anybody in this valley has said all winter that is about the future.**

“A rule about keepers names nobody,” said Iven Sarr. “You can copy that a hundred times and you have not made a second copy of a soul. Go on.”

The young man of twenty-two wrote four lines out on a scrap of rag paper about the size of a hand, in a hand that got smaller towards the bottom, and gave it back, and the man of fifty-eight read it and did not take it and did not tear it up, and left it lying on the trestles where it was, and it is not the only copy of a thing in this room and it is the first copy of a thing in this room, and the difference between those two facts is about four hundred years wide.

“He is going over the pass on the fourth day of the next cold,” said Osgar Vell, at the door, with the hurdle off the frame and on his shoulder. “About eleven of us, and it is a week to the top of it, and I did not go on the fourth day of this one and my mother has not mentioned it and neither will she. When I am at the top of it somebody is going to ask me what I am, because they always ask at the top, and I have never had an answer I could hand a person, and I have not told anybody that either, and I am telling it to a man who does not know what I am called and a man who kept a book.”
