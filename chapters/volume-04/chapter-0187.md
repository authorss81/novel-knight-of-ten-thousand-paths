# Chapter 0187

**The vote was on the second morning and it was taken at the front of the room with the fire in, and the nine of them were in the same nine seats, and the clerk of about sixty had the minute open on a trestle and a stub of the second fire in his hand, and there is no second fire in this valley, and it is the fire the man of fifty-eight writes with.**

The aisle was four feet wide and empty for both days, and nobody stood in it, and nobody was asked to.

And on the first night nine people walked down a bank to nine doors with a lantern and did not go in at any of them, and the man of thirty-four was one of the nine and had wanted to be asked one question for nine years and did not ask it, and the reason he gave, to a man of thirty-two on the fourth step of a stair at about the eleventh hour, was that a body which has never asked anybody anything does not begin at a meeting where nine people can see the length of an aisle.

“Do you want to be in there.”

“I want to be asked one question every winter for nine years,” said Wystan Roole, “and there is not one of the nine of them who is going to ask me, and the ninth generation was asked at nine hundred doors in the second month of this cold by five of us and four of us came back and told me it went well. That is not this.”

“You could put it in the minute.”

“The minute is a man of sixty who keeps it for twenty-two years and puts down what was said and not what was meant, and he did not ask me one question about a sentence this morning, and I have watched four people notice that he did not, and I am not going to be the tenth man in this valley to be surprised that nobody asked.”

---

**And the man of fifty-five spoke first and he spoke for about four minutes and half of it was about the room.**

“I am going to say all of what I want and then I am going to sit down and I am not going to speak again today, and I am going to say it once, because I have had nine years of saying it in yards to four people at a time and it has not once been put down.

“Thirty-five years ago this winter was kept and I was twenty years old and I stood in that aisle in the cold in front of the whole valley and a man asked me what I swore and I answered him, and I have not told anybody what I said, and I am not going to, and that is not a mystery. It is what a man does with a question like that at twenty and I have had thirty-five years to work out that I would take it to the grave with me and I have.

“Here is what I want. I want this valley to stop passing it on. Not this cold. This cold it is kept whatever the nine of us do with our faces. I want the ninth generation, and the tenth, and the eleventh, and I want all of it stopped, and I want it stopped by somebody who is not going to be asked to pick it up.

“And I am not asking for a vote on a winter. I am asking for a vote on who holds it. **There are four hundred people in this valley and two hundred of them are in a book from the day they were born and eleven of them are over a pass right now, and the oldest person in this room is eighty-one and I am the second, and there is nobody in this valley under fifty-five who can be asked to do this, and I have been told since I was twenty-five that the promise is not mine to hold, and I have agreed with that every time, and I would like somebody in this room to notice that a thing which is nobody's to hold has been going for a hundred and forty years.**”

“And what do you think happens when it stops being kept,” said the man of thirty-four, from the second seat.

**“I think it goes on,” said Torrin Hatch. “That is my whole opinion and I have never had it in words before and I have had it for nine years. I think the thing underneath does not care who is standing in the aisle. I think the reason I was asked thirty-five years ago was not the winter. I think it was so that a man could be in this room in nine years and be certain that somebody in this valley had stood up in a cold week and said the words out loud. I think that is what the aisle is for. **I do not believe a thing that does not ask is a thing anybody can rely on, and I have never been able to give a reason for that and I have stopped trying.****

“And I am fifty-five. I have nine years of legs in me and about nine years of hands, and I am the only man in this valley who is both old enough to be asked and not old enough to have been asked twice, and the tenth generation is coming of age in this cold, and forty of them have never been in a room with a fire in it, and if the nine of you vote to keep it then in nine years somebody is going to have to go and stand in that aisle and put a hand on a man of about sixty-four and ask him what he swore, and I have been that man's age when it was done to me and I am telling you in a room of nine people what it is, and I have told nobody else in thirty-five years.”

---

**And the woman of sixty-six at four looms answered him, and she had answered the second question on the first day and she answered the first one on the second, and it took her about four minutes and about a third of it was about him.**

“You are right about the aisle. I was in it. I have been in it and I have watched about thirty of the eighth generation and the ones before them in it and I have never once been in it with anybody of the ninth in the room, because the ninth generation has never been in it, and you are telling us that you are the last one and you are the last one.

“And I am going to say the other half of that, and it is not against you, and you did not ask me to say it. **The aisle does not need an old man. That is what it has been for forty years and it never needed one and the men who went into it were between twenty and thirty and they were the strongest backs in this valley, and the winter was kept every one of those times, and it has been kept nine times since by nobody at all, because there is no working in this valley that needs a hand in it, and a man of fifty-five wanting the promise put down by somebody who is not going to be asked to pick it up is wanting to put down a rope that nobody is holding.**

“And I will not do it. I will stand in that aisle at sixty and be asked what I swore, and I will tell the truth, and the ninth generation will watch, and in nine years one of the tenth will be in it and none of the nine of you will have to be in it twice. And I would like the minute to carry that I said it and that I am the first of the eighth generation to offer, and that I have not been asked by anybody in this room, and that I am offering to a body that is not a bench and knows it.”

The clerk wrote that in. It took him about a minute and a half and the man of fifty-five looked at what he had written and then looked at the floor of the room for about four seconds and did not say one word about it, and nobody in that room said one word about it, and it is in the minute of a valley in a room over a mill that nobody has ever been served with and that nobody has ever asked to read.

And then the man of fifty-eight said the other half, and he said it standing up, and the clerk stopped writing for a minute and a half and then went on writing, and both of those facts are on the page.

“You are right about all of it and I have been in this room for two days listening to you be right about all of it, and I have not once heard you be wrong, and I am going to say the other half and then I am going to sit down and I am not going to speak again either.

“You have said that a body which is nobody's to hold has gone for a hundred and forty years. It has. I have the book. It is the only roll in this valley and it is a form with about four columns taken out of the middle of it and a hundred and forty years of a man answering a count of men going out, one line at a time, with a birth, and nobody ever recognised it, because there is not one form in this valley for it to be recognised against.

“And you have said the aisle is not for the winter. It is not. I have been its keeper's son and its keeper's grandson and its keeper, and I have read the four lines at the head of the first spread about nine hundred times and I never once knew what they were a rule about, and they are not about the winter. They are about the book and the room and the day and the mark and the hand, and they say the keeper answers for no person in this valley and has been appointed by no person in this valley, and they say that wherever a man is named in a book of this valley it is because he did it himself.

**“Nobody in this valley has ever written down what the asking is for, and I have now asked a man of thirty-four and a man of thirty-two and a woman of sixty-six and a man of eighty-one and a man of fifty-five, and the answer is that nobody in this valley knows, and the reason nobody knows is that in a hundred and forty years not one person has ever been the kind of person who gets told. And that is not a thing you can vote on, and I am going to say that in front of nine people and then I am going to stop.**”

**And then the man of fifty-five said the thing that went in the minute, and he said it forty seconds into the second half of the second day and not at the end, and it was in the middle of an argument about the aisle and about the number a hundred and forty and about what a bench is, and he had it ready and nobody in the room knew he had it.**

“But we are not a bench. We have never been a bench. I have heard four people in this valley say it out loud and I have said it myself in about nine yards and nobody has ever written it down anywhere, and if it is going to be written down then it should be written down properly, and a body that knows what it is not does not behave like one, and I will say it in the way I had it off a man.

“**A bench that is generous decides. A bench that is asked is being told what it is for. And a bench that is asked three times and says yes three times is not a bench, it is a subscription.**”

The clerk wrote it out. It took him a minute and a half and he wrote it as it came and he did not ask who had said it before and he did not ask where a man of fifty-five in a valley nine miles long with no writ and no bell and no office had had it, and four people in the room noticed that he had not asked and nine did not.

Iven Sarr looked at his own hands for a while and then he said one thing, and it is the only thing in this chapter that was said by anybody who was not asked.

“I do not know where he had it from,” he said. “I have been the only person in this valley who wanted to know anything for nineteen years and I have been wrong about that for nineteen years, and this morning I have worked out what I am actually for. **I am for stopping people asking each other and for putting it in a minute, and I have been doing the first one my whole life and calling it keeping a book.** And that sentence is in a minute in a hand that is not mine in a room where nobody has ever been asked anything, and in about nine years a woman who is not in this room yet is going to read it and believe it was invented here, and she will be right, and that is what a sentence is for.”

And then the nine of them were asked, one at a time, how they were voting, and it is in the minute that they were asked in that order and that the order was the order they were sitting in, and that the man of about thirty-one of the ninth generation was asked third and the man of fifty-five was asked ninth and that the clerk had put the order in before anybody had argued for it.

The man of about thirty-one said he was voting to keep it and gave two sentences, and the clerk wrote both of them.

“I am on this bench because my father was on it and my grandfather was on it and there is no way in nine miles to be on it that is not that, and I have four steadings and about nine people who work them who cannot be in this room, and I have been asking this bench since the first of the cold what it is for and I have been told twice that it is the winter. I am voting for that. I am not voting because I know what the winter is, because I do not, and I am not voting because two hundred people asked me to, and I would like the minute to say that the ninth generation consented at nine hundred doors and I was at four of them and I said the six things to a woman of about thirty-five and she asked me what I would write down and I told her nothing, and she stood there four seconds longer than she needed to and then she said a thing I have been thinking about for nine weeks.”

The clerk wrote it and read it back and the man of thirty-one said it was right and the clerk entered it and nobody asked the man of thirty-one what the woman had said and the man of thirty-one did not say.

The man of eighty-one said he was voting to keep it and gave one sentence and the clerk wrote it and nobody in the room argued with a man of eighty-one about anything, and the sentence was, “I have been asked once in my life and I am not going to be the second, and I would like it in the minute that I said that and that I am voting to keep it anyway, and I have never once in my life not done a thing after saying I would not do it, and I am eighty-one and there is nobody in this room who gets to be impressed by that.”

The man of fifty-five voted to stop and gave no reason a second time, and the clerk wrote *against, no reason given, a reason having been given*, and the clerk read that back twice and did not change it, and the man of fifty-five looked at the page and did not object to any part of it.

And the vote was taken and it was eight for and one against, and the clerk entered the figures and not the names, because the clerk has been doing this valley's minute for twenty-two years and has never put a name in it that was not put there by somebody else in the room, and that is all his office and he has never said so and he was not going to be asked.

The ninth generation consented to be asked, on foot, at nine hundred doors, in eleven days, in the second month of this cold, with nine against it, and the nine is three households, and the three households are not in this minute and the three households are not going to be named in this minute.

The council was asked whether it is going to keep the winter, and the council was asked what happens if it does not, and the answer to the second of those is in the minute in four words in a clerk's hand, and the answer to the first of those is eight to one, and the eight of them said yes without one of them being able to say what happens if they had said no, and the one of them said no and gave a reason thirty-five years old in a room of nine people, and nobody in that room called him anything at all, and he was not called a villain by anybody afterwards in nine years, and he was not called anything else either, and he came to the room over the cross four more times in nine years and did not stand in the aisle at any point and did not say one word about the vote again, and that is all that is known about him and it is all he wanted.

And the minute of this valley cannot be served. There is no writ in nine miles, no bell and no office, and a man of about sixty keeps it in a room over a mill and nobody has ever asked to read it and nobody is ever going to be served with it, so the sentence about a subscription is in it word for word in a hand of about sixty, and there is a clerk, and a clerk is a person whose business is a thing that was said, and if the saying is safe then the thing is safe, and in a valley where nothing is ever asked that is the safest place a sentence can be put.

And the fire went out at the ninth hour and the clerk shut the minute and the nine of them went out, and there was a barrow standing in the aisle because a barrow had been brought in against the second row in the morning and nobody had moved it, and the woman of the Low Holm had a yoke on her shoulders and wanted it out of her way before she went, and so the barrow went down the four feet of the aisle and out of the door on two wheels, and it is the shortest way out of that room and it is four feet wider than a barrow, and the man who pushed it did not know that there was anything in this valley to know about the length of that floor, and he had not been asked.
