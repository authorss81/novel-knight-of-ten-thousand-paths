# Chapter 0167

By the ninth day the wall at the Low Holm was higher than a man's chest and it was the best wall anybody in this valley had seen, and there is no way to be in a yard at the Low Holm at any hour of any day of a winter and not know about it, because it is on the high side of a bank above a working byre and it is the only true line in nine miles.

It is a retaining wall and it is double-faced and it has a batter on it and a coping of flat stones on the top set in lime, and about every fourth course through the whole length of it there is a stone the length of the wall and the depth of the wall, laid across, and nobody in this valley had seen a through-stone in nine generations and the man who laid them had laid them in about nine seconds each and did not want to be watched doing it and was watched doing it by everybody.

The lime was the trouble and the lime was why it took eleven days.

Quicklime in a cold week is not mortar, it is a thing that is going to be mortar in about four months, and if you bed a course in it in the twelfth week of a cold you are bedding that course in a powder and the powder will set hard in the spring and squeeze the course it is in, and a course that has been squeezed from underneath for four months comes out at the top, and a wall comes out of true that way and not any other way, and there is not one wall in this valley to learn it from.

So they laid it dry. Every course in that wall is laid in lime putty and stone and nothing else, and the joints are packed with small stone and driven home with a bar, and the wall is a dry wall with lime in it, and it is stronger than a wall bedded in quicklime in a cold week and weaker than a wall bedded in it in June, and the man of thirty-four said so out loud to a stranger on the second morning and then said that he had laid about eleven of them in a warm week and about four of them in a cold week and that the four of them were the four he thought about most.

The frost got into the hearting on the fifth night, which is a thing that does not hurt anything and is the reason nine men spent a morning knocking the face of the wall back with a bar and a lump hammer, and it is the reason the wall is nine days and not six, and the man of twenty-four who had never had a job in his life knocked the face back better than anybody because he was the only one of the sixteen who had never seen a wall finished and so had no idea that it was supposed to look like anything.

The string came down on the fourth course and went up on the ninth and came down again on the eleventh, and the last time it came down it was out by about a quarter of an inch at the far end and level at the near one, and the man of thirty-four looked at it for a while, and then went and got the plumb bob, and then made the twenty-two-year-old go and get the plumb bob, and then did not correct it, and said one sentence about it that sixteen men have repeated in four yards ever since and that is not a sentence about a wall.

He came at the fourth hour on the ninth day and stood at the top of it and looked down the length of it and it was better than the fourth of eleven tons of stone on a gravel approach in a fen county, and it was better than a mile of re-cut boundary in standing water, and it was holding up a byre.

**And a man of thirty-four with sixteen men and no work said that out loud in a yard, in front of nine people, on the ninth day, and he did not say it as a complaint.**

“This is the best wall anybody in this valley has seen,” said Wystan Roole. “I have put up four of them that were better and none of them are in this valley. And it is holding up a byre, and eleven days of thirty men is a byre, and the men could put a causeway out of that gate to the ford in about nine weeks, and there is not one person in this valley who has ever asked us for that, and I have asked twice and been told twice that there is no road to build, and I am not saying it again, and I have said it this morning and that is the last time.”

Nobody in the yard argued with him about it, and the woman of the Low Holm came out with a yoke on her shoulders and looked up at the top of it for a while, and then she said the thing she had come down to say.

“You have built me a wall.”

“You had a bank going.”

“I had a bank going. I have had a bank going for nine years and I have put two barrow-loads of stone in the hole in it every third year and the stone has gone every time, and I knew it would go, and I did it anyway because it is a thing a person does with a hole.”

“What did you want.”

“I wanted my byre floor mended,” said the woman of the Low Holm. “It is lime and rubble and about four inches of it and it has been that way since before I was born, and half of it has gone under the beasts and there is a hole in it at the low end about the size of a dinner plate, and every year I put a stone in the hole and every year the hole is a dinner plate again, and I have been on my knees in it in the dark twice this winter with a bucket and a piece of slate.”

Nobody said anything to that, and the sixteen men who had spent nine days on a wall stood about forty yards away with their barrows and did not say anything to that either, and it is on the page because it happened and because two of the men heard it and have never said a word about it.

“You have built me a wall instead of my floor,” she said, “and I am not going to say thank you, and I am also not going to have the wall taken down, and the floor is still a dinner plate, and both of those are true this morning, and you are a man who laid a wall and I am a woman with a hole in a floor, and I would like both of those to be in the world at the same time and I would like neither of us to be the one who gives.”

**And there is no coin in this valley, and there is a price, and the two are not the same thing, and the only man in the valley who could put either of them on a line had a stick and a notebook and could not help it.**

The lime came up from the low country in three loads on the backs of four men before the frost, and the price of a load is a number a man from the low country says out loud, and he said sixpence for a load of quicklime and fourpence for a barrow of sand, and he said it in a yard to nine people, and a man of thirty-two wrote it on his own hand before anybody had finished the sentence because his hand does that.

*Quicklime, three loads, at sixpence a load. Six, six, six. Six and six is twelve. Twelve and six is eighteen.*

*A barrow of sand, fourpence.*

*Eighteen and four is twenty-two pence, which is one shilling and tenpence.*

And then he got the pen out again and wrote under it, because he is the kind of man who writes what is under it, and it is the only page in this valley in six weeks that has a money column on it:

*The above is a figure in a county's money. It is not a figure in this valley's money. The valley has no coin. The price of three loads of quicklime in this valley is nine bushels of barley and a peck, and that figure is on the back of a slate in this valley, and the man who wrote it on the slate is a woman of about thirty who does the births and has never argued with anybody about a number in eleven years, and I have not added her figure to mine and I am not going to, and there is nobody alive who could.*

*And the two are the same three loads of lime, and one of them is what the man from the low country asked for and one of them is what he took, and the difference is four bushels, and he did not say what the four bushels were for and nobody in this valley will ever find out, and the nine bushels and a peck came out of a seed house, which means it came out of the barley that a family puts in a byre roof in the warm week because there is nowhere else in this valley to put it, which means the lime in this wall came out of the same sack as a thing about forty feet away from where it went.*

The woman with the slate was in the yard when he finished it, because she had come about once in ten days and this had been close to ten days, and she read the two figures upside down in about four seconds, which is what she does.

“You have written my nine and a peck on your own arm,” she said.

“I have.”

“That is not a place anybody keeps a figure.”

“It is the only place in four hundred miles I have got that I can understand.”

She looked at the notebook for a while, and then she said the thing that the wall was actually for, as far as she was concerned, and she said it to nine people and a man of thirty-two and it is the only sentence anybody in this valley said that week that was about the future and it is not a prophecy.

“I have kept that number eleven years,” she said, “and in eleven years not one person has ever asked me for a figure off me that they were going to keep. You have asked me for one, and I have given you one, and it is going on your own arm instead of on my slate, and the slate is the only copy of it in the world, and that is right, because I am not a place a number lives in, I am a person who happens to have one.”

She put the slate under her arm and went down the bank.

The coping went on on the eleventh day and it took two men all of it, and one of them was the man of fifty-eight, who came up at about the fifth hour with no yoke and stood on the ladder holding the other end of a flat stone for about nine minutes, and nobody had asked him and nobody mentioned it afterwards, and the stone is on the top of the wall at the corner by the byre door and it is the only stone in the wall that is squared rather than broken, and the reason for that is that he squared it.

**And there are four seed houses in this valley and every one of them is a stone room with a slate floor and double doors, and about half of what is in two of them is not seed, and the nine bushels and a peck of barley that paid for the lime in that wall came out of one of them, and there are two families who count what is in those four rooms twice in a winter and who have never once compared their counts with each other in a book, and the whole of the accounting of a valley of about four hundred people is four stone rooms, two families, and a slate carried by one woman up a bank about once in ten days.**

That is the actual ledger of this place and there is no other one.

A man from a county with a press in it stands in a yard and does the sum and the sum is the following, and he has written none of it down and is not going to. Four seed houses. Two families counting. One slate. One woman. And the number of nine bushels and a peck exists in exactly one place in the world, and that place is a piece of slate about two feet by three with a groove round the edge for a thumb and a strip of hide tied at the top corner, and it is carried up and down this valley by one person, and it has been carried by one person for eleven years.

If that woman is hit by a barrow at the ford on a market day, the price of the lime in the best wall in this valley is gone, and not the lime, which is in the wall, but the price of it, which is a number that somebody said out loud in a yard one morning and wrote on a slate.

There is one other slate in this valley. It does not do this work. It is used for a different thing entirely and the woman of about thirty has said so in this room to a man who read a book this morning, and the two of them are not the same object and the two women are not the same woman and that is the whole of it.

And he could fix it. That is what went through his head at about the fourth hour on the ninth day, standing on a bank with a barrow, and it is worth putting on the page in his own words because it is the first true thing he thought about himself in six weeks and it was not flattering.

He could get a piece of paper. There is paper in this valley, about a bundle of it a season, that comes up the pass on the backs of men, and it is good paper, made four valleys over in a house with a press in it, and the back of the ninth spread of the only roll in a valley of about four hundred people is in a copy of itself in a county four hundred miles off, and the price of a press is not a thing this valley has. He could write nine bushels and a peck on a piece of that paper and give it to the woman with the slate, and then two people in the world would have the figure, and the day the second copy was lost the first one would still be there, and that is how a number stops being a woman.

And he did not do it, and the reason he did not do it is that he has spent four years and a half learning that a thing written down twice is a thing two people can be got at, and that the woman of about thirty had already told him the same thing about a figure and had been right, and that he had written her figure on his own arm instead of on her slate, and that the correct procedure in a valley with no coin is that a person is not a place a number lives in.

He got the notebook out on the ninth day and looked at the page with the addition on it, and the addition was a sum about three loads of lime, and it was on a page in a book under a bed in a county four hundred miles off, and he did not copy it into the valley, and he shut the book, and the sum stayed on his own arm, and that is a small thing and it is the whole of what he did in this valley for eleven days, and it is the only time in this case that he has had a figure he could have given away and given it to the person holding the only copy instead.

The woman of the Low Holm brought out a pail at about the fourth hour on the last day and set it down at the end of the line, and it was not in the place it had been in, because somebody had moved it four inches to the left so that a man standing on it in a wet week would not be standing in the wet place, and she looked at it, and she did not ask, and she did not say thank you, and she stood there for a while with her hands in her sleeves looking at about sixty yards of the best wall anybody in that valley had ever seen, holding up the byre it had been built instead of the road it could have been, on a bank above a working yard in the twelfth week of a cold in its ninth generation.
