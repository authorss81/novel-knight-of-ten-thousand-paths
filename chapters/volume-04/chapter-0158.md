# Chapter 0158

The notebook is in the bag and the bag is under the bed of a stranger, and the first thing he did on the first morning was the drift figure, because a man who does not write the figure down on the far side is a man who will get it wrong on the near side and blame the office.

He wrote it standing at the window with the bag open on the sill, and he wrote it in the register a path-side man uses, which is not a register anybody in this valley would recognise and which is a register nobody in this valley is ever going to check.

*Went out at the fourth hour, on a line made up in nine minutes with the hour against each of the nine. The office's printed figure is three weeks. It has been three weeks in an office's hand for two years and the office cannot say whose figure it is and I cannot either.*

*The days are not written because I do not have them. The error is not written because it cannot be had without the days. The warning is not the thing the error is over and the road is nowhere in it. I am writing this on this side of a line and it will be checked on the other side, and if the two figures are not the same number then I have put a month in it twice and I have earned that.*

*And I have a number and no frame, which has not happened to me before. In the county with a stone in a field I could not convert a rolling into a day. Here there is nothing to convert out of. A man who cannot count the days he is gone cannot tell anybody afterwards how long he was, and that is not a thing a warning can be beaten with.*

**And on the fourth morning he tried to convert, and the second time in this case that anybody has tried, and he refused it in his own notebook and the refusal is the whole of it.**

He is a man who counts things. He has counted load points under a structure every working morning for a year, and it is a habit and not a working, and there is no book in the county he came from that it goes in. He cannot help it. A place arrives and he counts what is in it, and a place that keeps its count in winters and generations has given him a number he cannot use, and he tried anyway.

*Nine winters. A generation is a winter. So there have been nine generations and we are in the ninth and the tenth is arguing. Ninety-one years if a year is ten, and it is not ten here, it is whatever it is, and if I had to guess I would guess about nine years to a winter and there is no authority in this valley that I could ask, and a guess is not a frame.*

*Three weeks since the gate. The woman at the Low Holm says it is the ninth week of the cold and I have counted ten sleeps. Ten sleeps is not a day in any calendar I have and I am not going to make it one. I have tried this twice before in this case, once in a county that keeps its year in rollings of four weeks, and both times the answer came out as a number with no meaning and I threw the paper away both times, and here I am on a third and I am not going to do the arithmetic at all this time, I am going to write the number of sleeps and the number of weeks and leave it.*

He put the pencil down about nine inches above the page and came back to it about a quarter of an hour later and read the first line of it again and did not write the conversion.

*Two sleeps short. Nine weeks and two sleeps since the gate, and the valley's own count is that the ninth week of the cold began on a morning it names in a way I do not use, and both of those are on this page, and neither of them is in any calendar I came with, and I am going to leave it exactly like that.*

That is the second time in two volumes that a figure has been refused in a book because there was no frame to put it in, and the first time it was refused on paper and the paper was thrown away, and nobody in that county knows to this day that it was tried, and nobody in this valley is going to know inside a generation either, and the reason it is in this book at all is that a notebook is a thing a man reads at the fourth hour of a morning he does not remember.

---

**The first three weeks of a winter in its ninth generation are the weeks in which a valley that is not starving looks exactly like a valley that is.**

He went up the valley over three weeks because a man who has been given a room over a byre for as long as he wants it does not stay in it.

There are eleven steadings and a scatter of hamlets and one market town, and the town is not a town, it is a wide place in the middle of the valley with a market cross and a smithy and a room over the cross that is the size of a barn, and the market is on a day that nobody here can tell you the date of except that it is a market day.

The Burn runs the whole length of it, about nine miles from the upper water to where it goes out under a rock, and it is a working river the whole way. It is not a river in a valley that is dying. There is a weir at the town with a leat that turns a wheel, and the wheel drives a mill that grinds, and there is a second wheel further down that drives something else, and both of them are turning in a valley in the ninth week of a winter in its ninth generation, and the man who looks after them is about thirty and cannot say anybody else's name for the thing the mill is.

Nobody here is hungry. That is the first fact and it is not a small one and it took him nine days to be sure of it, because a man from a county where a water table is a mile wide under four villages knows what the early days of a bad season look like, and he went and looked at the byres and the backs of the houses and the size of the children and the state of the boots.

The byres are full. Not full in the way a byre is full in the last week before you are in trouble, when the animals are standing in their own wet. Full in the way a byre is full when there is more stock than the ground under the house was ever meant to carry, and it has been like that for as long as the oldest man in the valley can remember being asked.

The stores hold. There are four seed houses in the valley and every one of them is a stone room with a slate floor and double doors and about half of it is not seed but barley for the two of the three weeks of a winter when there is nothing else, and there is enough of it, and it is counted twice in a winter by two families who do not know each other and who have never once compared their counts in a book.

The births are normal. Eleven children were born in the last twelve months in the eleven steadings and the town, and that is eleven, and the valley has about four hundred people in it, and a woman of about thirty with a slate comes into the room over the byre about once in ten days with the numbers on it and reads them out to whoever is in the room, and nobody has ever argued with her about a number.

**And the winter has been keeping for nine generations, and it has never once broken, and not one person alive in this valley has been cold in a way that killed them, and there is not a single orphan in it, and there is not a single person in it who is going to be sent for anything.**

That is the finding, and it is the whole of the reason there is nothing he can do here, and it is worth saying in his own words in the notebook that evening, because a man who cannot say what a place needs in the first three weeks of a season is a man who is going to be offered a job.

*There is nothing here that a man of my trade can fix and there is nothing here that a man of any trade can fix. The river runs. The stores hold. The births are normal. Nobody is hungry and nobody is cold and nobody is in a hole. Nine generations of a winter that has never broken.*

*And I have got a bar, and a chisel, and a level I do not own, and a leg, and there are about nine hundred yards of road in the county I came from and there is not one yard of made road in this valley, and I have not yet found out whether that is a fact about this valley or a fact about the ninth generation, and I have not asked, and I am going to be here long enough to be asked.*

---

**And he asked nobody anything for three weeks, and the oldest person in the valley asked him a question on the fourth.**

He is about eighty-one. He has cut withies for thatching since he was nine and he does not do it now and he sits on a bench outside a house near the upper water with a stick across his knees and the particular patience of a man who has been asked how he is and has stopped finding it interesting.

Two of his great-grandchildren are in the valley and one of them is dead, and the one that is dead is on the roll, and the roll is nine generations deep and it is the only roll in the valley, and a man of eighty-one has outlived two men who kept it.

“You are the man off the road,” he said.

“Yes.”

“There has not been a man off the road in this valley in my time and I have not seen one before that either, and I am not going to tell you what you are here for because I do not know and I am eighty-one and I have got about four things left that I am certain of, and one of them is the water in the Beck coming up in the fourth week, and it came up on the fourth this winter.”

They sat on the bench for a while. The Burn was somewhere below them and could be heard and not seen.

“Do you know how many winters this has been,” he said.

“No.”

“Nine,” said the old man. “Nine since the first one. And I will tell you the thing about it that nobody here will tell you, because I am the only one left who was asked to remember it in a way that stuck.

“Nobody has ever asked it.”

He waited, the way a man waits who has said a thing and wants to see whether a stranger has heard it.

“Asked it what,” said Aren Kest.

“Whether it is wanted,” said the old man. “Nine generations, and in every one of them somebody has stood up and asked this valley to keep the winter, in the room over the cross, in the cold, and it has never once been refused and it has never once been asked whether keeping it is the same as agreeing to it. Nobody has ever asked. Not one time in nine generations. And I have been in that room for it sixty times and I have never once been asked, and I am eighty-one, and I know exactly what I would have said, and nobody ever thought to want to hear it.”

He put the stick down across his knees again.

“Everybody in this valley would say to you that the winter is a good thing and that it is the best thing that has ever happened here, and every one of them would be telling the truth, and not one of them has been asked, and I have thought about that for about thirty years and I have never found out whether a thing that has never been asked about is a thing anybody is holding or a thing that is holding them.”

Then he said the thing that brought him back into the world, and he said it as if it were the second half of a sentence and not a speech.

“Have some more soup. You are thin. There is a great deal of it.”

---

**And the third week the woman of the Low Holm told him the one thing nobody in the valley says to a stranger, and she said it about a wall.**

“There is a room over the byre that is not a room for a stranger and I have said that once and I am not going to say it again, and I am going to tell you what is in it instead, because you are going to find out in about a week and I would rather you were told by me.

“It is a book. It is the winter book. It is nine generations deep and it is the only roll in this valley and there is no writ here and no bell and no office, and every person in this valley who has ever gone out of it is in that book, and every person who has ever come back into it is in that book, and about two hundred people are in that book who have never been anywhere at all, because they were put in it when they were born.

“I do not keep it. A man of fifty-eight keeps it who has kept it nineteen years and whose father kept it and whose father's father, and I have carried two pails of water up that stair every day for nineteen years and I have been in that room perhaps forty times, and I could not tell you one name in it. Do not ask me to. I have been asked twice and both times by a man who wanted something out of the room and not out of me.”

“He is going to be asked about the book by somebody in this valley whether he likes it or not,” said Aren Kest.

“Yes,” she said, “and I know, and it is not my business to stop it, and I would rather you heard it from me than worked it out in a week. And I will say the one thing I will say. He is not going to get that room in a day. Nobody in this valley has ever had that room in a day, and I have heard nine generations of men say so in this yard, and the men who said it were wrong every time about almost everything and they were never once wrong about that.”

She went out to the byre. There was a barrow to get out at the ford before the light went.

---

**And on the fourth week the woman with the slate came into the room over the byre and read out the numbers, and nobody argued with her, and that is how he learned what the word was.**

It is a slate about two feet by three and it has a groove round the edge for a thumb and a strip of hide tied at the top corner, and it is carried up and down this valley by one woman and it has been carried by one woman for about eleven years, and there is one other slate in this valley and it is not the same and it does not do the same work.

“Marrow,” she said. “Nine, then eleven, then ten, and the Low Holm one in the third week of the cold, which is a girl, which is the fourth at the Low Holm this winter and the woman over the byre says she is not going to be able to stand the winter on her own and the woman over the byre is right about that and I have written *needs a year* against it, and that is a thing I am allowed to write and not a thing I am allowed to say.”

Nobody argued. A man of about twenty sat on the sill with his hands round a cup and said that eleven was one under, and she said it was one over, and he said it was one under, and she said that last winter it had been one under as well and the winter before one over, and that a man who can remember two winters of a figure is a man who has a good memory and not a book, and he said that, and she wrote it down, and he put his thumb on the slate beside it, and the room was finished.

Then she said the thing that finished the evening.

“Twenty-one is nine generations and it is what we have been since the first of them, and there is no more of us than that, and the reason there is no more of us is that people go, and people go because they are put in the book when they are born and they are called what the book calls them and they go.” She put the slate under her arm. “You will find out what the word is. Everybody finds out. I am telling you it now so that you can have had it for a week before you ask anybody.”

That is the sum of a stone room over a byre in the ninth week of a winter in its ninth generation, and there was a fire in it, and a barrow went out to the ford at the ford, and the woman with the slate went down the bank, and a man of about eighty-one was still sitting on a bench near the upper water with a stick across his knees because the bench had not been asked to move.
