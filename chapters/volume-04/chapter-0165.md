# Chapter 0165

There is one woman in this valley of about four hundred people whose name anybody in it can say in the right order, and she is sixty-six, and she is of the eighth generation, and she is two steadings above the upper water in a stone room about the size of a cart with a press in it and eleven racks on a wall, and her name is Nev Treen and she gives it before anybody asks.

“You will be the man off the road,” she said. “Sit down. Not on that, that is the rack. On the stool, and put your stick in the corner where it will not go over the whey, and if you have been in a cheese house in your life you will take your boots off at the door and if you have not you will not, and either way you will not walk in the middle.”

He had not been in a cheese house in his life, and he found that he could not stop looking at the press, which is a thing that goes down and a thing that comes up and a thing that has a cloth over it, and that a woman turns twice a day for eleven months and that the whole of the room is warm and wet and smells like a bank in a warm week.

He had got there because of a woman of about thirty with a slate, who comes up the bank about once in ten days and reads the numbers out to whoever is in the room, and who had not argued with anybody about a number in eleven years. He had asked her, in the room over the byre, who the last person in this valley was asked anything by anybody was, and she had put the slate under her arm and thought about it for longer than a person thinks about a question like that.

“Not the ninth generation,” she had said. “The eighth. There is a woman up at the upper water. Ask her, and tell her what you want first, because she will tell you no to about nine things before she tells you anything.”

**And a woman of sixty-six had already told him no to eleven things in a quarter of an hour before she told him anything, and the eleven things were: no tea, no, do not sit there, no, you cannot see anything in this light, no, I did not say that, no, you are not going to write it down, no, I do not care what your county calls it, no, that is not a question, no, I have answered that, no, you may not stand in that doorway while I am turning the rack, no, you may not see the press, and no, the answer to that one is also no.**

“There is a woman up at the upper water,” she said, “and the last person this valley asked anything was her, forty years ago, in the room over the cross, in the cold, and I will tell you about it once and I will not tell you about it twice, and I am telling you now because you have not asked me one question yet and because you are going to go and stand in that room and I would rather you knew what you are going into.

“I was asked what I swore.

“They stood us in the room over the cross in the cold, in the winter my generation came of age, and the whole valley was in it, and a man stood at the back with a slate.

“It took nine minutes and it was the worst nine minutes of my life, and I am not going to tell you what I said, because you would have it in your head by the end of the week and I have had it in mine for forty years and I have never once said it out loud in a room and I am not going to start at sixty-six for a man I met this morning.

“And I told the ninth generation about it, because they were owed it, and I told them it was survivable.

“It is survivable. And I was wrong, and I know that I was wrong, and that is the whole of what I have got to say to you about it and I have said it and I am not going to improve on it.

“You are standing there waiting for me to say what was wrong with it. It is not the asking. The asking was right, it had never been done, and about half of us were glad and the other half were too frightened to be glad and I was one of the other half and I have never once been sorry about being one of the other half.

“What was wrong is that there was a man in the room with a slate. Not the asking. The man with the slate and the book behind him and the fact that everything any of us said went into a book, and a book does not know the difference between what a person said and what was true, and it has not known for forty years and it is not going to learn, and my hands have not been right since and I am told that is a thing about my age.”

She turned the rack over and did not say anything for a while, and her hands were the reason, and they were the reason in a way that was plainly not a thing about her age.

“I have been a woman for sixty-six years and those hands were good for fifty-eight of them and now there is a thing wrong with them that a woman of thirty could do in an hour, and the woman of thirty has said so to me twice and I have told her to go to hell both times and I did not mean it the second time, and there is nobody in this valley I can say that to, and that is what forty years does to a person, and I would like it recorded that I have been talking to a stranger about it for an hour and that is the first time in about nine years.”

**And then she said the thing that is the reason the ninth generation has not been asked, and she said it as a fact about a room and not as a story about herself.**

“There is no other way to ask anybody in this valley. That is the whole of it and it took me thirty years to be able to say it without crying about it, so I am going to say it now while I can.

“You cannot have a conversation in this valley. You can have a thing happen in a room over a cross in the cold in front of the whole valley with a man holding a slate, and whatever is said there is the thing that happened, because that is how this valley knows that anything happened at all. If you and I say a thing in this room this morning, then in about nine years there is no way at all anybody is ever going to know that you and I said it, and in about nine years one of us is going to be dead and the other is going to be alone in a stone room with a fact that never happened.

“So we do it the other way. We do it in the room, with the slate. And I do not know anybody in this valley who has ever been asked anything in any other way, and I have looked, and I have asked people, and I asked the man who keeps the book before anybody else and he said no, and I did not go on asking him, because I was forty-one and I had a bad thing to ask about and I did not have nine minutes spare for a man to be dignified in.

“The ninth generation will not be asked. And they are right, and I have told them so, and I have told them the nine minutes and the slate and the worst of it, and I told them it was survivable, and it was not, and I know it was not, and the reason it was not is that it took me everything I had and I have still got it and I am sixty-six and it is the only thing I have ever been proud of and I would not put it on a boy of twenty-two.”

“It is not an apology,” Aren Kest said.

“It is not an apology for anything.” She put the rack down. “There is nobody in this world I owe an apology to. There is a room over a cross and a stone room behind a chapel and about four hundred people and I have not done one thing to any of them that needs one. I told nine generations of my own family one thing about one morning and it was a lie, and that is a thing about a lie and not about a woman of sixty-six with bad hands.”

**And he asked her the one question he had come four hundred miles for, and it was not the question anybody in this valley would have asked her, and she answered it standing up, with her hands in a yoke, because she was taking the whey out.**

“How many of the eighth generation are still alive who were standing in that room.”

It took her a moment to count, which told him she had counted before and had not counted lately.

“Nine,” she said. “There were thirty-one in the aisle. Nine of us are left. Four of us have said for forty years that it was the worst thing that ever happened to them, in those words, and one of the four is me. And five of us have said it was the best, and two of those five have never said a word about it to anybody, and I have never asked them and I am not going to.”

“Nobody has ever asked the ninth generation which of the two they would rather.”

“No. And they have never been asked whether they would like to be in that room at all, and those are two different questions, and the first one is the one everybody in this valley has been asking each other about for forty years in about nineteen kitchens, and nobody has ever gone and stood in front of a person of twenty-two and said *would you like to be in that room*.”

“I can do that inside a day.”

“It is a thing you can do inside a day and it will get you thrown out of this valley, and I am telling you that as a woman who has watched about four people try something very like it.” She put the yoke down. “And I am telling you that I will stand in the aisle. And those are two separate pieces of information and I would like them kept apart.”

**And there was a thing in that room he was not going to do, and it was in a book over a byre, and it was the sixth spread.**

He had read the fourth. He had read about thirty names out of two hundred and eleven and had written none of them down and had not told anybody a word about that, and the fifth column stops in the middle of the fourth spread because the going stopped there.

The name column does not stop there. It is on the first six spreads and it is not on the last three, and he had not opened the sixth spread, and the sixth spread is the eighth generation, and a woman of sixty-six who had been asked forty years ago is in the book somewhere in a hand that is not the hand of anybody alive and under a mark with a stroke in it or without one, and he had made up his mind about the sixth spread on the bank on the morning of the fifth thing and he had not changed his mind since and he was not going to.

He had also made his mind up about the seventh and the eighth and the ninth, which is two hundred names he had already read and could not un-read, and about the thirty out of the two hundred and eleven, which was thirty people who were in a room with him.

That is five hundred and forty-odd facts about the living, and there is nowhere in this valley to put one of them, and he had a notebook in a bag under a bed in a county four hundred miles off and a bag in a room over a byre with a book in it that was not his, and he had decided on the sixth spread, and the decision was the first one he had made in six weeks that he did not think was the right one, and he made it anyway, and he did not write it down, and he was not going to be able to say afterwards what the right one had been.

**And then she took him up the hill at about the fourth hour to show him the room, because a woman who has been asked what she swore forty years ago knows exactly what the room is and knows that nobody off a road has ever been inside one.**

It is up four steps from the cross and through a door that shuts badly, and it is a room the size of a barn, and it is cold in there until the third hour even with the stove in it, and there are twenty-nine benches and they are fixed to the floor.

That is the part. They are fixed. Not carried in and set down and carried out again at the end of a winter, but bolted to the boards, twenty-nine of them, in four rows with an aisle down the middle, and they are a hundred and forty years old and every one of them has a number cut into the end of it.

“You can sit down anywhere you like in there,” said Nev Treen. “Nobody has ever done that. It is not an arrangement for sitting down in, it is an arrangement for standing in. They stand in the aisle. And the ones at the back are the ones who are the ninth generation and there is a reason for that and the reason is that when you are asked in this valley you are asked at the back.”

“Why at the back.”

“Because you can get out of the room at the back and it takes about a second and a half to get out of a room at the back, and I am not going to tell you it is a custom, because it is not a custom, it is a way of arranging a room, and the arrangement has been in this valley since before I was born and nobody has ever come to me and asked me to have it changed and I have wanted somebody to ask me to have it changed for about twenty-five years.”

She put her hand flat on the door frame, which is a thing everybody in this valley does to a door frame, and looked at the twenty-nine bolts, and then she said the thing she had been not saying since the cheese house.

“The ones at the back are numbered. Every bench in that room has a number cut into the end of it and the numbers are old, and there is one of them with a hundred and forty cut into it that is older than the bolts and was cut into the bench before the bench was bolted down.”

“What is one hundred and forty for.”

“For the ones who have been in the room. Not for a generation, not for a year. Every time anybody in this valley has stood in that aisle to be asked, a man with a slate has put a stroke on the bench, and the numbers are how many strokes are on that bench, and the bench at the back of the right-hand row has a hundred and forty on the end of it, and there is a woman of twenty-two in this valley who has never stood in that room and whose family has a bench.”

“Nobody has ever been struck off a bench.”

“Nobody. That is what I have been sitting in a cold room thinking about for two hours.” She let go of the frame. “A hundred and forty strokes on a bench is not a column of names. It is a bench, and it is in a room, and a room is a building, and a building is a thing that falls down. And that is the entire difference between what I went through and what you are about to do, and it is the difference between a thing being in a house and a thing being in a hand, and nobody in nine generations has worked that out, and I have had forty years and two bad hands and I have not worked it out either.”

“I will stand in it,” she said. “In the aisle, at the back, and I will be asked what I swore, in front of this valley, and it will take nine minutes and it will be the second worst nine minutes of my life, and I will do it because the ninth generation is owed it and because I am the only person alive who knows what being asked is like and that is not nothing.

“And I will not be standing there writing.

“That is what I have come to tell you, and it is the whole of what I have, and I would have told you it on the first morning without the other eleven noes if you had asked me the right question, and you did not ask me any question at all, and that is how it got out of me, and a woman of about thirty told me that was coming and she gave me a week's warning and she was right to within about a day.”

**And they came down the four steps together and out into the cross, and the light was doing the thing it does, and the room over the cross with its twenty-nine bolts was shut behind them, and nobody in that valley had asked the ninth generation anything yet, and one woman of the eighth generation had said in front of a byre that she would stand in the aisle at the back and be asked again, and she had said the one sentence that nobody off a road had come four hundred miles for, which was that she would not be standing there writing.**
