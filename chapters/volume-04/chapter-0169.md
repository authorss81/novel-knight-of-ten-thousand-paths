# Chapter 0169

The wall was finished on the sixteenth day and nothing happened on the seventeenth.

That is a real thing and it is the thing about work in a place that has had none: there is no ceremony at the end of a wall. Sixteen men put their barrows down at about the fifth hour and cleaned the lime off their hands in a bucket and went home, and eleven of the sixteen went up over the bank on a line, in ones and twos, and the yard was a yard again by the middle of the light.

Wystan Roole did not say anything about it, and neither did anybody else, and the string came down and went in a pocket, and the plumb bob went in a pocket, and the two pegs stayed where they were, because a man does not pull the pegs of a line he has finished.

And then it was ordinary.

The mill wheel turns in the warm part of the day and not in the cold part and the man at the mill does not know why and has not asked. The low-country men are getting their backs into a load of salt and oil and iron for the trip down, and the trip down takes nine days and the trip up takes seven with a load, and they will be in this valley in the warm week and nobody has ever thought to make an appointment of it. The beasts go out of the byres that were being emptied about eleven weeks ago. The woman of the Low Holm got her floor mended by a man of about sixty who lays forty sacks of stone in lime and does nothing else, and it took him two days, and it is lime and rubble and about four inches of it, and it is not a dinner plate any more.

And about the fourth hour on the eighteenth day, a man of fifty-eight came up the outside stair of the room over the byre, which he had done about forty times in nine weeks, and shut the book, and said the thing he had been not saying for nineteen years in a form anybody could act on.

**And it is not a reading that a man of fifty-eight has wanted for six years. It is a cold room, in the cold, with about forty people in it, and it is not the book, and it has not been the book since the fourth hour of a morning about a week ago.**

“I want the oath found,” said Iven Sarr, “and I want it read in the room over the cross, in the cold, by a man of about my age, to a room of about the size it was, and I want all of it read out and not the parts that have held.

“Not the book. The book is nineteen years of a man putting down a day and a mark and a hand, and the copying is how I know what the thing was, and I have said that to you and you wrote it down for about a minute and then you did not write it down. The oath is not in that book. It is not in this room. I have looked, and I am not going to tell you where I have looked, and that is not a trick, it is that I have found nothing in four places and I have a fifth place left and I am not going to have it looked at before I have read it myself.”

“What is in the book, then.”

“Four lines at the head of the first spread, and they are the rule about keepers, and I have read them about nine hundred times, and I will not read them to you, and I am telling you that I have read them nine hundred times so that you know that the thing I am not giving you is a rule and not the oath.” He put his hand flat on the shut book. “And you will notice that I have said the whole of what I want and that I have not once in that sentence said anything about the ninth generation, and you will notice that because a man of your age notices things, and I am telling you that I noticed it myself about four days ago and I have been angry about it for two of those days.”

“The ninth generation will not be asked. They said so in a market and the word went round by the next morning and about nine people in four steadings have it. That is not a decision. It is a thing that happened in a doorway and it is standing.”

“It is standing. And I am not asking them and I am not going to ask them and I told you in the second week that I wanted them told what it signed and I do not want it any more, and that is the fourth thing in nineteen years that I have wanted and then stopped wanting, and the other three are yours.”

“Then the reading is not an asking.”

“The reading is not an asking and that is the whole of what I have learned in nine weeks from a man with a limp, and it has taken me nineteen years and a stranger to be able to say it in one sentence.” He squared the book. “I am going to read out an oath in a cold room in front of whoever turns up. I am not going to ask anybody to be there. I am going to stand in the aisle at the front and read all of it out, and if a man of the ninth generation stands at the back of the right-hand row then I will read it slower, and if nobody stands at the back of the right-hand row then I will read it at the speed of a man reading out a thing he has not read in nineteen years, and both of those are the same reading and neither of them is a demand.”

“Who reads it.”

“You do,” said Iven Sarr. “I cannot read it and be the man asking the question. That is the arrangement and it has been the arrangement in about nine places in this case in four years and a half and it is the only thing I know that is certain.”

“It may not be there.”

“It may not be there. And if it is not there then I am sixty and I have wanted a thing for six years and I have not got it, and I will say that out loud in a room in front of about forty people, and that will be the last time anybody in this valley hears me ask for anything, because I will not be able to stand up again after it.”

“Where.”

“Next market day. At the third hour, which is when the room is least cold and when about forty people can be got in it, and the woman of the upper water has told me that is the hour it happens at. I will put it in the room. I will not put it on a slate and I will not put it on a bench.”

**And the man off the road said yes in about a second, and then spent a quarter of an hour putting four things on the table, and three of them were conditions and one of them was not, and the man of fifty-eight wrote none of them down because there is nowhere in this valley to write a condition down.**

“Yes. And four things.

“I will read what is there. If it is a page, I read the page. If it is two pages, I read both. If it is a bit of a wall with something scratched on it, I read the scratching and I will say out loud that I am reading scratching. I am not going to read the parts that have held, I am not going to smooth anything, I am not going to say *and here the meaning is* and I am not going to say *what he meant by that was* and I am not going to give a room of about forty people a version of anything.

“I am not going to explain a hand. Not the one in the ninth spread, not the one on the back of the last leaf, not any of them. If a person in that room asks me what a hand is, I will say that I do not know, and it will be the fourth time I have said that in this valley and it will not improve.

“And I am not going to be asked anything. Not by the man of fifty-eight and not by anybody who comes up the hill to the stair. If somebody in that room asks me a question I am going to read on, and if somebody asks me the same question after, I will answer it after, and the reason is that the last thing this valley needs is a man from off the road answering questions in the middle of a reading.

“And the thing that is not a condition.” He looked at the shut book. “I am going to read it slowly, and I am not going to know what any of it means, and I have never read anything in my life that I did not know what it meant, and that is the reason I am the wrong man and the reason I am the right one, and I would like that on a page somewhere and I know that there is not going to be one.”

“That is four things and one of them is not a condition and the other three are the whole of what I have got, and I am fifty-eight and I have never had this from anybody and I did not know it was available.”

**And a man of eighty-one was told about it on the nineteenth day by a woman who makes numbers and not by anybody else, and he said the only thing he said about it, and then he asked her to say it again.**

The bench near the upper water is not a long walk from the ford and a woman of about thirty with a slate goes past it about once in ten days and this time she stopped.

“Next market day,” she said. “At the third hour. In the room over the cross. A stranger is going to read the oath out.”

The old man took a while.

“I have been in that room sixty times,” he said, “and I have never once heard what it says.”

“That is what I have told you.”

“No, that is not what I am saying and you know it is not.” He put the stick across his knees. “I am saying that after next market day there will be a man in this valley who has heard it, and I will have been in the room and heard it too, and nobody will have asked me anything, and that is the right way round for once, and I want you to notice that I am eighty-one and I have just been told about a thing in a valley of four hundred people and I am the only person in it who is pleased, and you may write that down, and I know you will not, and I have got about four things left that I am certain of and that is the newest of them.”

**And a woman of sixty-six came down off the hill on the same afternoon and said that she would be in the room, and then said the other thing, which was the thing she had been not saying for two days.**

“I am coming as a person in a room and not as a person in an aisle,” said Nev Treen. “Nobody is going to ask me anything, and I am not going to stand at the back of the right-hand row and be asked what I swore, and I have given that up. It is mine to give up and I have given it up this afternoon and I have not told the ninth generation yet and I am going to tell them tonight and they are going to say nothing about it, because they are twenty-two and they do not say anything about anything, and I shall have told nine generations of my own family that I am not going to be asked and every one of them will hear the sentence as a thing about a woman of sixty-six and not one of them will hear it as a thing about a bench with a hundred and forty on the end of it.”

“That is not a small thing to give up.”

“No,” said Nev Treen. “It is the only thing I have ever had that was mine and I have given it up for a man reading a page out in the cold and I would like it written down, and there is not going to be a page, and I have noticed that, and I have decided that I do not mind, and that is new, and it happened this afternoon, and that is the whole of what the winter has done to me.”

**And a man of thirty-four with sixteen men and no work, who had been sent for and had come, and who had been standing at the bottom of that stair for about four minutes, said the thing he has wanted to say in a council in this valley twice and has been told twice that there is no road to build.**

“He will not come because nobody comes to anything in that room in this valley unless the whole valley comes, and the whole valley comes when there is a winter in it, and that is once a generation.”

“Then I shall read it to whoever comes.”

“You shall read it to about nine people and a man of eighty-one and a man who makes nails and a woman of thirty with a slate, and every one of those nine people will go back down the hill and say that a stranger read out a thing in the cold, and in about four years there will be no way at all to check what it was, and that is what I have been trying to tell you for nine years with a wall.” He put his hands in his coat. “And I will say the other thing now because there is a man in this valley who reads floors and I have been waiting nine years to say it to somebody.

“A road is not a thing you are given. A road is a thing you dig. That is the whole of it and it is nine words and there is not one of them this valley has any use for, because there is not one person in it who has ever dug one.

“We have had a hand at every wall in this valley for nine generations. Nine generations of a craft. And there is no road, and the reason there is no road is that a road costs nine winters and a road pays nothing back, and a valley that cannot leave is a valley that cannot argue with anybody about anything, and I have been trying to say that to a council for nine years and I am going to say it to a man in a doorway in about a week and get exactly the same answer, and it will be the right answer, and I will go on digging.”

“That is a good sentence and I have heard it before.”

“You have heard it before,” said Wystan Roole, “and I have said it before, and the fact that a thing has been said twice in a valley means it is true and not that it is working, and I would like that put on the wall when it is dry, and it is not going to be on the wall because there is no lime left.”

**And on the evening of the eighteenth day the man of fifty-eight took a leaf out of the chest under the boards, and it was the same shape and the same weight as the leaf he had written a sentence on in the fifth year of keeping the book and had not looked at in nine years, and he turned it over and wrote the day on the back of it, and the back of a leaf is the one place in this valley where a thing can be written that is not a line in the roll.**

He did not write a month on it and he did not write a year and he did not write the hour, and the reason is that this valley has no months and no years and no hour anybody keeps, and a man of fifty-eight has been keeping one of the three for nineteen years and knows to the day what the other two mean.

He wrote four words. *Next market day.*

And then he put the leaf under the book on the trestles, and the book went over the leaf and the leaf was not visible from anywhere in the room, and that is the whole of the arrangements that have been made for the reading of the oath of a valley nine generations old, and it is a leaf of paper under a book in a room over a byre, and it is the only piece of paper in this valley with a date on it that is not a birth.
