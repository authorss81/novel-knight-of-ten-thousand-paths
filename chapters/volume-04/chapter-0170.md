# Chapter 0170

The leaf came out from under the book at the fourth hour and it was on the trestles by the time the man off the road came up the outside stair, and the man of fifty-eight was standing at the window with his back to the door.

He had not slept. He said so in the first four words and then said it again in the fifth, and then did not come back to it for half an hour, which is a considerable piece of self-knowledge for a man of fifty-eight, and he did not appear to think of it as any kind of achievement.

“Read it out,” he said. “That is all of it. I have been standing at this window since the light came off the ice working out how to say the rest of it, and I have got the rest of it now, and I would rather say it at you than say it at a wall.”

“There is a lamp on the sill and the fire is going and the door is shut,” said Aren Kest. “Say it.”

“There is a door in the back wall of this byre,” said Iven Sarr. “Under the eaves. It is four steps down and it is not a door anybody uses, and it is in the wall that my father's father built this room against, and I have known about it since I was a boy, having looked at that wall my whole life. I went down it at the seventh hour last night with a lamp.”

“And.”

“The room is about four feet by six. The floor is a board and the walls are stone and they are older than the byre. There is a sheet of paper on the board with a stone on it, and the sheet has columns ruled on it, and it is a form.”

“You read it.”

“No.”

It took him a moment to go on and he did not take the moment to be dignified over it.

“I stood in the doorway with the lamp and I looked at the shape of it, and I am going to be exact with you on how much I know, because you are a man who counts things and I have watched you not count things for as long as you have been in this valley, and I am not going to be one of the things you count.

“I know that it is ruled. I know that it is ruled in more columns than my book has. I know that there is writing on it and that the writing is dry and that it is not my writing and not my father's writing. I do not know what is in the columns. I do not know who ruled it. I do not know how long it has been on that board. I have stood in the doorway of a room in this valley holding a lamp and I have not gone four feet into it, and I am fifty-eight years old, and I have kept the only book in this valley for nineteen years, and I have just told a man off a road that I could not do a thing with a lamp in my hand.”

“Why,” said Aren Kest.

“Do not do that.”

“Do what.”

“Ask me a question about it.” He turned round, and his own face had not been told anything. “That is what you were about to do, and you have done it four times in this valley, and every time you have taken a thing out of me that I had not intended to hand over. I have decided tonight that I am not going to be asked one word about the room at the back of my byre.”

“That is fair.”

“It is not fair. It is a man of thirty-two being decent, and I want it noticed that I called it unfair before you corrected me, because I have found in nineteen years that everything I have is that I say the difficult thing first and then people let me have the second half.”

They had the second half inside nine seconds and he did not look like a man enjoying it.

“Then you will have it in your own words and it will be short,” he said. “A form is a thing with columns in it that somebody else made. My book is a thing with columns in it that I made, and every line in my book is a day and a mark and a hand, and I know what every mark in it is because I put it there or my father did. A form is a thing that arrives. And when a thing arrives in this valley you put something in it, and I have watched four people in my life answer a form and I have not one of them still living in this valley, and I am not going to be a fifth, and that is the reason, and I would like it noticed that nobody asked me.”

“You did not have to be asked.”

“I have never in my life said no to a piece of paper without somebody in a room being disappointed in me,” said Iven Sarr. “That is new and I do not trust it and I am going to stand in a cold room this morning on the strength of it.”

**And then he told the man off the road what he wanted, and it was not what he had wanted six weeks earlier, and he said so himself before anybody could.**

“In the ninth week of this cold I wanted that book read, and then I wanted the oath found and read in the room over the cross, all of it and not the parts that have held. That was a big want. It was the biggest thing I have wanted in nineteen years, and I said all of it to a stranger in a room in the ninth week of this cold and I have not been ashamed of one word of it and I am not going to be today.

“Since then I have learned what a form is from a man with a limp, and I am not ashamed of that either. And I still want the reading, and I want it to be four words long.

“Read the leaf out at the third hour. And then, when you have read it, say out loud in that room, in your own words, that there is nothing else this morning. Not that I have not found it. That there is nothing else to read.

“Because if I stand in a cold room and tell a roomful of people that I have looked in a fifth place and there is a thing in it, then this valley will talk about a thing in a room for a year, and nine of them will be right about it, and none of them will have seen anything, because none of them can, and in four years there will be nine accounts of a form in a room behind a byre and not one of them will be checkable against the other eight. I have spent nineteen years refusing to make a second copy of one book and I am not going to make nine of a thing in a back room over a winter.

“A thing four hundred people can check is worth more to me than a thing they cannot. I have found that out at the age you are now and it has taken me nineteen years and I would like it on a page and there is not going to be one, and that is why it is yours to say instead of mine.”

“How many are coming.”

“I do not know, and I am not going to guess at it, and I am not going to stand at that door counting faces. If there are nine then there are nine, and I have wanted forty for six years and there was never going to be forty.”

“And I will read it as a date or as a thing you wrote.”

“That is the question and I have not got an answer to it and I have had all night.” He put his hand flat on the leaf. “If you read it as a date then nine people will sit in a cold room being told what day it is, and this valley keeps no days and not one person in it can tell you what day anything was. If you read it as a thing I wrote then nine people will sit in a cold room being told that a man of fifty-eight wrote four words on a leaf and put it under a book, and that is a fact about me and it is not about them at all, and I would rather it was about me. The only thing I am sure of after nineteen years is that I am the one in this valley who can be got at.”

“Then that is how I will read it,” said Aren Kest. “And people will ask me what it means.”

“They will ask me. I will be standing in front of them with the leaf in my hand and that is where a question goes, and I have been dodging questions for nineteen years and last night it came to me that what I have been doing is dodging the four that would have mattered. So let them ask me. That is the arrangement, and it is the first arrangement I have made in nineteen years that I have not made by saying no to a thing.”

He squared the leaf with both hands and then did not pick it up, which is a thing a man of fifty-eight thinks hard about for nine seconds and then does not mention, and they went down the outside stair, and the fourth tread said so under the second boot, and neither of them mentioned it.

**And the main world ran on without him, on the ninth of Rainmonth of the year after, a Tuesday and not a market day, and the scales under that room had not been used since the fourth of it.**

The room over a weigh-house is the warmest room in that town on a day the scales are used and the coldest on a day they are not, and a hearing came to the second hour of it.

The resolution of the twentieth of Hearthmonth of the year before had entered that this office would hear the delegation of the Hearth Marches on the terms the Marches had set, at the second hour, in that room, and that the delegation was not to be sent for, and that if the delegation did not come the hearing was not adjourned and not refused and it stood. It was entered against no person. The claim number on the wrapper was the number of the account, which is a thing a clerk may do twice and which a keeper may not do once.

The keeper of records sat in one of the two chairs. The second copyist sat in the other with a sheet of fair-copy paper and a rule, because a hearing that is not minuted is a thing that happened, and the day-book was the only book in that office nobody could be served with, and the copy was a copy and went on a shelf with the number against the office and nothing against a person.

The junior clerk came up the two flights at a quarter of the hour with the post and put the bag on the table and went down again, and the bag was the ordinary post of a Tuesday and there was nothing in it that concerned the morning.

The second hour went.

Nobody came. Nobody was sent. The resolution said both of those things in the plainest words it could find, and the road at the other end of it had been shut in four lines of writing, and the person who used to hold that end had been struck and the county had not replaced her in time, and none of that was in dispute in that room, and none of it touched anybody sitting in it.

“There is a thing I have to say out loud,” said the keeper, “and it is not a complaint, and it is not about the county four hundred miles off, and it is the second time in four years and a half that I have had to say a thing like it in this room and the first time was worse.”

She put her finger on the resolution and read the first of the six clauses of it out.

“A body corporate that is sued is represented by a person. That is what the door and the number are for. I have a door and a number and four pence a year, and I am a person, and I am employed and I am not appointed, and if this office hears a thing then this office is a party to it, and a party can be sued, and I have just made this office a party to a matter against a county that is not on any map, because eighteen months ago I agreed in this room to hear a delegation.”

“That is correct,” said the second copyist, who had the tip of her pen half an inch off the paper.

“It is correct and I did it for the right reason and the right reason has now cost me the only thing I have got, which is a room that a person can be turned out of by a person with a door. I am not saying that to anybody in a room. I am saying it to a second copyist at a table.”

“You cannot un-resolve it.”

“No, and I would not, and if the Marches had sent anybody I would have read out every line of it and been glad. What I have lost is not the thing I agreed to. What I have lost is a thing I did not know was attached to it, which is that hearing a thing makes you one of the sides of it, and there is no instrument in this kingdom for hearing a thing without becoming one of the sides of it, and I have now found that out four years and a half in.”

She wrote for about nine minutes and she wrote four lines, and the second copyist copied them as they went, and the day and the hour went in as two things and not one, because that is the county's rule and this office has one and the office's own form is ruled in seven.

The date. The claim number, against the office. That no person came. And that no person was sent, with the resolution that said so, and the day it was entered.

And then she put the pen down.

“You have not written the fifth line,” said the second copyist.

“No.”

“The minute says a room with a keeper in it at the second hour and no person at the other side of the table.”

“Yes.”

“That is a room with nobody in it.”

“Yes,” said the keeper, “and it is in a book that cannot be served, and in four years somebody will come to this office and ask what happened at a hearing on this day, and the answer will be on that page, and the page will say that there was nobody here, and that will be a true answer, and it will take the person who asks two years to work out that it is true and that it is not all of it.”

“Then would you have me write the rest of it.”

“I would have you write what I have written and nothing else,” said the keeper, “because what a thing means is not a fact, and a fact is what this office is for, and if I put a meaning on that page then in forty years somebody will read the meaning and skip the fact, and they will be able to do it because I made it easy for them.”

She squared the two sheets, and put the copy on the shelf with the number against the office, and the sheet she had written on went into the day-book at the page with the rest of that day's work, which is the ordinary place for a thing that is true.

“He is not in this county,” said the second copyist, and then stopped, because there was nobody in the room it made sense to finish it to.

“He is not in this county and he has not sent anything and nothing has come from him, and I have not asked, and it is not a question for a minute,” said the keeper, “and I am going to close this now, and the second copyist will take the bag down, and I have a column of eleven counties entered in the early part of this year that I have looked at four times in four minutes and about which I still cannot say a word, and that is the state of this room and I am content with it.”

**And at the third hour of a market day, in a room the size of a barn with twenty-nine benches bolted to the floor in four rows, ten people came, and a man of thirty-two read four words off a leaf.**

The room was cold at the door and cold at the back and four degrees less cold at the front, where a stove had been lit at the second hour by somebody who had not come in to hear anything. The aisle between the four rows is four feet wide and it goes from the door to the stove, and forty people is what the room takes, and ten is what was in it.

Iven Sarr stood in the aisle at the front. He did not put the leaf on a bench and he did not put it on the stove and he held it in his left hand at about the height of his belt, which is where you hold a thing when you have not held it in your hand for a day and a night.

“He is going to read what is on this,” he said, to the room and not to anybody in it. “There is no other paper in this room and there has not been one in nine generations, and I have not got anything else, and I am going to say that out loud after he has read it. You will not ask him anything until I have said it. After that you can ask me anything you like and I will stand here and be asked, and I will be here as long as anybody wants to ask.”

The old man was at the back of the right-hand row, on a bench, with his stick across his knees.

“Go on,” he said.

Aren Kest read it. He read it in the voice of a man reading out a figure off a board, which is the only voice he has for it, and the leaf was in a hand of fifty-eight and the four words were on the back of it and he read them without stopping and without putting a thing round them.

*Next market day.*

And then he said the thing he had been asked to say, in his own words, as promised, and he said it in about four seconds.

“That is all of it. There is nothing else to read this morning, and he has not got anything else, and I am not holding anything back that anybody handed me.”

The room did what a room does. It waited four seconds for a second half, and there was not one, and ten people in a cold room in a valley of four hundred found out at the same moment that they were not going to be told anything else, and not one of them said anything.

The man of eighty-one put his hand out.

“Say it again,” he said.

“Say what again.”

“Whatever it is. Once,” said the old man. “I have been in this room sixty times. I have never once heard what is in it.”

So a man of thirty-two with a limp read four words off a leaf a second time in a cold room, and the second reading was slower and it was not better, and the room heard the same four words, and about three people in the room understood that the old man had not asked for the words.

Iven Sarr said the sentence he had come to say, and it was the sentence he had said to a stranger in a room over a byre, and the room was not the room.

“I have wanted a thing for six years and I have not got it. I am fifty-eight years old. And I thought there would be forty people in this room and there are nine and a girl, and I am not going to stand here and tell anybody that I would have preferred the forty, because there is no version of the forty in which any of the nine is here, and I have wanted forty for six years and I did not know until this morning that wanting forty was the same as not wanting the nine.”

Nobody argued with him and nobody comforted him and nobody said anything at all for a minute and a half, which in a cold room with a stove at the front is a long time, and it went the way that minute and a half went, which is that the stove ticked and somebody's boots went on the boards at the back and then stopped.

Then the questions started, and every one of them went to the man with the leaf in his hand, because he was standing in front of them with it and he had told them in advance that he would be there to be asked, and the man of fifty-eight answered eleven of them in about half an hour and did not improve on one of them.

The man who made nails asked whether it meant there was going to be another market day.

“There is a market day every so often,” said Iven Sarr. “I have not got a way of saying how often. I have never been able to say how often and I have not been asked for six years.”

“That is the first straight answer anybody in this valley has ever given me,” said the nail man, “and I have been at that stall four market days running and I have asked about nine things.”

A woman of about thirty with a slate took the slate out from under her arm while this was going, and held it in both hands, and looked at it, and put it back under her arm, and did not write on it, and nobody asked her why and she did not say.

The woman of about sixty-six came down off the hill and stood on the left of the aisle halfway back and took her hands out of her sleeves and put them flat on the end of a bench, on the wood, and kept them there for a minute, and then put them back in her sleeves. She had not done that in that room in forty years and there was nobody in the room who knew that except the man off the road, and he did not say so, and she looked at him while she did it, and he did not nod.

A man of about thirty-four with sixteen men and no work stood at the back by the door with his hands in a rope-belt and counted the room twice, which is a thing a man does who has spent nine years counting rooms.

“Nine of us,” he said, “and a man of thirty-two who is not of this valley, and there are about four hundred people in this valley, and I have just said that number out loud in that room and nobody in it has said it before in my hearing.”

“You have not said it before in my hearing,” said Iven Sarr, “and I have kept a book for nineteen years and I have never put it at the head of anything, because a book of days and marks and hands is not a book of how many people there are, and I have had two hundred in that spread and four hundred in the valley, and I never once had a place to write the second figure down.”

“Write the second one down on the wall,” said Wystan Roole, “and I will cut it in.”

“No,” said Iven Sarr, and then, before anybody could have looked at him for it, “and that is the last no of this morning and I have had four of them and I intend to stop.”

And then the girl of about nine, who had come in with the woman who had a yoke, and who had gone round the front bench and touched the end of it, and who had a barrow to get out at the ford most days and had not gone and got it out.

“Is that all of it,” she said.

The room turned round, which a room of ten people does very fast.

“No,” said Iven Sarr.

She waited. He did not go on, and after about four seconds she put her hands in her sleeves, and said “All right,” and went and got her barrow out at the ford, and it was all she said in that room, and the arrangement that had been standing between the two of them since the ninth week of this cold was that neither of them asked the other one anything, and it survived her asking a question of a third person inside four feet of him, and he noticed that, and he did not improve on it.

The young man of the ninth generation was at the back of the right-hand row when it started and he was still at the back of the right-hand row when it was over, and the reason that row is at the back is that you can get out of that room at the back in about a second and a half, and there was nothing in the room to get out of, and nobody asked him anything, and he had not asked to be asked, and he was twenty-two.

Aren Kest stood at the front with the leaf handed back to him and did not go and stand near him, on purpose, and the reason was that a man of thirty-two from a county four hundred miles off standing four feet off a boy of twenty-two in a room where nobody has asked the boy anything is a man arranging himself, and he knew it, and he stayed at the front.

They came down the four steps and out into the cross and the light was doing the thing it does at that hour, which is to stop being one thing and start being another without a line between them.

“There is a door in the back wall of that byre,” said Iven Sarr, “under the eaves, and it is four steps down.”

“I know. I have been in the yard about forty times in nine weeks and I have looked at every wall in it.”

“Then you will not mind that I have not gone down it tonight and that I am not going to, and I would like it said out loud in the open, once, by me, because in nine years there will be a woman in that market who remembers a man of fifty-eight standing in a cold room this morning and saying the word no, and I would like there to be a second thing she remembers about the same morning, and the second thing is that I said it in a yard and not in a room.”

He went to the door of the back room and stood on the top step and did not go down, and Aren Kest came and stood on the step beside him and did not go down either, and the two of them looked at a low door in a stone wall under the eaves of a byre in a valley in its ninth generation.

He shut it with his palm flat on the wood, which is a thing everybody in that valley does to a door.

The woman of the Low Holm had left her own door open behind them and there was a light in the byre and the trough was standing where the trough has stood for sixty years, and the wall was outside in the dark, four hundred feet of the best wall anybody in that valley had ever seen, holding up a byre instead of being the first four hundred yards of a road. Neither of the two men said anything about it on the way up the outside stair. The stair has nine treads and one of them is soft, and the man of fifty-eight went up first, and the man off the road went up second, and the fourth one said so under both of them.
