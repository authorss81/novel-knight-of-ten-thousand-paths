# Chapter 0162

The light in a room over a byre in this valley does not do what light does in a county that keeps its hours. It comes off snow and it thins, and then it comes off ice, and then at some point in the middle of it there is a stretch that is neither, and a man who has been somewhere else for six weeks sits over a book and waits for the light to change the way he would wait for a bell, and an hour after he stops waiting for it he notices that he has stopped.

The fire had gone down twice and he had put his hands over it twice and the third time he did not, and he read the ninth spread of the winter book of a valley four hundred miles from any county that is on a map for an hour and about ten minutes, and he did not read one name out loud, and he wrote nothing down, because there was nothing to write down.

**And the first thing about the page is that it is not a page of names, and the man who kept it for nineteen years had told him that there would not be, and he had not understood until he had the fire on his hands that he had not understood.**

Three columns are ruled and they are ruled the same way all the way down every spread in the book, and the ruling is a fine even line that goes deeper than you would expect from something done by a man with a pen at a table, and the day is in the first of them and the mark is in the second and the hand is in the third.

A hundred and ninety lines or thereabouts on the spread. He did not count them twice, because he counted them the first time and the second time gave him the same answer, and a man who counts a page of people twice ought to be asked what he is doing.

The day column runs down and never goes back up. Every day on the page is later than the day above it, and there is not one place in a hundred and ninety lines where a day is out of order, and he has laid out boundary ditches and read four men of a yard's week-book and he has never once seen a page that behaved this well. A man could take the order of a whole valley off that page without reading a single thing on it that was a person.

**And the second thing about the page is that the mark column has three marks in it and the ninth generation is two hundred of the same one.**

A small mark like a barred T, which is the mark for a person who is in this valley and has not gone out of it.

A mark with a stroke under it, which is the mark for a person who has gone out of this valley, and the stroke is the only part of the mark that means anything, and every going in the fourth spread and the fifth and the sixth has that stroke under it.

And a mark with a stroke through it from the top, which is the mark for a person who has gone out of this valley and come back to it, and there are nine of those in the whole book and the last one is a hundred and forty years old.

The whole of the ninth spread is the first mark. Two hundred people and one mark, and the mark says nothing about any of them except that they are here.

He sat with that for a while.

Nine spreads, and a hundred and forty years of a valley, and a man of fifty-eight who had kept it nineteen years and who had told him on a stair in a fortnight, in his own words, that there had been two reasons in nine spreads and never a third and that he had looked for a third every winter for nineteen years. And here was the reason the ninth spread is two hundred of one mark, and it is not a reason anybody had to look for. The ninth generation has not gone anywhere. The ninth generation is here. That is what the mark says and that is all it says, and two hundred-odd people in a valley of four hundred are in a book in a room over a byre as a small barred T, two hundred times, and there is no way on that page to tell one of them from another of them.

There was a boy in this county who could not walk and was in a bench's minute four times in a year as *the boy at the third gate*, and everybody in that county knew which boy he was, and that is what a page of people is for.

**And the third thing about the page is that the hand in the third column does not vary, and that took him the better part of an hour, and that he had to be shown it and could not have got there alone.**

He read every one of the two hundred hands twice, and they are the same hand, and he had a fair idea of what two hundred of the same hand looks like, because his own is not the steadiest thing he has ever done on a cold morning, and he knows what happens to a hand at the bottom of a page of two hundred lines. It goes small. It goes fast. It gets worse.

That hand did not do any of those things anywhere in a hundred and ninety lines, and the pen was not a pen he knew, and the ink was not ink he had seen, and it was on the first line and it was on the last line and it was the same size on both.

Near the foot of the spread there was a day he knew.

He put his finger on it and his finger was cold and he took it off again and put it back, which is a thing a man does to be sure of a thing.

Nine days ago, in a yard at the ford with a barrow to get out, a girl of about nine had stood at a gate with her hand on the bar and told him that there was nobody on the road and had not been anybody on the road all winter, and had refused to give him her name and had given him the name of a house instead, and the day she was born is four lines up from the bottom of the ninth spread of the only roll in a valley of about four hundred people, in a small mark, with a hand in it that is not the hand of anybody alive.

He now knew a fact about a child of nine. He had not asked for it, nobody had offered it, and there was nothing whatever he could do with it. He could not write it down, because it was a name and because it was somebody else's. He could not tell the girl, because there was no way to say the first half of it without the second. He could not ask the woman over the byre, because the woman over the byre would have to look in a book to check it, and a woman looking in a book to check a thing about a child is the whole of what this valley has done to itself for nine generations.

He sat with that for a while as well, and the fire went down again, and he did not put his hands over it.

**And then he went to the fourth spread, because it was the only place in nine spreads with a name in it, and it is the sixth generation, and there are two hundred and eleven names in the column at the right-hand edge, and he read all of them in the same hour.**

Two hundred and eleven names in a column four inches wide, written small, and a man who had been in the valley six weeks and had heard about forty names spoken aloud in that time.

He found thirty or thereabouts.

He did not go looking for them. They came up out of the page on their own, the way a thing comes up out of a wall. There was a name in the fourth line that is the name of the woman who carries water up the outside stair of the Low Holm and shut a gate with a wedge and a piece of shoddy oak, and there was a name eleven lines under that one which is the name of the man of about eighty-one who sits on a bench near the upper water and was asked nothing in nine generations. There was a name about two thirds of the way down which is the name of the man who makes nails, and it is his mother's, and the column further out has a stroke under it, and that is the whole of the record of her: she went out of this valley and her son has a stall in a market four market days running because there is nothing here to put nails in.

Those thirty names out of two hundred and eleven, and every one of them is a person who has been in a room with him in six weeks, and he read them all, and he wrote none of them down, and the not writing took him forty minutes or more longer than the writing would have taken.

A man who counts things for a living read a page of two hundred names and did not write one of them down, and he could not have told anybody afterwards why he had done it the slow way, and he sat there until his hands were cold again and he could not have told anybody why he had done it at all.

The column at the right-hand edge of the fourth spread stops in the middle of that spread and does not begin again anywhere above it. Five spreads, three of them blank of names at the right-hand edge, and the two hundred and eleven names stop where they stop because that is the last year anybody in this valley went out of it and was gone long enough that a name in a book was a fact about where they were rather than a fact about a family.

That is the whole of the fifth column. It is not a register of who this valley is proud of. It is a note of where the valley's people have gone, and it went out of use the same way the going went out of use, and nobody in nine generations has ever gone to look at it and asked why it stopped.

And after the two hundred names, on the last leaf of the ninth spread, there was another page.

It was ruled exactly the same way, and it had no lines in it. The corner of it was not creased and the ruling on it was not new and had not been rubbed, and there was a ruled space at the top of it for a heading, and the space was empty, and the first ruled line below that empty space was the first place on that page where a person could be put if anybody ever wanted to.

The ninth generation is not finished. Two hundred and about as many again could go in that space in the ordinary way, and the page had been lying in a room over a byre for nineteen years waiting, and the man who kept it had not once, in nineteen years, written the day at the head of that page in his own hand, which is the thing you do when you are about to open a spread, and there is a rule about it, and he knew the rule better than anybody alive.

**And the man who kept it came up the outside stair at about the fourth hour with two pails on the yoke and did not make a noise about it, which was a thing he had clearly decided on in the yard.**

“You have been at it about an hour and a bit,” he said, coming into the room sideways because of the yoke.

“An hour and about ten minutes.”

“You will have found the marks.” He set the pails down. “Three of them.”

“There are three.”

“There are three and the first one is two hundred times in that spread and the other two are the whole of what this book knows about going anywhere, and you have worked out in an hour what took the man before me six years to say out loud, and I am not going to ask you what it is, and I would like it noticed that I did not ask you.”

He put his hand on the third column.

“Now show me what you cannot make out.”

It took them about a quarter of an hour and it needed both of them, because the thing about it is not a thing a man finds by looking hard. It is a thing a man finds by putting two things next to each other, and the man who kept the book had been putting those two things next to each other every winter for six years, and the stranger had them in nine minutes, and both of those are the same act done at two different rates.

The hand at the head of the ninth spread, in the heading, with the day in the first column and the hand in the third and nothing in the second.

And the two hundred hands in the third column, which are all one hand.

And the last entry in the name column of the fourth spread, which is the fifth name column of the fifth spread, whichever way a man counts them, and it is a name with a stroke under it, and beside the name there is a hand in the third column, and it is the same hand.

“There,” said Iven Sarr. “I have shown that to four people in six years and two of them were dead inside a year and one of them is the woman who read it over two nights and she is four valleys off and I have had a letter from her once a season for six years and we say the weather in it. She has never once asked me about the hand and I have never once been able to work out whether that is because she does not believe me or because she has thought about it more than I have and has decided it is a thing you do not ask about. Those are the two possibilities and I have had six years to tell them apart and I have not.”

“No.”

“You are the first man in six years to say a word to me about it and the word is *no* and you said it in about a second, and I want you to understand that I am not going to be insulted by that, and I am not going to be comforted by it either, and I have not decided which of the two I would rather have had.”

**And then the man who had kept the only roll in a valley for nineteen years asked the man off the road a question, and it was the first question in six weeks that anybody in this valley had asked him that he could not answer.**

“A man in a county four hundred miles off,” said Iven Sarr, “has come four hundred miles to read a book, and I have not asked him once what he is, and I am not going to, and you have not told me and I have not asked and that is the arrangement and it is the right one and it is going to stay the right one as long as you are in this valley.

“And you have been in this valley six weeks and you have asked me two questions, and one of them was about a stone and one of them was about what I do all day, and both of them were decent questions, and I want to say something to you and I have no way to say it in the words anybody in this valley uses, so I am going to use the words a county uses, and if they come out wrong then they come out wrong and I am too old to be embarrassed about a sentence.

“I have wanted that book read for six years. That is finished. It is not finished, it has changed, and it changed in the fourth hour of a morning four days ago when I told you the fifth thing and you did not write it down. What I want now is a thing I cannot put in a sentence and I have been looking for it for nineteen years and I have not found it, and you are the only man I have ever met who has spent his life inside the exact thing I am asking about.

“I want to know whether a person can want a thing and not have a page for it. Not a man. A person. Anybody. In any place. Can a person want a thing badly enough to be the reason sixteen men put up a wall and not be able to have it written down anywhere, and still have it be real, and still be a thing that happened.

“Nobody in this valley has worked that out in nine generations. I have not worked it out and I am fifty-eight and I keep the only book in it. And you have not worked it out either, and I can tell, because you have a leg and a stick and you have been in my room for six weeks and you have not once looked at the notebook in your bag as though it were a place you kept things.”

“Can a person want a thing and not have a page for it,” Aren Kest said.

“That is what I asked.”

“I do not know,” said Aren Kest.

He said it in a second and a half, and he heard himself say it, and it was the first true thing he had said in a room in six weeks, and the man of fifty-eight put his hand flat on the shut cover of the only book in the valley and looked at the stranger of thirty-two over the top of it, and neither of them said anything else for a while.
