# Chapter 0161

The outside stair of the room over the byre at the Low Holm has nine treads and the fourth one down from the top was put in by a different man from the other eight about eighty years ago, and it is the only tread on the stair that talks under a boot, and anybody who has been up that stair twice knows which one it is and goes over it.

Aren Kest went up it at about the fifth hour of the tenth week of a cold in its ninth generation, and the fourth tread said so, and the door was open and the fire was in and Iven Sarr was standing up with his hand flat on the shut book on the trestles, and he had not been standing up when the stair creaked.

“You have a question,” he said.

“I have a question that is not about the book.”

“Then I will stand here and be told what it is and I will not answer it until I have put my hand on the book again, because if I answer a question with this shut in front of me it is a different thing than if I answer it with it open.”

**And the question was about a stone, and it had been in his head for forty days, and it was the reason he had not come up the stair twice.**

“The lintel over your door,” he said. “There is nothing on it.”

“My wife's grandmother cut it.”

“There is nothing on it. Not a mark and not a letter and not a hole worn almost away. In the county I came from there is one dressed stone in four miles and it has three things cut into it and one of them is a date.”

“Then that is a county that has had something to say on a stone.” Sarr turned the book over and put his palm flat on the cover and it was the sound of a man settling. “Here there has not been. There is a book over this door and it has four columns and not one of them is for saying things out loud, and every one of us knows every one of the others, and a mark on a stone in this valley would not tell anybody anything they did not already know standing next to it.

“That is the whole of the lintel, and it has taken you forty days, and I would have given it to you the first morning if you had asked me. You have a stone you have looked at about forty times and there is a woman in this yard who watched you do it on the first morning and has said one thing to me about it since and she is not a woman who says things often.

“What she said was, *he is going to ask somebody one day and it is not going to be me,* and I told her it would be you, and she said it would not be me in about the voice she uses for a thing she has settled, and she was right about herself and right about you and she has not asked you a question in forty days either. It is the same trick. You are both doing it on purpose now and neither of you would call it that.”

He looked at the shut book for a while.

“Ask me the hard one.”

“All right.”

“Before you do. A thing about a lintel is a thing you can carry down the stair and put down, and a thing about that page is not, and if you go down that stair in ten minutes with a thing about a page in your head you will carry it about this valley for a year and say nothing, and in about four years one of us is going to be dead and the other is going to have a page with nothing on it, and that is the ordinary way this valley loses everything. It has lost nine generations of a good deal in under nine generations and nobody can tell me one thing that was lost. It was just not written down and it was not asked about, and those are the same mistake and they are the only one anybody in this valley has ever made twice.”

“All right,” Aren Kest said. “Ask me the hard one.”

---

**And the fifth thing is that the ninth spread was opened and dated and had a hand in it before there was anything at all to put in it, and he has been alone with that for six years, and nobody in this valley has ever asked him about it, and he has been waiting nineteen years for somebody who had asked.**

“Not a name first,” he said. “You will want a name in the next ten minutes and I am telling you now that you are not going to get one, so that you will not spend the ten minutes looking for it and I will not spend the ten minutes stopping you.

“What I have kept is this. I have kept this book nineteen years. My father kept it before me and his father kept it before him and there has been a man in this valley keeping it for longer than there has been a book, because the first spread is not the beginning of anything, and I will not read you the line at the head of the first spread and we are not going near it this morning.

“Nine spreads, one to a generation, and the ninth spread is the ninth generation and it is the one I have had the most of. Two hundred lines, near enough, entered at the birth of a person, the day and the mark and the hand, and a column further out on the older spreads with a name in it, and that is the only place in nine spreads that a name has ever been and there is no name in this valley in this book now and there has not been one for three generations.

“And I have written the day against every line of it and the mark against every line of it, and I have not written a hand against one line of it, and I did not write those two hundred lines, and my father did not write them, and there was nobody in this valley to write them.

“At the head of the ninth spread there is a line.

“Not at the foot. Not in the middle. At the head, above the first birth on the page, and it is ruled the same as the rest of the page and it has a day in the day column and a hand in the hand column and nothing at all in the mark column, and it is the only line in nine spreads that has a day and a hand and no mark, and I have looked at it every winter for six years and I cannot tell you what it is and I cannot tell you who wrote it and I can tell you the day on it, and the day on it is four days before the day on the first birth on the page.

“A keeper opens a spread when there is a first thing to put in it. That is the whole of the rule and it is not written anywhere and I have done it four times and my father did it twice and there is no reason on earth to open a page and rule it and put a day in it and no mark, four days before there is anything in the book at all, and I have checked it every winter for six years, and I have run out of things to check it against.”

He put his hand flat on the book and left it there.

“That is the fifth thing. It is the last thing I have got. I have not told it to my wife and I have not told it to the man of thirty-four who wants a road and has never asked me a question in his life, and there are four other people alive in this valley who would understand exactly what I have just said and I have not told any of them, and the reason is not that I do not trust them.”

“What is the reason.”

“The reason is that a man who has had a thing like that for six years and has not told anybody begins, after about four years, to be able to hear himself wanting to tell somebody, and he cannot tell the difference any more between wanting to tell somebody and needing to, and the day he cannot tell the difference is the day he tells a man off the road in a room over a byre in the ninth week of a cold, and that is what has happened, and you asked me a question this morning that was not about the book and that is the whole of what I asked for.”

The room had one window and it faced the slope and the light in it was the flat grey that comes off snow that is not white, and it does not get dark at any hour anybody keeps, because there is no hour here that anybody keeps.

“A man came through with a cart in the third year of my keeping,” Sarr said, without any change of voice at all. “He was the first reading. He wanted a man in it and I gave him four spreads and the fire and a seat by the fire, and he read the first four generations in an evening and got the fifth one wrong in a way that has been in a copy ever since, and I have never seen that copy and he is dead. And you are about to read one spread, and you are going to do it better than he did, because you have looked at a floor for four seconds from a doorway, and I want you to understand tonight that a thing read once by a man who came through is not a reading. It is a man coming through.”

“You have never told me that.”

“I have never told anybody anything, which is a habit and not a religion, and it is the only one of my habits that I am proud of, and I have been proud of it in four households and in one room over a byre, and I have just broken it in one morning, and you will want to know what the charge was, and the charge was a stone.”

**And the thing that made it a question and not a favour is that he had said something in a letter four months ago that was true and was the worst sentence anybody has ever written about that book.**

“You wrote to a filing office,” he said, “and you said, *that is my own name, and it is in the book in my own hand.*”

“Yes.”

“That is true and it is the reason there is not one name of mine in nine spreads, and I have never seen anybody get that right before and I have never seen anybody get it wrong so well. My name is in this book the way a hand is in this book. There is a mark in the mark column that is mine, and a day in the day column that is mine, and there is no name of mine anywhere in it, and the reason is that I found that out before you were born and I did not find it out from a wise man. I found it out from my own grandmother, who kept this book for eleven years and would not tell me a single name out of it when I was twenty-three and asked her for one, and I went at her for a year about it, and in the twelfth year she was dead and I had it, and I was fifty-eight years old before I understood that she had not been withholding anything from me. She had been withholding it from the book. She had put a hundred and forty years of people in nine spreads and not one of them can be produced, and the only reason is that nobody who has ever kept this book is in it, not one, and I am not in it, and I will not be.”

**And then he made the terms, and there were three of them, and he made them the way a man makes terms he has had ready.**

“You may read the ninth spread. In this room, on those trestles, and I will be down at the byre and I will not come up, and the door will be shut and it is not locked because there is nothing in this valley worth locking and a man who locks a book is telling you what is in it.

“You will not read a name out loud. Not one, not to yourself, not to the fire, not under your breath at the ninth hour of a night when you cannot sleep on that leg. There is no name in that spread to read, and you will find that out inside five minutes and it will not be what you expected, and the thing you are about to do instead is read the marks, and a mark is a person, and if you say one of them out loud in this room then you have started something that neither of us can put back.

“And there is no page. Not a leaf, not a corner, not the back of the last leaf, and if you put a mark on anything in this room to remember it by then you have made a second copy of this book, and a second copy of this book is the thing this book has been doing for nine generations without ever being asked to do it.

“There is already one of those and I have never seen it. The man with the cart made a fair copy of four spreads and took it away with him and it is in a county four hundred miles off, and it has been in a copy ever since, and what is in the copy is the fifth generation wrong, and the wrongness has been the truth of that generation for about sixteen years to anybody who has read it, and there is no correcting of it because a corrected copy is a second copy.

“And the second reading was a woman, and she read it over two nights, and on the second morning she came down off the stair and told me she had been wrong about the first, and then she read me about four hundred words of what she thought was in it that I had never once asked her to look for, and two thirds of it was in the book and one third of it was not, and she could not tell me which was which and neither can I and neither can he.” He nodded at the door. “That is the sum of two readings and nineteen years and it is the reason I have wanted a third for six years, and you are the third, and you have had it for an hour and a bit and you have not said one thing about it yet, and I am not going to ask you.”

“I am not going to write it down,” Aren Kest said.

“I know that. You have been in this valley forty days and you have not written one name down and I have watched you not do it, and there is a man in the room over the byre who was told the same thing by a woman with a yoke about a page of her own.” He took his hand off the book. “Go down and work. Do not come up this stair again today.”

The woman with the yoke was in the yard with it on when he came down, and she did not ask him anything, and he went out to the bank and got the length off the rail, and then it was light enough to see the two rooms of the Low Holm from the wall, and then it was not.

**And he read the ninth spread for an hour and about ten minutes, and he did not read one name out loud, and he wrote nothing, and at the end of it he put his hand flat on the page the way the man who kept it had put his hand flat on it every morning for nineteen years, and he understood that he had already done that in about four days.**

The fire had gone down twice while he was at it. His hands would not keep. He had to take his gloves off to turn the leaves and after about nine minutes he had to stop and put his hands flat over the fire for two of them and start again, and he did that three times, and on the second time he noticed that he had already done it twice.

At the top of the spread there were three ruled columns and about a hundred and ninety ruled lines, and the day column ran down the page and never went back up, and every day on the page was later than the day above it, and a man could read the order of a whole valley off that page without reading a single thing on it that was a person.

And at the head of the spread, above the first birth, there was a line with a day in it, and a hand in it, and nothing at all in the mark column.

He put his hand flat on the page.

He had been in this room four days and he had already done that, and the man who kept the book had been doing it for nineteen years, and neither of them had ever once been asked what the hand was.
