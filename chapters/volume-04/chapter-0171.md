# Chapter 0171

The fourth tread said so under both of them going up and it said so under the man of fifty-eight going down four hours later, which is a thing about a stair nobody ever thinks about, and the door at the bottom is not a door anybody opens and it is not locked either, and there is nothing in this valley worth locking, and a man who locks a book is telling you what is in it.

He took the lamp because the room has one window about the size of his hand and it faces the slope and there has been no sun on that side since before his father was born, and the fourth step is the bottom one and the sill is stone and the walls are stone and the stones in them are not squared and there is more of a joint in a course than there is of a stone in places.

“Stay on the step,” he said. “That is not being difficult. You cannot get your head above the lintel and if you come down two more steps your leg will be under you and I cannot get you up it.”

“You are fifty-eight years old.”

“I have got a yoke up two pails of water every morning for nineteen years and I can get up this stair carrying one, and I have never once had to carry two, and I am not going to start on a man with a stick at the bottom of a hole in my own byre.” He went down the last two steps sideways with the lamp held out in front of him at about the height of his own knee, which is how a man carries a lamp in a room he is in more often than anybody in the valley knows and has not been in for six years.

**The room is about four feet by six. That figure has been in four books in this project and it is the size of a man lying down with his boots on.**

The floor is boards, and the boards run the other way from every other floor in the valley, and they are on a bed of lime about a finger thick, and somebody laid that bed, and the boards are older than the lime because the boards are what is left of a floor and the lime is what was put under it.

The sheet of paper is on the floor in the middle with a stone on it, and the stone is a piece of the old coping off that byre, which is a hundred and forty years old and squared on one face by a man of fifty-eight, and the square is about four inches across and it is a square somebody did for a purpose, and Iven Sarr put it there, and it is not the same stone as the one on the wall.

He took the lamp down to the sheet in his hand and put the stone in the crook of his arm and stood in the middle of four feet by six with a sheet of paper in his hand about a foot and a half long, and then he did not read it.

“You have been holding that about a minute,” said Aren Kest.

“I have been standing in my own back room holding a piece of paper for about a minute, and I have not read a line of it, and I want you to understand the size of that before I read it.” He turned round to face the step. “I did not sleep. I went up the outside stair at the sixth hour and I put my hand flat on the book and I came down here at the seventh and I stood in that doorway with a lamp, and I looked at the shape of it, and I told you in a cold room this morning what I knew, and I knew it in about four seconds because a man looks at a form for four seconds in the dark and takes away the shape of it.

“Now I have it in my hand in a lit room, and I am fifty-eight, and there is a thing in this valley where a man stands in a doorway and does not go four feet, and the reason is never a good one and I have not got one, and I am going to read it out loud because I cannot read it in my head in four feet by six and hear myself doing it.”

“I am not going to ask you a word about this room.”

“I know that, and I know what it is worth, and it is worth exactly nothing to me this morning, because the last thing I want in the world is a question in it. I want a voice.”

**And he read it out in a room four feet by six in about four minutes, and the man off the road stood on the top step with his hands on the lintel and did not come down, and not one word of it was written down anywhere by anybody, and that is the arrangement it was read under and it is not going to be improved on.**

The head of it is printed. Not written. Printed, in a small even type with the letters all the same height off one another, which nobody in this valley has a thing to compare it to.

*Office of Quarters, at Grayford.*

*Return of the quarter. To be sent round once in every seven years, and returned to the same, and not to be sent on.*

And then eight numbers and eight headings, ruled, in the same even print.

*1. The quarter. 2. The book of the quarter, and the name of the keeper of it. 3. The number of men going out of the quarter. 4. The number of those that have gone out and not come back. 5. The name of the person appointed in the quarter to answer for the men that go out. 6. The year. 7. Withdrawn. 8. Answer received.*

He stopped there and put the lamp on the sill against the stone, which took a hand's width of it, and the light came off the paper at an angle and he bent over it and read the middle of the sheet.

“There is writing in it,” he said. “There are three hands on this sheet and I have had it in a lit room for a minute and a half and that is what is in it.”

“Printed, and two hands.”

“Three. The print is not a hand, that is your word and it is a good one and I will use it. The print, and a hand that is not mine and not my father's, and a hand that is not that one.” He turned the sheet so the light would come across it. “The first hand is a slow careful hand. It has written one thing and it has not written it neatly, and the letters are all the same size, which is a thing I have never once seen a person do.”

“Everybody's letters are the same size on the same line.”

“Not on every line. You have written nineteen years of days against two hundred lines in that room over the byre and your ninth line is smaller than your fourth and your fiftieth is worse than either, because you are a man and it is four hours past the fourth hour on the twentieth day of a cold.” He tapped the sheet with a fingernail. “This one is the same on the first line and the last and it does not hurry anywhere, and the down strokes are the same depth the whole length of it, and I could not tell you to save my life which of the two men is better with a pen. That is not a hand that a person had. That is a hand that an office had, and there is a difference and I have never had cause to know it until this minute.”

“Read me what the first one says.”

He read it out, and he read it as a man reads a figure off a board.

*Of the upper water, being a valley of about two hundred people, and there is no bylaw in it, and no market officer, and no writ has ever come up the water.*

“There,” he said. “That is two hundred years old and it is a description of this valley by a man who had never seen it, and everything in it is true, including the last four words, and I would like somebody in this valley to tell me what the last four words are for.”

“They say that nobody has ever sent anybody here.”

“They say that nobody has ever sent anybody here. And that is on a printed form that was made to be sent to a place, and it was not sent to a place. It came up the water, or it came over the pass, or somebody carried it, and it came to a valley with about two hundred people in it and no bylaw and no writ, and it came to a man who kept a book of days and marks and hands, and the man put it under a stone on the floor of a room he had not been in for six years.” He put the lamp back in his hand. “And that is all of it. There are five more lines on this sheet and they are all short, and I am going to read them and then I am going to sit down on this step, because I am fifty-eight and I have been standing for a long time.”

“Read them.”

**And there is one more thing about the third column that a man who counts things for four counties cannot leave where it is, and it is on the page because he said it in a room four feet by six with a lamp on a sill, and it took him about nine seconds and he has not thought about it since.**

“Four in this year,” he said. “And there is no year in this valley. There has not been a year in this valley since before the first of the nine, and there is no authority anywhere in nine miles that could tell you how long one is, and I have asked four people since the ninth week and I have had four answers and not one of them is the same. So a form two hundred years old has put a figure against *this year* in a place where a year is not a thing, which means the office that printed it sent it out on a count and took the answers back to the same desk, and the desk has a rule drawn across the head of a space for the day somebody stops asking.

“And this valley has been given a figure of itself, four men going out of it, in a hand that is not mine, on a sheet I have had in my hand for about an hour, and it is wrong, and it is the only one anybody has ever given this valley by anybody from anywhere, and I have not got anywhere to put it, and neither has it.”

*3. The number of men going out of the quarter. Four in this year.*

*5. The name of the person appointed in the quarter to answer for the men that go out. None appointed.*

*6. The year. A hundred and eleven of the reckoning of this office, which is not the reckoning of this valley and is not the reckoning of any person in it.*

**And then there are two columns on that sheet that a man who has kept a book for nineteen years looks at for a long time and says nothing about, and they are the two that are empty, and he named them in the order they come.**

“Number seven. Withdrawn. It is a ruled space and there is a rule drawn across the head of it, and a man does not rule a line across a space on a form unless he is going to write one word in it. That line is there to be ruled across and it has not been ruled across, and I would like to know how old a paper has to get before the line stops looking like something anybody is ever going to do.”

“And number eight.”

“Number eight is *answer received*, and it is a ruled space, and there is nothing in it, and the space is about two inches deep and the rule at the bottom of it is not faded any more than the rule at the bottom of number three is faded.” He folded his hand flat on his knee. “I have been looking at a piece of paper for about nine minutes and I have got two empty columns out of it and I am a man who has spent his life putting a day against a line and I know exactly what an empty ruled space is. It is not a space waiting to be filled. It is a space that has been filled with nothing, and it has been filled with nothing for a very long time, and there is nothing about this sheet that says anybody ever looked at it again.”

He sat down on the bottom step, which took him some time to do, and the lamp between his boots, and the sheet on his knee with the printed head of it bent back out of the light.

“Right,” he said. “That is eight columns and I have read you six of them and the two I have not said anything about are the two that are the end of it, and I have not said anything about them on purpose and you did not ask me about them, and I would like that noticed as well.”

“No,” said Aren Kest. “It is noticed.”

“There is a foot to it,” said Iven Sarr, “and I have not come to it, and you have not asked me for it, and I am going to read it to you now because I have read you the rest of it in a room where there is no page and if I do not do it now I shall not do it at all, and I have been not doing things for nineteen years and I have got to be better at this before I am sixty.”

**And the foot of it is printed in the same small even type as the head of it and it says that the figure required is the figure of men and not the figure of names, and a name entered against the return is not returned to the sender and is kept by the office, and the man of fifty-eight read it out in about half a minute and the man off the road heard about eleven words of it and then stopped hearing it, and he did not say anything for a moment, and then he said one true thing and it was not the whole truth.**

“I have read that wording before,” he said.

Sarr did not move.

“Where,” he said.

“In a county four hundred miles off. On a printed form. Printed, in a small type, in the same place.” He put his hand flat on the door frame of the room, which is not a thing a man does for the sake of his hand. “It is not in a bag with me and it is not in a room and I cannot lay it on the floor in front of you and say here it is, and I have not got it, and that is not a trick and it is not me being careful. There is no page in this room and there is not going to be one, and a thing I say down four steps in a byre in a valley that is not on any map is a thing that about four people will remember for about nine years and that nobody can ever hold against anybody else.”

“So you have read it before and you are not going to say where, in a room with a lamp in it, to a man who has just read you eight columns of a form.”

“I have read it before and I am telling you that I have read it before, and I am not describing it, and I have not said a word about the second half of that sentence and you did not ask me for it, and I would like that noticed too,” said Aren Kest.

And Iven Sarr looked at him for a while in a room with one window about the size of a man's hand, and then he put the sheet down flat on the board floor, square to the boards, and put the squared stone on it, and it went in a foot and a half about where it had been, because the boards run the other way and his hand knew the boards better than the lamp did.

“They will think in that market that I have got a piece of paper,” he said. “There is not one piece of paper in this valley with a day on it that is not a birth, and there are about four hundred people in this valley who believe that, and in about four days there will be four people who have been told a man of fifty-eight read a piece of paper out in a room, and in about nine years there will be a woman in that market who will remember it and will not be able to tell anybody what was on it.”

“That is a thing about a valley and not about the paper.”

“It is a thing about a valley and not about the paper, and the paper is the only part of the two of them that anybody in the world can check.”

He shut the low door, and it shut on the frame without a sound because it has shut on that frame about nine times in a hundred and forty years, and then he was on the fourth tread with the lamp and Aren Kest came up after him, and the fourth tread said so under both of them.

At the top of the stair there was a woman in the yard with a yoke on her shoulders who had a question in her face and did not ask it, and had not asked it for eleven days, and had decided about four days ago not to ask it. And the woman over the byre had left her own door open behind them and the trough was standing where the trough has stood for sixty years, and the wall was outside in the dark, four hundred feet of the best wall anybody in that valley had ever seen, holding up a byre instead of being the first four hundred yards of a road, and it had gone through a hard week and a soft one and it was still up, and nobody in that yard had any idea it would not be for about eleven weeks, and neither of the two men said anything about it on the way up the outside stair, and the man of fifty-eight went up first and the man off the road went up second and the fourth tread said so under both of them.
