# Chapter 0176

The fourth day of the cold is the day they go over the pass, and the fourth day of the cold is the only date in this valley that anybody can name without being told, and on the fourth day of this cold about eleven people went up the bank in a line with what they could carry, and it takes a week to the top of the pass and then they are gone, and about four of them come back in the first warm week.

They took the beasts out on the sixth day and the byres of nine households have been standing empty since, and empty byres in a cold are not a tragedy. They are a hole in a roof, a floor that has not been walked on, a door that a family shuts behind them and does not open until there is somebody in the yard, and nine roofs in nine steadings up this bank have had a hole in them since the summer and are not going to be mended before the water comes.

That is the work, and there is nobody in this valley to do it, and there are thirty men under the cross.

Wystan Roole brought sixteen of them up the bank on the ninth day at about the fourth hour and they stood nine feet off the back of the Low Holm in a line in the cold and looked at nine roofs, and not one of them said anything for about four minutes, and the reason they did not say anything is that they had all worked it out in nine seconds and none of them wanted to be the first to say it out loud in front of a stranger.

**And then the man of thirty-four said it out loud in front of a stranger, because he has said things out loud in front of strangers once in nine years and it has cost him nothing, and there is no more of that in him.**

“There is a roof that wants mending and I cannot mend a roof,” he said. “I have got sixteen men who can put up a wall in eleven days and there is not one of them who has ever put a rafter on, because there has not been a roof in this valley in nine generations that anybody has ever paid anybody for, and I have been to four houses in nine years and asked at three of them, and I am not going to ask at the fourth because I know.”

“You built a wall,” said Aren Kest.

“I built a wall at a byre whose owner had not asked for it and had told me to go away, and the wall is up and it is the best wall anybody in this valley has seen and the floor of that building is still a dinner plate, and the two of those are in the world at the same time and neither of the two people involved is the one who gives.” He put his hands in a belt that was not a belt. “I have not said that to you before and I am not going to say it again, and there is a reason I want to say it here and not in a council, and the reason is that I have been refused twice for a road and I have understood the refusal this week and I understood it in a yard at the Low Holm while a man of thirty-two was looking at a footing, and I am going to tell you what I understood and then I am going to stop.”

“Go on.”

“There is no road in this valley because there is nobody in it that needs to be anywhere. Not that there is no road to build. There is no road because about four hundred people have never once in nine generations wanted to be somewhere, and they have wanted it so little that nine generations of men who could dig have been able to say *there is no road to build* and be believed, and I believed it myself for six years.” He wiped his hands on a rope. “I have spent six years thinking that a council had decided not to build a road. It has not decided anything. It has never been asked by anybody in a position to be told the truth, because the only people in a position to be told the truth are the four hundred people, and about eleven of them went over a pass in the cold with what they could carry, and the other three hundred and eighty-nine are here and know exactly what they are doing, which is not going anywhere, and they have known it for nine generations.”

Nobody on that bank said that a man of thirty-four had just said the truest thing anybody had said in nine years, and he did not wait for anybody to, because a man who waits to be praised on a bank has already lost nine years.

---

**And then a man of the ninth generation of about twenty-two came down the bank with a barrow and stopped at the bottom of it and asked a question, and he had a hurdle over his shoulder, and the question was a rafter, and it is the fourth time in nine years that the ninth generation has asked an older hand in this valley a question about the trade.**

The question was whether the rafter goes on the plate or on the wall head, and the man who answered it was the mill man, who is about sixty-one and cannot leave the wheel, and who had come up because somebody had told him there were men on a bank.

“On the plate,” said the mill man. “And not the wall head, and I will tell you why, and it is because the wall head is the one that is already moving, and by the time you have loaded a roof onto a wall head you have loaded it onto the thing that is going to let go. You take the weight off the wall and put it on the plate and the plate is on the beam and the beam is on the wall, and now there is one step between the roof and the ground instead of two, and the one step is a timber and you can see the timber.”

“That is not right,” said a man of about fifty, from the second of the sixteen. “That is what a man says who has built on a soft ground. On a wall head the load goes down and the wall head is where the wall is thickest, and a plate needs a plate to sit on and if you have not got a plate you have not got a plate.”

“I have got a plate.”

“You have got a plate off a mill house two steadings down that came off in the summer and has been in a barn since, and the barn is dry and the plate is oak and the plate is a foot wide and the plate is not going to be under the end of a roof at the wrong end.”

Then the two of them argued about it for about eleven minutes in the cold, and about four of the ninth generation stood about and listened, and did not interrupt, and did not have anything to say, and one of the twenty-four-year-old put his hand on the plate and said *that is the one* and was wrong, and the man of fifty told him why he was wrong in about nine words, and the twenty-four-year-old went and got another one.

That is all nine generations of a craft is when nobody will let it do the thing it was learned for, and it took a roof nobody had asked for and a man of sixty-one who cannot leave his wheel, and the ninth generation listened to two men argue about a plate in a cold bank and about eleven of them will be at the top of a pass by the fourth day of the next cold.

**And the roof is not the job, and Wystan Roole worked that out in about a quarter of an hour, and what he said about it is the truest thing anybody had said in that yard in nine years, and he said it standing at the foot of a bank with sixteen men behind him and a stranger in front of him, and he said it once.**

“There is no roof in this valley in nine generations because a roof is for a family and there is not one family in this valley that has asked us for anything since before I was born, and I am not going to say that they are wrong to not ask, because they are not going to be here in nine months and I am, and that is the difference between us entire and it is not a thing anybody can argue about on a bank.”

“That is a hard sentence and it is your own and I have been here thirteen weeks and I have not heard it before,” said Aren Kest.

“You have not heard it before because I have never said it before and I have not had anybody to say it to who was going to write it down, and I want to be careful here, because I watched you not write it down four times this week and I have not decided whether I mind that.” He turned round to the sixteen. “We are not doing the nine roofs.”

“Wystan.”

“I am not doing the nine roofs and I am telling you why on the bank and not in a room, because in a room this valley will have four accounts of it by the morning. A roof is for a family. There is one family in this valley that has asked us for a thing in nine years and she has a hole in her floor the size of a dinner plate and she has moved a pail four inches twice and has never thanked anybody and has never refused anybody, and she is the eighth generation, and her byre has a bank behind it and a drain that has been coming out under the wall of a different building for sixty years, and the best wall anybody in this valley has seen is holding up her byre, and the floor of it is still a dinner plate, and both of those are in the world at the same time this morning and neither of the two of us is the one who gives.”

He pointed at the ground on the far side of the wall.

“There is a beck comes out under that wall and it has come out under a wall for sixty years, and it goes four feet before it drops into the bottom of a cutting, and a woman with a yoke has been standing in the wet place at the low end of that wall to put a pail in the beck since before I was twenty. I have watched her do it four times this winter and I have not said anything to her about it because she has not asked me for anything and I do not get to turn up with a wall and then a second thing.”

“So what is the second thing.”

“There is no stand across that beck anywhere,” he said. “There is no stand in this valley. There has not been a stand in this valley in nine generations, and I have had sixteen men and about thirty under the cross for nine years and not one single one of them has ever put a span across anything, because a span is for traffic and there has never been any traffic in this valley, and the only traffic there has ever been is a woman with a yoke and a pail and about four feet of water.”

Nobody on that bank had heard the word before. It is not a rare word and it is not a trade word. It is the word this valley has for a bridge and it is in forty mouths in nine miles and there is not one book in the valley that has it in it, and a man of thirty-four used it nine seconds before he noticed he had used it, and did not take it back, and a man of thirty-two with a limp and a stick he had brought four hundred miles for four hundred yards of road heard it for the first time in this valley and wrote nothing down, which is the fifth time in thirteen weeks and which he would have counted himself if anybody had asked him to.

**And the stand went up in four days over about four feet of a beck at the bottom of a cutting, with a stone abutment on each side and two oak sills and two bays, and the stone came off the field wall at the top of the bank that the wall at the Low Holm had already had, and the lime was lime putty out of what was left of the three loads, and there is no quicklime left in this valley and none coming, and a stand laid in lime putty in the second month of a cold is a stand that will want looking at in the warm week and will get it.**

Four days. Sixteen men. Two bays over four feet, which is a short span, and the shortest thing Wystan Roole has ever built, and he said so on the second morning in a voice nine other people could hear, and nobody disagreed with him and nobody said anything about it either.

The ninth generation asked about the sills for two days. That is all the ninth generation did on that job. They asked about the sills, and about the batter on the abutment, and about whether a sill wants a sill wall under it or a footing in front of it, and about the difference between a stand that is built to take a load all day and a stand that is built to take a barrow across it four times a day, and they were answered every time, and nine of the sixteen of them had a question ready before they got to the bank.

And on the fourth day, at about the fifth hour, the water came up behind the abutment for the first time all winter, and the ninth generation of them carried the sills' spoil out on a slate each, and a man of thirty-four stood with his hand on the head of the new stone and did not say anything for two minutes, and then he put his hand on the old wall above it, which is four feet away and nine weeks old and holds up a byre, and said, to a stranger and not to the sixteen:

“Three weeks ago I could put my hand on that and know it. I have not got anything like that for a stone I built nine weeks ago. There is a wall and there is a stand and I can tell you the difference between them from nine feet off and I could not tell you what it is.”

“Which is what you are frightened of.”

“No,” said Wystan Roole. “Which is what I have got. I have had it since the second winter and I have not said a word about it to anybody, and I have been perfectly comfortable not saying a word about it.”

**And on the same afternoon, at about the sixth hour, the man who makes nails came up the bank with a barrow and stood at the low end of the cutting for about four minutes and did not go over the stand, and then he went over it, and then he came back over it, and then he stood on the bank above it again, and a man of thirty-four watched all of this and said nothing about it until the man was forty feet away and had not looked back.**

“He is the first customer this valley has ever had for a nail,” said Wystan Roole, “and he has been a nail man for nine years and he has never had one, and it is a stand over four feet of a beck in a cutting at the bottom of a bank, and there is no road in this valley and no cart in this market and about nine people in four hundred who would come a hundred yards out of their way to stand on a bridge, and he has just come ninety feet.

“I am not going to tell him what that means and I am not going to charge him for the nail and if he tries to pay me I am going to tell him that a stand in a valley with no road is not a thing you can put a price on, and I have had nine years of a craft and four walls and not one of them has ever had anything on it, and this one has got a nail in it. That is all the ninth generation of a trade is for, and nobody in this valley has ever had it, and he will be at that stand four times a day for the rest of the winter and he will chalk it on the back of a stall in a market, because that is the only place he has ever kept a figure, and in nine years there will be a nail count on a board in this valley and a nail count is a figure about a thing that people are using to go over a bridge, and that has never happened here, and I have no further thought on it and I have nine weeks before the warm week and I intend to spend them building a second one at the ford.”

He put his hand down off the stone and went round the outside of the abutment to the far side, where the beck is four feet deep in the cutting and there is no way to get at the stone at all, and stood there on the bank above it in the flat grey light that does not get dark at any hour anybody keeps in this valley, and looked at the third stone from the bottom of the far face of it, for as long as it takes a man of thirty-four to decide that he is not going to get a bar and a ladder and a boy to hold them in a freezing cutting at the fifth hour of the fourteenth week of a cold.

Behind him the ninth generation went over the new stand one at a time with barrows of timber on a slate each, the way you cross anything anybody has built for you in a valley where nothing has been built for you, and nobody looked at the stonework while they were on it, and the eleventh went over and came back and went over again with an empty barrow, and a man of thirty-four stood on the far bank of a cutting with his hands in a rope-belt and looked at a stone, and nine people in a valley of four hundred were in a place they had not been before the ninth of this cold, and two hundred were in a book, and a girl of about nine with a barrow she had not gone and got out at the ford was watching all of it from the top of the bank with her hands in her sleeves, and she was counting, and she was not going to ask anybody what the number was for.
