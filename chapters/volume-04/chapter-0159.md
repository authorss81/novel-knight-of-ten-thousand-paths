# Chapter 0159

Iven Sarr is fifty-eight and he keeps the winter book of a valley four hundred miles from any county that is on a map, and he has kept it nineteen years, and it was his father's before him and his father's father before that, and the room he keeps it in is the room over the byre at the Low Holm and the woman who carries the water up the stair has told Aren Kest to ask for it, and he asked for it on the fourth week of a winter in its ninth generation and got it.

He is a big man with a stoop in it that came early, and he has a way of putting his hand flat on a book before he says anything about it, and he did that when Aren Kest came up the outside stair, and he did it again when the door was shut.

“Sit down,” he said. “That stool is the one people sit on. That is not a thing I say to be civil, it is a thing I say because in about a minute you are going to ask me what the book is and I want you sitting down when you do it.”

That is the first time anybody in this valley has been ready for him and it took him nineteen years and two other people’s mistakes to learn.

He knew about the letter. Of course he knew about the letter.

“It went to a filing office with no name on it, and the filing office has a door on a lane and a man on the post at his own cost, and it took about a month to come back into a county, and a man in that county has put it into a county council’s minute and the minute says that nothing was done with it.” He said it without any weight at all. “I have had two readings in six years and I did not expect a third and I have stopped wanting one about every fortnight, and I am telling you that so that you know what kind of man you are sitting across from, because I would rather be known for that than be helped with it.”

---

**And the book is on a board on trestles under the window and it is nine generations deep and it is the only roll in this valley, and the woman who carries the water up that stair cannot tell you one name in it.**

It is about two feet by three feet and it is a single thickness, and it is bound in a hide strip at the left-hand edge that has been replaced twice, and it is not a book anybody in this valley has ever had copied, and there is not a second copy of it anywhere and there is no other valley within a season’s travel that has one.

“Nine generations,” said Iven Sarr. “That is the whole of the book. Nine spreads, and the first of them is not the beginning of anything, it is the first page of the first man who ever wrote in it, and he is not in it, and that is the first thing about it that you will not believe.

“Nobody who has ever kept this book is in this book. Not one. Not my father, not his, not me. And I know every one of their names and none of them is in here, and that is deliberate, and the man who did it first wrote one line at the head of the first spread about it, and I will not read you that line either.”

“Why not the line.”

“Because I will not read you a name and a line is where names go, and that is the answer to both of your questions and I have given it once.” He took his hand off the book. “If I read a name tonight, that name is in your head in the morning, and you are a man who went out of a county to be on a bridge, and there is a bench in that county with a minute in it, and I have read about that minute from four hundred miles off and I have read a good deal of the handwriting in this valley that people thought nobody outside the valley had ever seen. I am not being careful for the book. I have never been careful for the book. I have been careful for about two hundred people and I have never worked out how to do it in a way that also lets anybody read anything.”

He turned the book round so the top spread faced the stranger and put his finger on the left-hand edge.

“That is nine generations. The columns are the same all the way down and there are four of them and none of them is an office. The day. The mark. The hand. And in the older spreads there is a fifth column on the right with a name in it, and in the spreads from the seventh generation down there is not, and the fifth column did not stop because anybody stopped writing it. It stopped because by then nobody had gone out of this valley for long enough for a name to be a fact about where they were.”

Aren Kest did not ask the next question, which was the obvious one, and Sarr waited for it anyway, and then went on, and the going on was the answer.

“The fourth spread is the sixth generation. Two hundred and eleven names in the fifth column, and I have never counted them and I am not going to, and the woman who keeps the slate counts births, not that.

“Ninth spread. Ninth generation. And it is not two hundred and eleven lines. It is about two hundred lines, and they begin at the top of a spread and they go down in one run of the page, and every one of them has a day against it and a mark against it, and a day against a line of that kind is the day the person was born.”

“That is all of it?”

“That is all of it and that is the whole of the ninth generation and I have kept it for six of those years and I did not write any of it,” said Iven Sarr. “And I will tell you the thing that you came here to be told and then I will stop, because I have said that there is a thing I am going to stop at and I have never yet not stopped where I said I would.

“The people in the fourth spread are on that spread because they went. The people in the ninth spread are on that spread because they were born. Those are the two reasons and there has never been a third reason in nine spreads and I have looked for a third reason every winter for nineteen years. A man of my age in a valley this size runs out of new things to want by about the sixth year. I have wanted one thing for six years and I know exactly what it is and it is not in the book.”

---

**And what Iven Sarr wants is not a reading. He said so, in his own words, in about a minute, and he said it badly, and he said why he said it badly.**

“I want the original oath found,” he said, “and I want it read in the room it was made in, and I want the ninth generation told what it signed.

“Not the book. The oath. The book is nineteen years of copying a thing somebody else wrote down and the copying is how I know what the thing was. I want the first page, if there is a first page, and I want it in the room over the cross in the cold, and I want the people in that room to be the people whose grandfathers were in it, and I want it read out loud by a man of about my age to a room of about the size it was, and I want the whole of it read out and not the parts that have held.

“Nineteen years. And nobody in this valley has ever asked me what it is for, and I have never been able to make anybody ask me, and I have not told my want to a stranger in nineteen years, and I am telling it to you because you have been in this valley a month and you have not asked me one question about it and that is the only reason I am telling you. If you had asked me in the first hour I would have told you nothing and gone on not asking you, and that is what has happened four times.”

He put his hand flat on the book again.

“And I will say the other half of it and then I will not say any more.

“A valley that has kept a thing for nine generations and has never once been asked whether it is keeping it or agreeing to it is not a valley with a tradition. It is a valley with a habit. **And a habit is a thing a place does to itself when nobody is looking.** And I have never once said that sentence to anybody and I have never once heard it, and I wrote it on the back of a leaf in the fifth year of keeping it and I have not looked at the leaf in nine years, and I am telling you a sentence I found in my own handwriting, which is a thing you should not do and which I have done because you are a stranger and there is nobody else in this valley to say it to.”

---

**And the sixteen men in the room at the market cross had not had work for a long time, and nobody in the room had said so, and a man of about thirty-four was the one who said it, and nobody used his name.**

He did not introduce himself. He said, “You will be the man off the road.”

“Yes.”

“Then you are the only man in this valley who has seen a bridge that was built by somebody who had to come back over it, and I have sixteen men in this room and thirty-one under the cross, and not one of them has had a job to do in this valley since before I was twenty, and I have asked for one twice and been told twice that there is no road to build.”

“Where is the road.”

“That is the whole of it and that is why I have not asked you before now,” he said. “We are in a valley with a river and eleven steadings and a market town and there is not one yard of made road in it, and no one will build one, and when I say no one I mean the council and I mean it in the ordinary way and I am not making an accusation, because a council that has kept a winter for nine generations is not a council that owes anybody a road.”

He took his hand off the wall and put it flat on the wall instead, which is the thing a man does with his hands when he wants them to be somewhere that is not in his pocket.

“You can see that floor is laid wrong,” he said. “The joists are the wrong way round for the span and somebody has been in and had a go at it and made it worse and did it about four years ago, and there is a man in this room who did that and he will tell you so if you ask him and I would rather you did not ask him in front of me.”

Aren Kest looked at the floor of the room over the cross for about four seconds. He could see three things and one of them was the joists and one of them was a footing on the near wall that was not on the wall, and the third was a lead strip that had been put in upside down, and there are not many men in this kingdom who can see the third of those from a doorway in four seconds, and there are about nine in this county and none of them in this room.

“All three,” he said.

The man looked at him for a moment.

“Right,” he said. “And that is the whole of the first day, and there will not be a second day about it, and you are welcome to the room and there is tea in the pot and I am not going to ask you to do anything and I would like it understood that I have not asked.”

---

**And he did a job on the fourth day, and it took four hours, and it was not a bridge, and nobody in the valley thanked him for it, and he has written down that nobody thanked him for it and that he would have been surprised if they had.**

The woman at the Low Holm had a gate frame out of true. The frame is oak and it came out of the ground in the spring thaw four years ago and it was put back in a hurry by four men who had about nine other things to do, and it is out of plumb by about an inch and a half at the top and it shuts badly and it has been shutting badly for four years and everybody in that yard has been shutting it badly.

He asked her for a mallet and a wedge and a piece of shoddy oak and got all three, and he did not ask for the frame to be taken off because a frame taken off in a valley in the ninth week of the winter is a gate that is not there, and a gate that is not there in a winter is a different thing from a gate that shuts badly.

So he packed the loose joint with a wedge behind the head and drew it up in three places and then hung the gate on it again and shut it and shut it again, and it shut.

Four hours. He could not stand on the cold side of it for more than about nine minutes at a time, and he sat on an upturned pail for the rest of it, and he used the stick as a gauge for the plumb because a stick is a straight thing and he had nothing else straight, and the woman of the Low Holm watched him for about two minutes and then went to the byre and left him alone, which is a thing that people do in this valley with a man working and it took him a month to understand that it was not politeness.

“It shuts,” she said, when she came back.

“It shuts.”

“You have been on that gate two seasons and you did it in four hours with a stick.”

“Yes,” he said, and then, because he had been in the valley a month and had not asked anybody anything, he asked one.

“What is a gate for?”

“Everything and nothing,” she said. “You do not close it for the winter, there is nothing to come in, and you do not open it for anything, and if it is open a dog goes out and if it is shut a dog stays in, and that is the whole of a gate, and it is the same in every valley that has ever had one, and I do not know why you asked.”

“Because there is not one yard of made road in this valley,” he said, “and you have a gate that a man has to shut with his hands every day of a winter, and I do not know how those two facts go together.”

She looked at him for a while.

“They do not go together,” she said. “That is what I have been trying to say to you since you came and I have not found the words for it and I have a gate to shut. I will tell you the one thing I know. There are eleven steadings in this valley and eleven gates, and in four years I have not seen a cart come through one of them that was not going to a mill, and I would like you to be able to say what the rest of the gate is for, because I have never been able to and I am the one who shuts it.”

She shut it, and it shut.

---

**And a young man of the ninth generation stopped in a doorway at about the fourth hour of the same day, and nobody noticed, and Aren Kest noticed.**

He was about twenty-two and he had a hurdle over his shoulder and he was coming up the outside stair of the room over the byre when two women on the landing said something to each other about him, and one of them said the word.

Not at him. About the hurdle, or about the byre, or about the fact that he was going to be gone in a week; it was said in the middle of an argument about a gate and the word was in the middle of it, and the second woman said something back that was not a question.

He stopped in the doorway with the hurdle on his shoulder and he stayed there for about as long as a person stays when they have heard their own name used in a room for the second time, and then he came in and put the hurdle down and said, “Is there tea,” and somebody said there was tea, and he sat down.

It took about two seconds and nobody in the room turned round, and the two women on the landing went on with the argument about the gate, and the room was warm, and a man of fifty-eight had a book on a board on trestles with his hand flat on it.

Aren Kest was standing nine feet away with a cup he had not drunk from. The word is not a long word and he has never heard it before and he did not ask, and there were about nine people in the room and every one of them had heard it a thousand times, and a word that everybody in a place uses is not a word a man in a room is expected to stop and ask about, and he did not ask.

The young man drank his tea and took his hurdle and went, and the two women on the landing finished the argument about the gate, and the man of fifty-eight turned a page of the book over and looked at it and did not read it.
