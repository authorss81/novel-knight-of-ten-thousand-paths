# Chapter 0181

**The mark is in the coping of the far abutment of the stand at the Low Holm and it has been there since the ninth week of the third cold, and it is a line and a bar and a figure at the height of a thumb on the face that gets the weather, and about four hundred people know it is there, and not one of them has done anything about it, and on the ninth day of this cold a man of thirty-four is going to stand at the front of a room over a cross and tell a room full of strangers what he did and what he did not do.**

The woman of the Low Holm found it. She goes over that stand four times a day with a yoke on her shoulders and a pail in one hand, and on the second day of this cold she put the pail down on the coping instead of on the flat stone at the end of it, because the coping is where you put a pail, and her hand went down on something that was not stone.

She did not tell anybody for a day and a half.

That is on the page because it is true and because everything that happened afterwards is about what a valley does with a thing it has found and cannot account for, and it took a woman with a yoke and a pail a day and a half, and she is of the eighth generation and has never had a book in her hands in her life and has said in a room in the second month of this cold that she does not thank people and does not refuse people, and the day and a half was neither of those things and she has not said what it was.

And then the man of thirty-four went down and looked at it, and went back up the bank, and slept, and nobody in this valley worked out what he was doing for the four days in between except himself, and he has not said.

“A mark,” he said, at the ninth hour on the ninth day, in the room over the cross, standing at the front of it and not in the aisle.

There were about eleven people in the room. The stove was in at the front and the twenty-nine benches were in four rows with a number cut into the end of each one and a hundred and forty cut into the end of the one at the back of the right-hand row, and the number had not moved in the whole time the man of fifty-eight can remember and nobody had ever been struck off one of them, and the aisle is four feet wide and runs from the door to the front and nobody has stood in it for three generations.

“Right,” said Wystan Roole. “I am going to say this once and I am going to say it in the same words to everybody in this valley over the next four days, and I am going to say it in front of the man who cut it, because eleven people have asked me since the second day of this cold why I have not said anything, and the answer is that I laid that coping, and I have not been able to say out loud in a room that I laid a coping with a stranger's mark in it and did not know it was there until the second day of this cold, and I can say it now, and I am saying it now, and I would like it noticed that it took four days to say and not four years.

“I cut that coping. Nine stones, and the fourth one from the far end is a different stone to the other eight because it is a stone off the byre coping at the third generation's house and it is a hundred and forty years old and it is squared on one face, and the eight round it are field stone off a wall that was coming down anyway. I laid the course in about half an hour on the second afternoon of a job four days long, in lime putty, with a hammer and a cold chisel off a bucket, and the mark is four inches in from the end of the fourth stone and about a thumb off the top of the course, and I know exactly where it is because I have put my hand on it every day since the second day of this cold.

“And I did not cut it. And I have laid about four hundred copings since I came here and I have not put a mark in one of them, and I have never in my life wanted to.”

Nobody said anything. The stove ticked.

“Now I am going to say why it has to come off, and I have thought about it for four days and I have got it in nine sentences, and I want it said in this room in front of the eleven people in it before a single man puts a bar on a stone, because I have been in this valley nine years and I have learned the one thing about it that I would not have learned anywhere else, and the thing is that if a thing is done in this valley without the reason being said out loud in a room first, then in nine years there will be four accounts of it and none of them will be checkable against the other three.

“Here is the reason. **A stand is not made by a man. A stand is made by two ends and a day and somebody who was not either of the two ends.** You do not get a stand by carrying stone to a place. You get it by having a place you start and a place you finish, and a day you did it on, and a person who is not you standing at the finish who saw it happen and can say it happened. Take those three away and you have not got a stand. You have got a pile of stone in a cutting, and I have built four of those in this valley and I know what they are worth, which is nothing, because a pile of stone in a cutting does not know whose it is.

“That mark is a line and a bar and a figure. I cannot read the figure. I have had my hand on it every day since the second morning of this cold and I do not know what it says and I am not going to guess, and the figure is the part that matters, because a figure in a mark means somebody was keeping a day, and a day in it means somebody meant it to be findable, and there is not one person in the nine miles of this valley who could tell you what day it is. And I laid that coping and I am not a witness, because a witness is a person who is not the one keeping the thing and I have been the one keeping the thing since the fourteenth week of the second cold.

“And then there is the other end, and this is the half I have not said out loud in front of a room, and I have never seen a road and I do not know what one of them is made of and I am telling you that before I use the word.

**“A line like that does not finish at the coping. It finishes somewhere else, and that is the point of a line, and something at the other end of it has agreed to it. Not you. Not the man who cut it and not me. Somebody. Who has been asked. And I have been to the two ends of this valley and there is nobody at the other end of anything, and there is no road out of here and there has not been one in nine generations, and whoever this mark belongs to is about four hundred miles off, and nobody out there has been asked for anything by anybody in this valley, and nobody out there knows this valley is on a map.**

“So the half of it that is not there is not missing. It is absent, and it is absent on purpose, and there is no way anybody in this room can supply it, and I am not going to pretend that is anybody's fault. **It is a mark on a stone. That is all of it. It is not wrong and it is not broken and it has not come to any harm, because there was never anything at the other end of it for it to do.** I want that said in this room, because in about four years somebody in this valley is going to tell a child that a mark came out of a stone on a bridge and meant something, and a thing that means something is a thing somebody can be shown, and the child will be the one shown it.”

Nobody in the room moved. The woman with the slate had the slate out and had not written anything on it, which is a thing she has not done before in a room in eleven years.

“Which brings me to the part that is about this valley and not about him,” said Wystan Roole, “and it took me two of those four days and it is the part I am actually asking for.

“Look at where it is. The coping of the far abutment of the only stand in this valley. It is the highest thing in nine miles that a person stands on. That is where the barrow rests when the barrow is loaded. That is where a pail rests. That is where a girl of nine sits with her feet in the beck and does not know that she is sitting on the only thing anybody in the length of this valley has ever cut a mark into.

“And any of them can put a hand on it. Not four men. Any of them. A child can put a hand on it and an old man can put a hand on it and a woman with a yoke puts a hand on it four times a day. That is the difference between a mark on a private thing and a mark on a public thing, and it is why I want it off, and it is not a trade reason. It is this: a mark on a public thing is not a mark any more, it is a handle, and before this cold is out there will be a story on that coping, and the story will have started in the place where the mark is, and there will be no way anybody in this valley can ever get back to the ninth day of this cold and find out whose it was.”

He put his hand in a belt that was not a belt and took it out again.

“Four days.”

“Four days is not enough,” said the woman of sixty-six at four looms, from the fourth row, and she is the old woman with the looms by her own arrangement and she has been at that trade for fifty-one years and she had said nothing for about four minutes.

“No it is not,” said Wystan Roole. “Four days is one more than I am owed and about forty fewer than I would like, and I have taken four because I could not get anybody to come to this room at the eighth hour and I could not get anybody to come to it at all in a cold week without four days, and because I would rather it were done in a hurry and properly than slowly and properly. And I would like the four days to be about a thing anybody can watch. I want the work on the fourth day at the fourth hour in daylight with this coping in the open and anybody in this valley who wants to stand and watch.

“And I want the whole course off. Not the mark. The course, all nine stones, and a new one on, out of our own stone, laid by the sixteen, and not one of the old ones broken and not thrown in the beck and not carried off to be forgotten in a corner. I will say where they go when I know where they go, and I have not known until this morning, and I do not know yet.”

A man of about forty-one at the back, who farms three steadings and is of the ninth generation and had not asked to come and came, asked the only question anybody in that room asked.

“If you take the stone off, is it still there.”

“Yes,” said Wystan Roole. “In the ground under four feet of water at the ford, in the order it comes off, for about four months until the water comes up over it in the warm week, and after that for as long as that footing is standing, and I have not decided yet where the footing goes when the ford moves, and the ford moves about a foot a year, and I have known that since I was twenty-five and never once said it out loud in a room.”

**“You could chip it out,” said the man of forty-one. “It is a chisel mark. I have watched a man take a name off a beam with a chisel and a hammer and it takes about half an hour and it leaves a place that is not stone any more and everybody can see the place.”**

“I am not going to chip it out,” said Wystan Roole, “and if I did, then before this cold is out there would be a coping with a smooth place in it and a smooth place is a question, and I am not going to put a question in a public stone in a valley with no writ and no bell and no office. We are taking the course down and putting a course up. That is the whole of what I am asking and I am asking it in front of the eleven people in this room and I have not asked the man who cut it and I am going to ask him now.”

---

**And every head in that room went round to the back of the right-hand row, where a man of thirty-two with a limp had a stick standing against the end of a bench, and the stick had been carried four hundred miles and had not been put down since a day this county does not use.**

“Did you do it for a reason that I would understand.”

“Yes,” said Aren Kest.

“Not what the reason is,” said Wystan Roole. “Whether there is one. I have been in this valley nine years and I have wanted a road twice and been refused twice and I have worked out in one week that no council ever decided not to build one, because nobody in this valley has ever wanted to be anywhere. **I do not need to know what a mark in a coping is for. I need to know there is one.** And there is one, and that is the end of it, and I am going to work on a coping on the fourth day at the fourth hour whether you tell me or not.”

He looked at the eleven people in the room and then at the aisle.

“I would like it noticed that this body is not a bench,” he said, “and that it has known that longer than any man in it has been alive, and that there is nobody in this room who is going to be asked anything, and that the reason I can stand at the front of this room and say nine sentences about a coping is that nobody in it can make me answer them, and that I have wanted to be asked one question for nine years and I have just been asked one and I am not going to be the one who starts it.”

And the man of thirty-two said the thing that his fourth cold in that valley turns on, and it is a decision, and he made it in about four seconds, and he did not argue anything, because there was nothing to argue, because the man who had made the argument was a man of thirty-four and had not once said a thing that was not true.

“I have said that sentence in four counties,” he said, “with a form in my hand and a room full of people in front of me who wanted me to be right about it, and I have never once had to take the stone out. That is not because the four counties are wiser than this one. It is because in the four counties the stone is four hundred miles off and I am not standing on it and none of them has a yoke and a pail and has never been asked anything in forty years.

“You are all standing on it. Take it down.”

He put his hand out flat on the end of the bench with the hundred and forty cut into it, and it was the first time in about five months that he had put a hand on anything in that room.

---

**And then the notice went out of the valley on the morning of the tenth day, and a notice in a valley with no writ and no bell and no office is a man who walks, and Wystan Roole did not walk it alone.**

He took sixteen men out at the fourth hour on the tenth day and gave each of them the same eleven words and told them they were not to say more than the eleven words and were not to answer a question and were not to be asked anything, and about nine hundred doors in the length of the valley took the eleven words in eleven days because four hundred of them are people and the other five hundred are gates.

The eleven words were: **A course of stone is coming off the coping at the Low Holm on the fourth day at the fourth hour. It is the course. Anyone may watch. Nobody is asked anything.**

Nobody was asked anything. That is the arrangement and it held for the eleven days, and about four hundred people heard eleven words at a gate and not one of them put a question back, and the man of thirty-four walked the last stretch himself and told them afterward that in nine years of asking this valley for things he had never once put eleven words at nine hundred gates in a valley where nobody had ever put anything at a gate.

And then the coping stood at the bottom of a cutting for three more days with a stranger's mark in it and the weather on it, in front of a valley of about four hundred people who now knew the hour it was coming off and had put it in about four hundred mouths and in no book, and the ninth day of the fourth cold of a cold in its ninth generation is the day a place decided that a mark a stranger cut in a public stone was not a thing it was ever going to have to be able to explain, and about four hundred people went on using that coping for three more days and put a pail on it and a barrow on it and a hand on the mark, and not one of them said anything about it to anybody, and there is a girl of nine who crossed it four times a day and counted every stone in the course and has not been asked what she counted and was not going to be.
