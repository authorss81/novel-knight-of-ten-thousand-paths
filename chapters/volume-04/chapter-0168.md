# Chapter 0168

The hole in the wall came on the tenth day and the man who cut it was the man of thirty-four and he would not let anybody else touch it, and the woman of the Low Holm stood in the wet and watched the whole of it and did not go inside.

He cut it wrong first. That was the point of it and he had said so on the bank a week before, and he cut a hole in the wrong place in the second course at the low end, about two feet from where it should have been, and the two feet was not a mistake anybody could have made by accident. It was a hole he cut to show the wall what a hole was going to look like, and it took him about nine minutes, and then he knocked it out and packed the face back over it and dressed it, and it is in that wall and you cannot see it and there are four men alive who know.

Then he cut the real one.

A hole in a wall with water behind it is not a hole. It is a lintel and two jambs and a haunch on each side, and the whole weight of the wall has to go round it, and if you cut it small the wall settles onto the lintel and cracks above it in about six years, and if you cut it in a cold week with quicklime in the wall you have four months of the wall wanting to move and no way to stop it.

He cut it in the third course. He dressed the jambs himself, one at a time, with a point and a lump hammer, and he took about four hours over the two of them, and the stone for the lintel was a piece of the old coping off the byre, which was a hundred and forty years old and squared, and it went in about four inches wider than the hole on each side, which is the part that carries it.

Nobody helped him and nobody spoke to him for about four hours except to bring him something warm, and the man of fifty-eight brought him a cup of something at about the second hour without being asked, and stood there while he drank it, and did not stay.

**And when it was done he stood back and explained it, out loud, to about eleven people, and he did not explain it to the stranger, and he was asked by the stranger afterwards and answered him, and the explanation was one sentence and it is the reason the volume does not make him wrong to do anything else he is going to do.**

“A wall with water behind it has to have a way out, and if you do not give it one then you have not built a wall, you have built a place for the water to go when it decides to, and it will decide to, and it will decide to at night in the thaw with about eleven cows in the building.” He wiped his hands. “I knew about the drain in the middle of the week and I asked her for a wall before I had worked out what I was going to do about the drain, and that was wrong, and it is the only wrong thing anybody has done on this wall and it is mine.”

“You have put a hole in the best wall in this valley in the bottom of it.”

“I have put a hole in the best wall in this valley in the bottom of it and put a lintel over it that is stronger than the wall on either side of it, and the byre behind it is not going in the night in the thaw, and that is the whole trade in one afternoon.” He put the hammer down. “And the byre floor is still a dinner plate and I am not going to be the man who mends a floor as well. That is a different man. That is a man who lays forty sacks of stone in lime and does nothing else for eleven days, and I have laid it four times in forty years and I would not want it done by me.”

The woman of the Low Holm went in, and came out with a pail, and set the pail down where it had been before anybody had moved it.

**And there is one thing about a wall like that which is not known until the spring, and the spring in this valley is about eleven weeks off, and not one person in that yard was going to be told anything about it in the meantime.**

Nobody can test a retaining wall in a cold week. That is not a saying, it is the shape of the job: a retaining wall is tested by water coming against it in a thaw, and a thaw in this valley is about eleven weeks away, and in eleven weeks everything above the notch will be under a foot of moving water and a bank of thawing ground, and either the wall is right or it is not, and there is nothing anybody can do in a cold week to find out which.

“The whole of the next eleven weeks is a wait,” said Wystan Roole, on the tenth day, with the bar over his shoulder. “And I have built four of these and I have seen one of them go, and the one that went went in the thaw, at night, in about two hours, and I was not in the county for it and I got a letter about it nine days later.”

“So you do not know.”

“I know what I know and I know the notch is right and the lintel is right and the batter is right and the coping is squared by a man of fifty-eight in the dark. That is the whole of what there is.” He looked down the wall. “And in eleven weeks there will be a foot of water against the bottom of it and forty tons of bank behind the top of it, and about a hundred and forty sacks of barley in the building, and either this is a wall or it is a very good nine days.”

“You will be here in the thaw.”

“You will not be here in the thaw,” said Wystan Roole.

Nobody said anything about that, and he did not explain it, and it turned out afterwards that he had been right and that nobody in that yard had ever met anybody who came back in the spring.

**And a girl of about nine came round the wall on the eleventh day, because a wall in a yard is a thing you have to go round, and she went round it twice, and came back, and told a man of thirty-two how many through-stones were in it.**

“Nine,” she said. “And the second one is not like the others.”

“What is wrong with the second one.”

“It has a hole in the bottom of it,” said the girl, “and there is a stone over the hole and the stone is squared and the others are not, and I have been round it twice and I have counted the stones in the wall as well and there are four hundred and six of them and I do not know if that is a good wall or a bad wall and I am not going to ask anybody because then I will have to be told.”

“That is a good wall,” said Aren Kest.

“That is what the man with the bar said, and he said it before you did, and he said it about the fourth course and not about the hole.” She put her hands in her sleeves. “You are the man off the road. You have been here eight weeks and my gate has not had a cart at it once and I have said that to four people and none of them have been you, and I have not asked you anything because you have not asked me anything, and that is the arrangement and I would like it to go on for as long as you are here.”

“It will go on for as long as I am here.”

“Then I will be at the gate,” said the girl of about nine, and went to get her barrow out at the ford, and had a barrow to get out at the ford most days, and the man of thirty-two stood in a yard with a notebook in his bag and four hundred and six stones in a wall and knew that the day on which a child of nine was born was four lines up from the bottom of the ninth spread of the only roll in a valley of about four hundred people, and had told nobody, and was not going to, and there was nowhere in four hundred miles to tell anybody.

**And a man of thirty-two wrote in a notebook in a room over a byre on the tenth night of a wall that the first drift figure of this crossing could not be put on a page, and he wrote down why, and the why is not the same why he gave in the first week and he did not improve on the first one.**

He had been at it for two days. That is on the page because it is the reason the page is in that state.

He had a number and he had no frame, and the two of them had been sitting side by side since the fourth week of a cold in a valley four hundred miles from any county that is on a map, and in the first week he had tried to turn nine winters into years and had refused it in his own notebook and written *a guess is not a frame*, and that was the first refusal, and this one is not that one.

This is what he wrote, and he wrote it in the valley's own register because that was the only register he had, and the valley's own register is that you say how many weeks of the cold it is and that is the whole of what a person can say about when a thing happened:

*Out at the fourth hour on a day this county does not use, from a sill with no glass in it, on a line made up in nine minutes with the hour against each of the nine. The office's printed figure is three weeks. It has been three weeks in an office's hand for two years and the office cannot say whose figure it is and I cannot either.*

*The valley's count. I have been on the far side of a line since before the ninth week of this valley's cold and it is now the fourteenth week of it. By their count a week is whatever a person standing up in a cold room says a week is, and there is no authority in this valley I could ask what a week is, and I am not going to guess one a second time.*

*My own count was three weeks and two sleeps at the fourth week and I stopped counting at the ninth, because a count of sleeps with nothing to hold it in is not a figure, and I have not started again, and the number of my sleeps and the number of their weeks have never agreed and I have not tried to make them agree and that is the third time in this case that two people in two countries have counted the same thing in two ways.*

*And now the part I have to put down, and I have got it wrong for two days and I have got it right tonight and here it is.*

*The error is not the difference between three weeks and the time I have been here.*

*I have had that sum in my head since the first week and it is the wrong sum and I have known it was wrong since about the fourth hour yesterday, and the reason it is wrong is that the time I have been here is not the absence. The absence is a number in a country that keeps its days, and it is not mine, and it is not going to be mine until I am standing on the other side of a line again. If I do the sum here then I get a figure, and the figure will be a number with no meaning in it, and a number with no meaning in it is worse than nothing at all, because it looks like a figure and I will believe it in about four months and put it in a return.*

*So the sum for this crossing is three weeks, printed, unchanged, in a man's hand in an office two hundred miles off, and a valley that keeps no months, and a man with no frame. And I am not going to write a figure, and this is the third time in this case that I have got to this page and not written one. The first time I threw the paper away and the second time I left the two numbers side by side and did not add them. This time I have written down why, and that is the difference, and I do not know yet whether it is a better thing or just a slower way of failing.*

*There is one figure that is mine and it is four lines long and it is in this book already. Went out at the fourth hour, and a line made up in nine minutes, and no name in it anywhere.*

*That is the whole of the record of it that belongs to me.*

He put the pencil down about nine inches above the page and came back to it a quarter of an hour later and read the whole of it and did not write anything else on it, which is the first time in this case that he has left a page alone after writing on it.

There is one more thing about that page and it is not on it, because he did not write it and is not going to.

Nobody in this valley knows that there is a figure at all. Not the keeper of the only book, not the man with sixteen men, not the woman of sixty-six, not the man of eighty-one, not the girl at the gate. They know that a man came up the road out of a county that keeps months and that he counts things, and the woman of about sixty-six has watched him shut a gate for eight weeks, and there is a line in him like a bar. What none of them has is a reason to suppose that there is a piece of paper about four hundred miles behind him with a number in an office's hand on it, and no idea at all what the number is for, and that is the only thing about this crossing that is actually his.

In about four hundred miles there is a woman of twenty-nine in a room over a weigh-house with a day-book she cannot be served with, and she has a figure of the length of an absence and no figure to put it against, and she said once, to a man with a limp, in a gateway, that that was the only sentence anybody had said to her about it in four months, and he did not improve on it by being right about it twice.

He does not know whether he is going to be able to give her one. That is the first thing in this case that he has not been able to work out on the far side of a line, and he has not written it down, and there is a rule about the number of things in his coat.

**And the man who kept the book came up the outside stair at about the sixth hour with no yoke, which is the third time he has come up that stair without one, and asked whether he could read a page, and was told that he could, and read it standing, and put it back down on the trestle without a word.**

“I have kept this book nineteen years,” said Iven Sarr. “I have four other books in the room under the boards. I have a day in every one of them for every birth in this valley for a hundred and forty years, and I have never once been able to turn one of those days into a figure, and I could not have told you why until about four minutes ago, and the reason is that a day in this book is the day a person was born and a birth is not a unit.”

“That is not a figure either.”

“It is not. It is a day and it is a thing that happened and it is not a length of anything.” He put his hand flat on the shut cover, which is what he does, and which the man off the road had been doing for about six weeks without knowing that there was a man in this valley who did it twice a day. “I have wanted that book read for six years and I have just found out in about four minutes that the man who wanted it read has been keeping a book for nineteen years in which there is no number, and the two of those facts have been in the same room the entire time and neither of us could have said it.”

**And he put the book flat on the trestles and squared it with both hands, and the woman of the Low Holm put her hand flat on the coping of a wall about nine feet long, in the way that everybody in that valley puts a hand on a thing, and neither of them noticed that the other had done it, and the man of thirty-four was at the other end of the wall with a bar in his hands not looking at either of them, and it was the fourteenth week of a cold in its ninth generation and the wall was holding up a byre and the notch was in the wind out of the slack.**
