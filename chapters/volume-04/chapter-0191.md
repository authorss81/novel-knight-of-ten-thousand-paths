# Chapter 0191

**The fourth cold has run for ten weeks and the fourth of its weeks is the last of it, and on the ninth day of that week a man of thirty-four is going to put four days on the end of the back bench in the room over the cross in chalk, and about forty people are going to be at the ford at the fourth hour on the second morning of them, and one of the forty is a man of thirty-two with a limp who is not allowed to lay a stone in this valley.**

The chalk was the man of fifty-eight's idea and he gave it away in about four words and would not have it back. There is no chalk in this valley. There has been a piece of it in the room over the cross since before the second of the ninth, in a box on the shelf behind the fire that has been opened four times in a hundred and forty years, and the man of thirty-four asked for it and the man of fifty-eight said no, and then said yes, and then would not say why for three days.

“Why I would not have it back is not a thing I am going to say in a room with eleven people in it,” he said, on the sixth day, at the end of the fire. “Four days in a valley with no writ and no bell and no office is a thing that has to be written on a public thing or it is a rumour by the second morning. That is all. Write the four days on the end of that bench and every person in this valley can come and read them, and none of them has to be told anything by anybody, and if a thing is on the end of that bench then it is not being kept by the man who wrote it.”

Nobody in the room improved on it. Four people understood the rest of it and as many again did not, and the man of thirty-four said the six things at nine hundred gates in the second month of the second cold and had been told about that arrangement by a man of thirty-four and had not understood one word of it and had understood all of it.

---

**And on the ninth day of the fourth week of the fourth cold he said the reason out loud in a room with about eleven people in it, and it took about four minutes, and he said it before anything was done and not after.**

The stove was in at the front. The twenty-nine benches were in four rows with a number cut into the end of each one and a hundred and forty cut into the end of the one at the back of the right-hand row, and the aisle was four feet wide and empty all the way through, and it had been empty for three generations.

“I am going to say it once in here and I am going to say it again at the ford in the morning in front of forty people,” he said, “and the reason I am saying it twice is a reason a man of thirty-four learned from a man of fifty-eight in a cold room in the second month of this valley and did not understand at the time and has not stopped thinking about since. A reason given once in a closed room is an account. A reason given twice in the open is a reason.”

Nobody said anything, and nine of the eleven had understood most of it, and the man of thirty-four had worked out over the last four days that the reason they had understood it is that it is the only argument anybody in this valley has ever heard that was about their own hands. The stove ticked.

“There is a thing under this valley nine generations old and it is holding, and it is not a promise, and I am not going to say what it is made of or how it is done or who put it there, and the man of thirty-two who has the other end of it has not told me in six months and I have not asked him, and you are not going to get it out of either of us in this room this morning.

“**It is straining. A man of thirty-four who has looked at nine things on this bank every day since he was nine years old can feel it in a road and cannot name it, and he has had it since the first cold of the first winter and has told nobody, and he is telling me because I am a stranger and there is nobody else in this valley he can say it to. There is no instrument in this world for putting a load on it, and there is not one person alive who knows where to stand with a bar.**”

He put his hands in a belt that was not a belt and took them out again.

“And a man can take it out. He says it will take four days and about forty people, and I have believed him for a week and I have not slept, and I am not going to stand in this room and pretend that I have been asking him about it for a week. I asked him once and he gave me nine words and I have had nine words for six months and they have been enough to put forty people on a bank.”

“Then we are doing it,” said a man of about thirty-one of the ninth generation from the second row, and he was not going to be quiet about it, and four people in the room agreed with him out loud and as many again did not.

“You are not deciding anything,” said Wystan Roole. “You are nine people in a room and about two hundred people are in a book under a byre and not one of the nine of you is in that book as anything, and there is no box on this bench for the two hundred and there is not one way in this valley of putting them in a room and asking them. That was done. Nine hundred doors, eleven days, the second month of the second cold, five of us, the same six things in the same words, and nothing written down at any of them. **I am not going to do it again and I am not going to ask anybody anything this week, and the reason is not that the asking was wrong. The asking was right and it is the only thing anybody in this valley has ever done that was right, and I am not going to go back over it and put a second time on top of it.**”

---

**And then he said the part of it that was about the eleven, and he said it standing up, and the clerk of about sixty put his hand on the minute and wrote it out, and it went in a minute in a room over a mill that nobody has ever been served with and nobody is ever going to be.**

“Eleven of the ninth went over the pass on the fourth day of this cold. About four of them will come back in the first warm week. I have been in this valley nine years and I have never once known which four and I am not going to start.

“**The seven that went with the asking in the second month of the second cold are the only people in this world who could say no to the four days and they cannot be asked and have not been asked, and this is not going to be made unanimous by their absence, and I want the minute to say that it is not unanimous and that nobody is going to go and find out where they are.**”

A woman of about thirty took a slate out from round her neck and put it on the trestle at the front and said a figure out loud, and it was her figure and she had kept it eleven years, and a man of fifty-eight said that the ninth spread of the only roll in this valley has about two hundred of the ninth generation in it, and the two figures were not the same, and neither of them was struck out and neither of them was offered up to be corrected, and there is nowhere in nine miles that either of them can be put.

“Write the eleven down,” said Iven Sarr, from the third row. “I have got nine spreads and a day column and I have never once written a number of people that was not a count of people that went out or came back, and the eleven that went over the pass on the fourth day of this cold are the first number of people I have ever put in this book that I cannot finish.”

---

**And on the tenth day he brought the book down four steps and out of a room about four feet by six with a lamp in it, and about eleven people had been in that room in nine weeks and four of them had not known it existed a year ago, and he put it on the trestle at the front of the room over the cross with a piece of rag paper under it to save the boards.**

The last leaf of the ninth spread had the three figures in the day column, entered on the twenty-fourth day of the second cold, with a mark under each and a hand over the top, and the hand varies. *About four hundred. About a hundred and eighty-nine. Nine.*

And to the right of the day column, ruled in ink in a man of fifty-eight's own hand on the tenth day of the fourth week of the fourth cold, there was a second column the width of two fingers, and it was the first new column on that page in a hundred and forty years.

“There are eight names in it,” he said, “and they are in the book and they are not going to be read out in a room, and the reason they are not is not politeness. **A name in a book of this valley goes in because the person did it themselves and not because anybody asked him. That is the fourth line at the head of the first spread and it is a hundred and forty years old and I have read it about nine hundred times and never once knew what it was a rule about until a man of thirty-two stood on a step outside my byre and asked me what it was a rule about.**”

“Eight,” said the man of thirty-four.

“Eight names in eight hands, and the ninth line is empty, and I ruled it on the tenth day and it is the ninth line from the top and it is the same depth as the other eight and I am not going to rule it across.

“The figure of nine is yours. You made it on a step in the cold in about a minute with a rope-belt on and it is in the day column and it is what was said at nine hundred doors and it is what about four hundred people heard, and I am not going to strike it, because a figure that is in a book and is struck out is two figures and this valley has one.

“**And it is wrong.** The three households hold eight people. I have counted them twice this week and I have counted them with a woman of about thirty-five who has lived next door to two of them for thirty years, and it is eight, and the nine was made on a step in the cold by a man with a rope-belt on in about a minute.

“And I have not been able to account for the ninth. Not in a week. There is a person in one of those three houses who was not at a door and has not been asked anything and cannot be found, and I am not going to put a name against the ninth line, and I am not going to strike the nine, and I am going to write in the margin in my own hand that eight are named and one is not found, and if the man who wrote the nine is in this room he can come and look at the margin.”

The man of thirty-four looked at the margin for a minute and a half and did not say anything about it and has not said anything about it since.

---

**And on the second morning of the four days about forty people were at the ford at the fourth hour, and the light in this valley does not get dark at any hour anybody keeps, and the mill man of sixty-one came down with a barrow and could not leave his wheel and did not go back up it for two days.**

The face was on the low side of the ford, in the bank above the wet gravel, and there is about seven feet of good bank there and four feet of it that has never held anything anybody would build on, and the man of thirty-four had been looking at that bank twice a day for two years without touching it, and the reason he had not touched it is that there is no stone in this valley for a wall and a ford is a ford.

“Nobody lays a stone in this valley,” he said, to the back of the right-hand row, in a voice the whole bank could hear, about an hour after it had started. “You know that. I said it in front of forty people in the fourth week of the fourth cold with a bar in my hand and I have said it four times since and I am saying it now because there are forty people on this bank and eleven of them have never heard it.

“**You carry. That is all you have got. And you will do it about four hundred times today, and four hundred times tomorrow, and the same the two days after, and I am going to be standing next to you all of it, and I am not going to explain what for.**”

The man of thirty-two took a barrow and did not say anything and did the whole morning.

They took the face down by barrow-load and by bar and by hand at the bottom where the bar would not go. The ground was frozen a foot down and soft under it, and there was a seam of a different colour about a hand's width under the frozen foot, and eleven people in that valley had seen that colour once before in their lives and all eleven of them were of the eighth generation and had seen it in a bank that had come down in a wet week in the year before the first of the nine.

The barrow went up the bank to the four hundred yards of made ground and came back down, and the barrow went round the second stand at the ford because the stand was in and it went over it, and the woman of the Low Holm came down the bank in the middle of the morning with a yoke on her shoulders and a pail in each hand and four hundred yards of made ground under her feet, which is the only true line in nine miles, and she did not stop and nobody asked her to.

**And the ninth generation asked about the barrow run all morning, and none of it was about the four days, and that is the ninth time in nine generations that generation has asked an older hand in this valley a question about the trade and the first time anybody has been able to give them a different answer twice in one morning.**

They asked whether a face wants a step in it. They asked whether the barrow should go up the made ground or round the low end of it, and were told that it should go round the low end because made ground in a cold is a thing that has not been under anything yet, and as many again of them had been on the four hundred yards with barrows every day for nine weeks and knew that and had not heard anybody say it. They asked what a barrow-load is, and the man of thirty-four said it out loud, and a man of about twenty-six said it was not a fixed number, and the man of thirty-four said it was not, and said why, and eleven people heard the why.

“And the man who made nails has been on that run,” said a woman of about thirty-five from the top of the bank, “and he is one of the three, and he has been on it for the first time since the fourth week of the fourth cold, and he has not looked out past the top of the bank once this morning, and I have watched him for an hour and a half and I am telling you in front of forty people and I would like it noticed that I am saying it.”

“Put it in the minute,” said Wystan Roole.

“You are not a minute.”

“No,” he said, “and I would not have been one nine weeks ago either, and that is a different thing and I am not going to pretend to you that it is the same.”

And the man of thirty-four came down the face about two feet, on his hands, the way a man does when there is no ladder and no intention of there being one, and put his hand flat on the frozen foot where the colour changes, and stayed there with his hand on it for the length of a question asked from the top of the bank about whether a step was wanted in a face, and got his hand off it and came back up, and nobody asked him what was under the frozen foot and he did not say.

And at about the seventh hour they had four feet of face and the bottom of it was not gravel and was not stone and was not the frozen foot of a bank, and the man of thirty-four put his hand on it and took his hand off it and stood looking at the side of the face for a minute and a half with his hands behind his back.

“That is enough for today,” he said. “**Not because we have got to. Because in a cold in this light you cannot see the bottom of a four-foot face, and there are forty people on this bank, and I am not going to have the bottom of a hole in the dark with the ninth generation on it.**”

Nobody said anything about that either. Four of the nine hundred doors in this valley heard a man of thirty-four say something that was not about a trade in the seventh hour of the second day of the four days, and as many again told it, and the telling is the reason the words of the two of them are still in the mouths of four people in this valley in the warm week.
