# Chapter 0177

There is one thing a man who builds things knows that a man who reads things does not, and it is not a mystery and it is not a secret and it costs nine days to learn, and Wystan Roole put it on a bank at the Low Holm on the eighteenth day of a cold in its second winter, in a yard with a barrow standing in it the wrong way up and nobody in the yard to say so.

“A thing is not known to hold until it is put under a load,” he said. “That is the trade entire. I do not care what a thing is made of. I do not care how good the man who made it was or how old the stone is. I care about one thing and it is whether anybody has ever stood on it and watched it and come back afterwards.”

“There is one thing in this valley that has been under a load for nine generations,” said Aren Kest.

“There is about four hundred things in this valley that have been under a load for nine generations and not one of them has ever been tested, and you have just named the biggest one and you have got it right, and I have been sitting on the edge of that for two winters and I have not got round to saying it out loud because I have not known who to say it to and I have not wanted to say it to the sixteen.”

He put the rope down on the abutment and wiped his hands on it, which does not do anything to a rope.

“In the first winter I noticed it. It was not a thing about the ice and it was not a thing about the water. I go up the bank twice a day and I look at nine things and I have looked at them since I was nine years old, and in the first cold the ninth generation's grandmothers started saying a thing. There is no word in this valley for what they were saying either. The word they use is *the water has gone quiet*, and they say it about the Burn, and they say it in kitchens and at gates and over a stand in a cutting, and a man who has been here nine weeks hears it about nine times a season.

“And it is not a figure. There is no figure in it. You cannot go and put a stick in the Burn and find out what *quiet* is, and I have tried, and I did it in the ninth week of the first winter and got nothing, and I have got a man with a cart coming over the pass in the warm week and I am going to get him to look at it and I do not expect anything.”

“What is quiet about it.”

“The sound of it going over the stones.” He stopped there for a second and started again. “That is what *quiet* is and I have not said that to anybody and I said it to you in one breath. The Burn has gone over its stones for nine generations. A man who is eighty-one has heard it every day of his life and a man of nine can hear it from the top of this bank. And in the first cold it stopped, and it did not stop everywhere. It stopped below the ford and it did not stop above the ford, and it is still stopped below the ford and it is not stopped above the ford, and there are four hundred yards of this valley where a river does not make the noise it has made every day for longer than anybody has been keeping a book, and no person in this valley is frightened of it and nine people have said the word out loud and not one of them has asked another one what it means.”

“Nobody asks anybody anything,” said Aren Kest. “You have been here nine weeks and you know why better than I do.”

“I know it because the ninth generation will not be asked, and the eighth generation will not ask the ninth, and the seventh generation does not ask the eighth, and that is not a habit, that is nine generations of a valley finding out in about nine years each time that there is nowhere to put an answer.” He looked at the water behind the abutment. “And I know the reason I could not say it out loud for two winters, and I have had it in front of me since the ninth of this cold and I will say it now and then I am going to stop talking for a week.”

“Go on.”

“Everything in this valley is unproved.” He said it the way a man reads a figure off a board. “The wall I put up nine weeks ago stands in the wind out of the slack and it is the best wall anybody in this valley has seen and it has not had water against it for one hour in its life and there is a thaw about eleven weeks off and I will not be here for it. The stand across that beck has had a barrow on it four times. The lintel in the bottom of that wall is stronger than the wall on either side of it and it has not had a pail of water through it. The coping on that byre is a hundred and forty years old and it was done by a woman in lime and it has not been tested in a hundred and forty years.

“And every single one of those things is still standing. And they are all still standing for the same reason and the reason is that nobody in this valley has ever put a load on any of them and found out, and a thing that has never been tested is not a thing that has proved it holds. It is a thing that has never been asked.”

He sat down on the offcut of a byre roof with a hole in it, two steadings up, which is where the man of eighty-one sits, and which nobody offered him and which he has never asked for.

“That is the whole trade. That is what I have got instead of a road and I have had it since the first winter and I have not said it because I am thirty-four and I have sixteen men and no work and a valley that has never asked me a question, and the last man who asked me a question in this valley was a man of fifty-eight who wanted a book read and he is the only one, and he did not ask me about this.” He wiped his hands on the rope again. “I am not frightened of a thaw. I am frightened of a thing that has been asked nothing for nine generations, and there is one of those under this valley and it is not nine weeks old, and the ninth generation can feel it and cannot name it, and nine of their grandmothers have said *the water has gone quiet* and the young ones have not said anything at all.”

---

**And the man of thirty-two said the thing he had come four hundred miles for, and it was not said on a bank, and the reason it was not said on a bank is that a bank is where a man of this valley says things he has already settled, and this was not settled by anybody.**

He said it in the room over the cross at about the seventh hour on the nineteenth day, with the stove lit at the front and fourteen people in it, and the twenty-nine benches in four rows with a hundred and forty cut into the end of the one at the back of the right-hand row, and the aisle four feet wide, and nobody standing in the aisle at any point in any of it.

“You all know what the benches are for,” he said. “You have all been in this room. I am not going to explain them to a room I have been in once, and I am not going to say the word, and I am not going to ask anybody in it anything, and if anybody asks me a question I will answer it after and not during, and that is the arrangement I came into this valley on and it is going to be the arrangement until somebody makes a different one.”

Nobody said anything. The stove ticked.

“There is a thing under this valley that is nine generations old and it is holding, and it is not a promise, and I am not going to say anything else about it or what it is or what it is made of or how it is done, and I have been told in about four words by a man of fifty-eight that a promise cannot hold a thing for nine generations, and I have been told nothing else, and I am not going to manufacture the rest of it in a cold room with fourteen people in it.”

**Which was the first time all winter that anybody in that room heard their own valley's arrangement come out of a stranger's mouth, and four people in it understood exactly what he had just done, and nine of them did not.**

“Here is what I have to say and I am going to say it once and then I am going to go down the outside stair and stand in a yard for a bit.

“The thing under this valley is straining. That is not a figure and it is not a word I have invented. A man of thirty-four who has looked at nine things on this bank every day since he was nine years old has told me that he can feel it in a road, and he cannot name it, and he has had it since the first winter and has told nobody, and he is telling me because I am a stranger and there is nobody else in this valley he can say it to.

“It is going to go on straining. And the man of thirty-four has told me one more thing, and I am going to give it to you in his words because it is his and not mine. Nothing in this valley has ever been asked whether it would hold. And there is no way in the world to put a load on that. Not this winter. Not by any of us. There is no instrument for it and there is not a person alive who knows where to stand with a bar.

“And in about nine generations the next one after the ninth has to stand in the aisle of this room, in the cold, and be asked to keep it. And the ninth has not been asked and the tenth will not be asked and all of it is carried by about two hundred people who were entered in a book on the day they were born by nobody and who have never been asked a question about it in their lives, and eleven of them went over a pass four days into this cold and four of them are coming back.”

“That is a very great deal to say in a cold room on the nineteenth day of a cold,” said Iven Sarr, from the third row, “and you have said it standing at the front with a stick and I would like you to know that I have been in this room sixty times and that is the first time a person has stood at the front of it and told me something I did not know.”

“I have not told you anything you did not know. You have known all of it since the ninth week of the first cold and you have had four books in this room and a form under your own byre and you have not said one word about it to anybody, and I am not here to tell a man of fifty-eight what his own valley is.”

“No,” said Iven Sarr. “I am aware that I am not.”

“Ten days is everything I have got, and I am going to be outside for a bit, and there is a woman of thirty with a slate in this room and I am not going to be asked anything by her or anybody else, and I will answer it after.”

---

**And the man of thirty-four stood up in the middle of the four rows of benches at about the eighth hour and asked the only question in that room that was about the job, and he asked it in one breath and then sat down again, which is a thing a man of thirty-four does when he has said a thing he cannot take back.**

“How.”

“How am I going to get at about two hundred people in a valley with no road in it and a market that eleven people turn up to and a room that forty used to turn up to before there was anything to turn up for.”

“Walk,” said Aren Kest.

“Walk. Eleven steadings and the wide place and four hundred households and two hundred of ours, on foot, in a cold, in eleven days or about nine.” He did not get up again. “That is about nine hundred doors and it is about a hundred and forty miles of going up and down this valley with nothing to carry and nothing to sell, and I have asked twice in nine years for a road and I have been refused twice, and I would like it noticed that the first thing anybody in this valley has ever put me to is a job that is a road, and that it is not a road, and that nobody in the four hundred has to be in a room to be asked, and that I am going to have to stand on about nine hundred steps and say the same nine sentences nine hundred times and I have never in my life said the same thing nine times.”

“You have said the best wall in this valley once and said it was the last time,” said Aren Kest.

“I said that in a yard to nine people and I was not asked, and this is four men and a woman in a cold room and one of them has asked me, and that is the difference and it is the whole of it and I would like the record to show that a man who says no more in a yard is not a man who says no more in a room, and that about four people in this valley have never found out which one I am until this week.”

**And the sixteenth of them was the ninth generation, and there were nine of them in the room and two hundred of them in the valley, and one of the two hundred was a young man of twenty-two standing at the back of the right-hand row who had not come for this and had not been asked for it and had come anyway because there are about nine people in this valley who come to a room when there is a fire in it, and he had not stood in the aisle and he was not going to, and the hundred and forty cut into the end of the bench he was leaning on had not moved in about nine years and was not going to move that night, and the row is at the back because you can get out of that room at the back in about a second and a half.**

**And then the room did what a room does, which is that about four people said nothing and about nine people said one thing each, and the ninth generation did not say anything, and the man of thirty-four did not say anything, and the finding that came out of it was not in the room at all and was made by a man of twenty-two on the outside stair that night.**

“A man of thirty-four can feel it in a road,” said Osgar Vell, “and a man of fifty-eight can read a book, and a man of thirty-two can cross a line, and there is not one person in this valley who can do the thing, and the three of them have all told me so inside a week and not one of them has said what the thing is.”

“So you are going to be the fourth,” said Aren Kest.

“No,” said Osgar Vell, from the fourth tread, in the dark, without stopping. “There are about two hundred of us and about four hundred people in this valley, and I am one of the two hundred and I am twenty-two, and I have been in a book since the day I was born and I have never been asked anything, and the man of thirty-four has just told a cold room that the thing has not been tested, and the only untested thing in this valley that two hundred people have anything to do with is the two hundred of us, and you cannot load a person and find out what happens, and my grandmother would tell you the same and she would say it in a kitchen with a fire in it and not on a bank.”

He went up the rest of the stair and did not close the door, and the man off the road stood in the yard in the dark with nine people in a room over a cross who had not been asked anything by anybody, and the wall was outside in it, four hundred feet of the best wall anybody in this valley had ever seen, holding up a byre instead of being the first four hundred yards of a road, and the stand across the beck had four feet of water behind one of its abutments that nobody had looked at since the fifth hour, and somewhere in this valley about four hundred yards of a river was not making the noise it had made every day for longer than anybody had been keeping a book, and a girl of about nine was asleep, and the nail man was at a stall at a market four hundred yards off with a figure of the number of people who go out of this valley written on the back of it in chalk, where anybody can see it, and it was the only figure anybody in this valley was keeping, and there was one sheet of paper in the world with eight columns in it, and it was under a stone, and the stone was on crooked, and nobody in that valley would ever know.
