# Chapter 0174

The consequence of the two sheets lying side by side on the boards of a floor four feet by six is about nine weeks old and it is not going to be improved on by anybody, and the man of fifty-eight stated it in one sentence in the room over the byre at the ninth hour of a morning, and then he would not say it again for two days.

“A valley of about four hundred people has been keeping a winter that an office asked it to keep,” he said, “for about two hundred years, through nine generations, in a form, because a form is a form.”

Nobody in the room said anything at all, and there were three of them, and one of them was twenty-two and had heard a sentence about a winter before and had not liked it, and the two others had heard it a great many times and had never once heard it with a cause in it.

“Keep it. The office sent a sheet out and asked this valley for a figure of the men that go out. The valley never gave it a figure and never sent it back, and what it did instead was open a book and enter the whole count one line at a time, in its own hand, and keep that book going for a hundred and forty years, and put every child in it on the day it was born, including about two hundred of the ninth generation who have not gone out of this valley and have never been asked to and never will be. And the winter went on. And the book is the winter book. And a man off the road was called a carrier at a gate in the ninth week of a cold and there was nowhere to put it, because the only roll in this valley is the ninth spread of a form, and the only words in it are a day and a mark and a hand.”

“That is nine steps in a minute and a half,” said Aren Kest, “and I want to go back over two of them and get them right, because I have been getting things wrong in this valley for thirteen weeks by guessing and I am not going to start at the top of this.”

“Go back over them.”

“The form asks for a figure of men going out. It does not ask about a winter. The winter is what the valley put in the third column because the third column is the only place a valley has ever had for a count, and the form has eight columns and the one that would have held this valley's winter is not one of the eight, and I have looked at all eight, and there is nothing on that sheet about a cold.”

“That is right,” said Iven Sarr, “and I have been in this room for two hours working out how to say the thing wrong, and you have taken it off me.”

“Because it is not a form about a winter. It is a form about men going out, and this valley has been answering it in the only way it knows how, which is with a book, and the answer it has been giving for nine generations is that everybody here is somebody who goes out, including the two hundred who have never gone, because the only way to answer a count of men going out in a valley with no writ and no office and no clerk is to put down every person in the place where the men would be and let a reader work out the rest.” He put his hand flat on the ninth spread, which is what he does. “Nobody put two hundred people in a column to be struck. There is no form on this earth that the ninth spread could be used for. It is a birth list with a day in it. And it is a birth list that has been kept in the shape of somebody else's answer for a hundred and forty years, and the person who first put it in that shape did not write down that he had done it, and I am not going to say that is a sinister thing, because I have read the four lines at the head of the first spread this morning and the man who did it wrote his reasons at the top of his own book and put them in the order he did it.”

“Which was?”

“Which was that he did not want to fill in the fifth column.” Iven Sarr looked at his own hand on the cover and then took it off. “That is the whole motive of every written thing in this valley that I have read in my life. Not a plot. A man did not want to write a name, so he wrote four lines instead, and then he used the rest of the form.”

**And then the man of thirty-two said the thing that is the finding this whole valley has been built out of for two hundred years without knowing it, and he said it in the middle of a room, and it is not the last line of anything, and Sarr asked him to write it down and there was nowhere to write it down and that is how it got on the page.**

“An office is the most durable thing in this book,” he said. “I have been in four counties and two of them have been ruined and one has been rebuilt and none of the paper in any of them has gone anywhere. A county can break an oath in public. That is what the Marches did and it took them a year and a man with a spade and about nine days in standing water, and the oath is broken and it stays broken and everybody knows it. A family can burn a roll. That takes a night and a bucket and about nine generations to pay for, and I have seen the price of both of those things with my own eyes and neither of them once frightened the office that sent the form.”

“And yours,” said Osgar Vell.

“Mine. I will come to mine in about four months if I ever do and I am not going to spend a morning on it.” He turned round. “And what has happened here is that a piece of paper has outlived nine generations of men who all knew that it did not apply to them. Nobody in this valley ever answered it. Nobody in this valley ever withdrew it. Nobody in this valley ever even worked out what it was for until a man of fifty-eight stood in a doorway with a lamp. And the reason is not that an office is powerful. The reason is that an office that has asked a question and had no answer does not have a way of saying that it has not had an answer, and so it goes on being an unanswered question in a drawer, and the drawer is under a stone, and the stone is in a room that is older than the byre, and the byre belongs to a family of the eighth generation, and the family do not know.”

“That is all of it,” said Iven Sarr, “and it is nine words longer than I have ever said anything, and it took a man from a county that has an office in it to say it in a room that does not, and I have got the family of this byre standing in this room and I would like it noticed that they have not contradicted one word of it, because they have been in this valley their whole lives and they have known since they were old enough to count that nobody ever comes about that form, and they have never once said out loud *nobody ever comes about that form*, and I have now said it, and I am the keeper of the only book in the valley, and nine years ago I was thirty and I could have said it any week I liked.”

Nobody said that it was the wrong week. He waited to find out whether anybody was going to, and a minute and a half went by, and the fire had been out for two hours.

**And then the man of eighty-one was told about the form by a woman who makes numbers and came up the stair himself, because it is about four steps and a gate and a bench and nobody in this valley has asked him to do anything in sixty years and he has run out of things to want.**

He took a long time to get up the outside stair and the fourth tread said so under him and he went over it out of nine years of habit, and he came into a room with a fire that had been out for two hours in it and three people in it and a sheet of paper on a trestle with six lines on the back of it.

“Say that again,” he said, to the paper and not to anybody. “The part about nobody ever coming about it.”

“Nobody has ever answered it and nobody has ever withdrawn it,” said Iven Sarr.

“Then I am eighty-one and I have been in this valley since before there was a bridge over the beck, which there is not now either, and I have never once in my life wondered about that form.” He put the stick across his knees. “I have wondered about the other one. I have wondered about the room over the cross for sixty years. I have been in that room sixty times and I have never once heard what is in it, and I have never once wondered about a piece of paper under a byre, and I want to say that out loud in a room where a man of thirty-two can write it down, because it is what a person of my age is worth in a valley like this and I have four things left that I am certain of and that is the newest of them.”

“It cannot go in a book,” said Aren Kest. “A book of this valley is a day and a mark and a hand.”

“I know what a book of this valley is. I have been in the room over the cross sixty times and I have never once been in the room with the book in it, and I have just found out in about nine seconds that the two rooms are the same room with the door shut, and I am seventy-nine and I am not going to carry this out of the room with me in my pocket as though it had not happened in front of witnesses.” He looked at the window, which is about the size of a man's hand and does not do what light does. “You have been in this valley thirteen weeks. I have been in it eighty. I have never asked one question in this room and neither have you, and today a man of fifty-eight has read out eight columns off a floor and a young man of twenty-two has said his own name out loud on a stair, and I have come up nine steps to say that I would have asked one question in my life if anybody had ever stood in this room and looked as though they were going to ask it. That is what an old man in a valley with no office in it has got, and I have had it for sixty years and given it away for free, and I would like somebody to notice that the price of it was the looking and not the asking.”

Nobody looked at each other.

“And about the tenth generation,” he said, and stopped, and went on. “I have been in that room sixty times and I have watched thirty of the eighth generation and the ones before them stand in the aisle and be asked and come out, and there is a stroke cut into the end of the bench at the back of the right-hand row for every one of them, and it says a hundred and forty. And the ninth generation has not stood in that aisle once. I have been in that room sixty times and not one of the ninth has ever been in it and it is not because they are unwilling, because I have watched a hundred and forty of them go past a door that is open in a cold week. And the tenth generation is coming up behind the ninth and there is a woman of sixty-six at four looms who has told me she is not going to be asked again, and there is nobody in this valley who will ask them, and I am telling you that the aisle over the cross has now gone two generations without being used and the benches are still bolted down and the hundred and forty is still cut into the end of the one at the back, and nobody has ever been struck off one of them, and the number has not moved in nine years and it is not going to move again in my lifetime, and I would like somebody to write the word *why* somewhere and put it in the column about a person, and I am aware that there is no such column and that is exactly what I am asking about.”

“That is a question,” said Aren Kest, “and I am not going to be asked it and I am not going to ask it, and I have been in this valley thirteen weeks and that is the first time anybody has said a question to me in a room and not had it taken off me inside four seconds, and I would like it noticed that a man of eighty-one has done the thing the man of fifty-eight did at a gate about a stranger, which is say a true thing out loud in a gap in a sentence about something else, and neither of them has ever been asked about it by anybody, and the word over the cross has now been a question in this valley for nine hundred years and it has never once been a question in a room.”

**And then he turned the form over on the trestles and put his hand flat on the blank back of it, and there is a difference between the front of a form and the back of a form that everybody who has ever handled one knows, and the front has a place for you and the back does not, and that is why a man writes on the back of a thing.**

“I have not got paper,” he said. “I have got about forty leaves tied with a piece of tarred hemp in a chest under these boards, and nine hundred leaves of the rag paper in this room, and every one of them is a leaf of a book of this valley, and if I put a copy of anything on one of those I have made a second copy of a book, and I have spent nineteen years not doing that and I am not going to do it at the age of fifty-eight on a morning when I have found out what is under my own byre.”

“The blank back,” said Aren Kest.

“The blank back of a form that nobody has answered in about two hundred years, and I am going to put six lines on it, and it is going to be the only thing in this valley that has ever been written on the back of a form, and it is not a copy of a book and it names no person, and if it is the worst mistake I ever make then it will be the first mistake I ever make of a kind that was about being wrong rather than about being silent.”

He took a stub out of the second fire and wrote six lines on the back of a two-hundred-year-old form in a room with one window about the size of a man's hand, and the stub was not his, and the writing was not like the writing in his book, because the hand of a man of fifty-eight writing six sentences is not the hand of a man of fifty-eight writing two hundred days.

*This is the valley of the Burn and the book of the quarter is the book of the winter, kept in a stone room over a byre, and it has not a name in it and nobody has ever given it one.*

*The men go out on the fourth day of the cold and eleven of them go and four of them come back, and I have not got the figure for a year and I am not going to get it by writing it on a form in a room with nobody in it.*

*There is no person appointed to answer for the men that go out, and there never has been, and the reason is written at the head of the first spread and I have read it about nine hundred times and never once knew what it was a rule about.*

*I have kept this book nineteen years and I have not written a figure in it. I am not going to start on a form either.*

*Your third column has been answered nine times in a hundred and forty years and not once in a book, and the answer is a mark with a stroke through it from the top, and the last of the nine was cut about a hundred and forty years ago, and there is another one cut in a wall two steadings up this bank by a brother with a knife in a wet week, and nobody in this valley has ever put the two of them side by side and I have not either.*

*This is written on the back of a form and it is not going anywhere, and if it is ever taken out of this valley by anybody then it will be the first thing to leave this valley in nine generations that was not a person.*

He put the stub down and read the six lines and did not read them aloud, which was a thing he had told the man off the road he would do on a different morning about a different thing, and neither of them said anything about it.

**And then there is the stone, and the stone is the last of it and it took two hands and about a minute and a half, and neither man said anything while it was being done.**

The stone is a piece of the byre coping and it was squared on one face by a man of fifty-eight in the dark on the eleventh day of a wall, and its purpose is not to keep anything in, because a sheet of laid paper under a stone on a lime bed in a dry room in a valley is not going anywhere, and everybody in this valley has known that for a hundred and forty years.

It is to keep the room looking like a room.

He put it back on top of the six lines and it went on crooked, because he could not get the heel of his hand out from under the far edge and lift and slide at the same time, and the square face of the stone was down and the round went up and the stone lay across the sheet at about a hand's width on the diagonal instead of square to the boards.

He looked at it.

“That is a hundred and forty years of somebody's hand on a stone and it has just been put back wrong by a man with a bad back and there is nobody in this valley who is ever going to know that except the two of us,” he said, “and I am not going to straighten it, and if you straighten it I will take it away again.”

The door shut on its frame without a sound. The fourth tread said so under both of them, and the woman in the yard with the yoke had a question in her face and did not ask it for the eleventh day.

**And at the gate of the Low Holm there was a girl of about nine with a barrow to get out at the ford most days, and she was not getting it out, and she was standing at the gate with her hands in her sleeves looking at the fourth tread from about thirty feet off, counting the treads, and there are nine of them and the fourth one is the only one that talks, and a girl of nine can count a stair from thirty feet and has never once been asked by anybody in this valley what the stair is for, and she is the first of her family that has never stood in the room over the cross, and so is everybody her age, and there are about forty of them, and the man of thirty-two knew two of the figures in that number exactly and one of them had been written down at the bottom of a page of a book four hundred miles off where it was against a printed warning of three weeks that has not moved in two years, and the other one of them had been the number of a child on the ninth spread of the only roll in the valley, four lines up from the foot of it, in a hand that does not vary, and he had told nobody and he was not going to and there was nowhere in four hundred miles to put it.**
