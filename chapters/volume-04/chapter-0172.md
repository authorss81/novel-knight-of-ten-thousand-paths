# Chapter 0172

There are two hands on that sheet and one of them has never varied by the width of a hair in about two hundred years and the other one has.

It is worth being exact about which is which, because a man who has kept a book for nineteen years does not have many opinions and he has got two.

The slow careful one wrote four things and stopped. It is a hand that came to the sheet and did a job on it and went away, and the letters are all one size from the first line to the last, and the hand got tired in the last word of the second answer and came back up in the first word of the third. That is a person. That is a man about forty with cold hands in a room with a stone floor.

The other one wrote one thing.

It is a hand with a pen angle, which is a thing you can see in a man who has been taught to hold a nib down because the office ruled a line about it, and the down strokes are the same depth the whole length of the line and the letters do not hurry anywhere, and the *r* is the same on every single occasion, and it is the same hand that wrote the third answer and the fourth answer and the fourth answer is a different day to the third one and the hand is identical, and there is no way on this earth to write the same thing twice like that and mean anything by it.

“That one is an office,” said Iven Sarr, “and I do not know how I know that, and I have said it out loud and it is in a room with nobody in it.”

“You know it because nobody chose it,” said Aren Kest. “Pick up a sheet and write the same letter eleven times and you will not vary in the eleventh either. The thing that tells you is the fourth answer and the third answer, which are on different days. A man varies because he is tired. That does not vary because nobody was in the room.”

---

**And then they were up the stair and along the yard and he was in his own room over a byre at about the eighth hour of a morning with a lamp and a sheet of paper, and the book was on the trestles where it had been left, and the ninth spread was open at the last leaf, and he put the form down on the boards beside it, and the two of them lay there together in the light off the one window about the size of a man's hand.**

The paper of the roll is rags, made four valleys over, and it is soft, and it has gone furry at a corner where somebody turned it, and there is not a line in it of any kind. Nothing in this valley has ever been made with a mould in it. That is a thing you can be shown once and never have to be told again.

The paper of the form is laid, and there are chain lines in it about an inch apart running up it, and the ink on it has gone through the sheet in the strokes of the letters, which is what happens when a sheet has been folded in four in a damp place for a very long time, and the folds have gone soft like cloth, and the corner of it is a stub torn out of a pad and the edge of the stub has gone furry in a different way from a corner, because a furry corner is a thumb and a furry stub is a knife.

“Take it up to the light,” said Iven Sarr, and he took it up himself, and he held it a foot off his face for about a minute and a half without saying anything, and then he said what was on his mind, which was the only thing on his mind.

“There is a trough in my wife's mother's yard that was cut in one piece,” he said. “It is about nine feet and it is in the wall of a byre that was standing when my wife's grandmother was a girl, and she used to say it was older than anything, and I said that to you in this room in the ninth week and you wrote it down, and I am not taking it back. It is older than anything in this valley that a person can put a hand on. And I have had this sheet in my hand for about ten minutes and I would put that trough against it and I would put the lintel over my own door against it and I would put the coping that you laid on the wall at the Low Holm against it, and that coping is a hundred and forty years old and my father told me it was a hundred and forty years old when I was eleven years old.”

“So it is older than the trough.”

“It is older than the trough. I am not able to put a number on it and I have not been able to put a number on it in about nine minutes, and I have been in this room fifty years and I have handled paper every day of it.” He put the sheet down. “And I am not going to ask you to put a number on it either, and I would like to know that you have noticed that you have not.”

“There is a number on it.”

“There is a number in the sixth column. It is a number of years in a reckoning of theirs, and I have read it out to you and I could not tell you what it means, and I have had the sheet in my hand for ten minutes and I have not been able to work out what it is a number of. I have never had a year in my life that I could turn into anything. This valley has a cold and a week of the cold and a generation, and a man of my age can tell you to the week how long it is since the first of them and I could not tell you to the year, and not one person alive could, and there is nobody to ask.” He put two fingers on the sixth column. “And I want to say one more thing about that number and then I will leave it.

“The only count of anything anybody in this valley has ever made in a hundred and forty years is a girl of nine going round a wall in the eleventh week of the first cold and counting the stones in it, and she got four hundred and six, and she would not ask anybody what it meant because then she would have to be told, and it is on nobody's slate and it is in nobody's book, and that is all this valley counts.”

**And the man of fifty-eight sat down on the edge of the trestles in a room that has one window about the size of a man's hand, and put his hand flat on the book, which is what he does, and then took it off again and did not do it twice.**

“Now the practice of it,” he said. “You asked me at the bottom of the stair what a thing is that gets written on a form by an office and not by a man, and I have not got to the answer yet and I have got something else and it is the same thing.

“The head of it says, in the printing, *to be sent round once in every seven years, and returned to the same, and not to be sent on.* Three things in eleven words. Seven years, and the same, and not on.

“Not to be sent on. That is an office saying that a form does not get passed on to a place that could deal with it. And there is nothing improper about that at all. It is a sensible instruction. It is the instruction of an office that intends to deal with the return itself, once, in its own hand, and not to hand a piece of work to somebody two valleys away who will do it worse and take four years.

“And that is why nobody has ever answered that sheet. Not because anybody in this valley cannot write. Because a form cannot be sent on, and a valley of about four hundred people cannot carry a sheet of paper to Grayford, because there is no road out of here and there never has been, and there is not one cart in this market. An office that builds a form so that the answer has to come back to it has built a form that a valley like this one can never answer, and it did that about two hundred years ago, and the office was right to design it that way, and the office has never once been told about this valley, because the thing an office sends round is the thing that tells it.”

Nobody in that room had anything to say to that for about a minute, and the fire had gone out at some point in the morning and nobody had noticed.

“And the other half of it is the column,” he said. “Number seven. Withdrawn. There is a rule drawn across the head of the space and a man rules a line across a space when he means to put one word in it, and it has not been ruled across. I have been keeping a book for nineteen years and I have wanted to know about the difference between a space waiting to be filled and a space that has been filled with nothing for a hundred years, and it is the difference between a trough and a hole.

“Nobody has answered that sheet and nobody has withdrawn it, and those are two different pieces of work and one of them is a clerk's and one of them is a decision, and neither of them has been done in about two hundred years, and I have got both of them in one hand and I have not been able to hold them for longer than a minute without wanting to put one of them down.”

---

**And the fourth column is the emptiest-looking thing on that sheet, and it is the only one of the eight this valley has answered at all, and it has answered it with something that is not a figure, and there is no way on this earth to enter it in the space provided.**

*4. The number of those that have gone out and not come back.*

“That is an inch and a half of ruled space and there is nothing in it, and I want to tell you what is in it, and then I am going to stop and go and do the two pails, because I have been at this with a lamp for two hours and my wife's mother's cattle come in at the fourth hour whatever is on the floor of my back room.

“The answer to that column in this valley is a mark with a stroke under it. That is a man who went out of here and has not come back, and there are strokes under marks in nine spreads, and I have never counted them and I am not going to start, and I have wanted to start for nineteen years and I have not, and I know exactly why I have not and it is that the stroke is a person and a figure of persons in a room is a list.”

And the mark with a stroke through it, from the top, is a different thing, and that one is a man who went out and came back, and there are nine of those in the whole book and the last one is a hundred and forty years old. Nine. And the ninth spread has about two hundred marks on it without a stroke of either kind, which says that they are here and nothing else, and a stroke is not a figure, and a mark is not a figure either, and the column wants a figure.

Nobody in that room had said anything for a while.

“So there are two columns on that sheet that this valley has answered, and I have told you about one of them, and the other one is the fifth, and you have heard about the fifth already,” said Aren Kest.

“You have heard about the fifth and you have not been down the stairs and I have been down the stairs,” said Iven Sarr.

**And then the count, which is the thing on the form, and the finding that the count has been kept four years in chalk on the back of a stall at a market.**

“That is the number of men going out of the quarter,” said Aren Kest. “Third column. It is asking this valley for a figure.”

“Yes.”

“And this valley has never given it one and has never been asked in a way that could be refused, because there is nobody here to ask and nobody here to be responsible to a refusal, and that is the arrangement and it is nine generations old and it has worked.” He put his hand on the form. “Except that it has not worked, and here is what has happened instead, and I have been in this valley thirteen weeks and it took me until this morning.”

“There is a man in this valley who keeps a figure of exactly the thing that column asks for, on the back of a stall, in chalk, where anybody can see it, and he has kept it for four years, and he keeps it because there is nowhere else in this valley to keep a figure, and he has told me himself that his wife says it is not a market day for a nail man.”

Iven Sarr put his hand off the book.

“He has never heard of that sheet,” he said.

“He has never heard of that sheet and neither have I, and he is the only man in four hundred people who is doing what the third column was asking for, and he has been doing it four times a year on the back of a thing he sells, and he is going to go on doing it, and the reason he does it is that a man who does not keep a figure of anything is a man who has nothing to say when somebody asks.”

“Four years.”

“Four years. And the form has been on that floor for about two hundred.” He turned his hand over and looked at it as though there were something on it that had just appeared. “That is a thing I cannot do anything with and I have been in this room for an hour with it. That man is keeping the count that sheet was sent out to get. He does not know the sheet exists. Nobody will ever tell him, because there is nowhere in this valley to put an explanation, and if I told him on a market day he would be a man who had been told about a form, and in nine years there would be a woman in that market who could remember it and not one thing else about it, and that is how this valley loses everything, and it has lost nine generations of a good deal in under nine generations and I have told that to a man off the road in this room once and I am not going to say it again.”

“All right,” said Aren Kest.

**And that was the last of it, and the finding came about four minutes later, when the two sheets were lying side by side on the boards of the floor of the room over a byre and neither of them was on a table because there is no table in the room.**

They were the same.

Not the same paper and not the same size and not the same weight. The same rule. The ruled lines on the form and the ruled lines on the last leaf of the ninth spread stand at the same distance apart, within a hair, and the depth of a ruled space on one of them is the depth of a ruled space on the other, and the margin on the left is the same margin, and a man who has ruled about nine hundred yards of string and a mile of boundary in four counties can see that from a foot away with his hands behind his back.

Three columns on the spread, ruled to the same three widths as three of the numbers on the form, and the day of a birth in the first of them, and a mark in the second, and a hand in the third.

“It is a form,” said Aren Kest.

“It is a form.”

“It is a form with the numbers left off and about four columns taken out of the middle and about a column of names put in at the right-hand edge on the older spreads, and the person who made it did it that way on purpose, and the reason it has never once been recognised as a form in nine generations is that there is not one form in this valley for it to be recognised against.”

“That is it. That is all of it and I have kept it for nineteen years and I have never once laid a piece of paper beside it.”

“The sheet asks for a figure of men going out. And the ninth spread has got a figure in it. Not a figure of men going out, a figure of children arriving.” He did not touch either of them. “A valley that had a form in front of it and eight columns to fill would have put four in the third and gone on with the winter. And instead it entered the whole count one line at a time in its own book, with a day against every line, over a generation, and it never once occurred to anybody that it had been asked for a figure, because a figure comes out all at once in an office and a figure comes out one line at a time in a family, and the second kind does not look like the first kind, and a man who has been putting down a day against a line for nineteen years is not going to look at a page of rules and see his own handwriting in it.”

He got up off the trestles, which took him a moment, and stood over the two sheets with his hands behind his back in a room with one window about the size of a man's hand, and he looked at the top of the ninth spread for a good deal longer than he looked at anything else, and he did not say anything about the line at the head of it, and neither of them mentioned it, and it was still there.

“Then who took four columns out of it,” he said.
