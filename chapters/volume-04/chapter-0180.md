# Chapter 0180

The twenty-fourth day of a cold in its second winter is the day the keeper of the book in a valley of about four hundred people put a figure in it for the first time in a hundred and forty years, and he did it at about the sixth hour with four people in the room, and he did it with a stub of the same second fire that had written six lines on the back of a form four days earlier, and he put it on the last leaf of the ninth spread in the day column, because there is no other column for it and because that is the column where a day goes, and a figure of people is the same kind of thing as a day if you have not got anywhere else to put it.

There are three of them and he wrote them in a stack, and under each of them he wrote a mark, because a line without a mark in this book is a line that has never happened, and over the top of the three of them he wrote a hand, and it was his own, and it varied.

*About four hundred. About a hundred and eighty-nine. Nine.*

Nobody in that room said anything for a while, and the fact that nobody said anything was not respect and was not shock, it was that four people were looking at a page with three numbers on it where nine spreads of days and marks and hands had been, and there is no speed at which a person gets used to that, and the room had one window about the size of a man's hand and the light in it does not do what light does in a county that keeps its hours.

“Nine is three households,” said Aren Kest.

“Nine is three households and I have put nine on the page, and a hundred and forty years of this valley has never had a household in it, and I have had a man of thirty-four do that arithmetic in about nine seconds with a rope on in front of me and about four other people, and he is a good man with a level and I would trust him with a wall and I would not trust him with that.” He put the stub down. “Nine is what four people in their own heads said on a walk over eleven days. It is not a count of anybody. It is the best anybody in this valley can do, and it is the first figure anybody in this valley has ever worked out about the people in it, and it is wrong, and I have written it down anyway, and I am not going to strike it out, and I would like somebody to say why I am right about that.”

“Because a struck figure is a figure with a reason in the margin and there is nowhere in this valley to keep the margin,” said Aren Kest. “Your own house, four days ago.”

“My own house, four days ago.” He looked at the nine for a bit. “And if I put a name against the nine then in nine years there will be nine people in this valley who know that they are on a page, and three households will have to leave, and one of them is a man who has been at a stall four market days running and says a straight answer about when the next market is and gets thanked for it by nobody. So there is a nine and there is not going to be a name, and the man who made the nine is in this room and knows he made it and I have not written his name either, and that is the first time in nineteen years I have had a fact in this book with a person on it and left the person off, and the reason is the reason I have had for nineteen years and it is the reason I wrote four lines at the head of the first spread about a person, and I have never once known that until this afternoon.”

Nobody had told him that. That is on the page because it is true, and because the man of fifty-eight said it in a room with four people in it and did not look at any of them.

---

**And then the money, which is the third thing in that room that week that had never happened, and it is on the trestles in chalk because a trestle is a thing that falls down and a book is a thing that does not.**

“Eleven days,” said Wystan Roole, “and four of us and a woman, and about nine hundred doors, and there is no coin in this valley and there has not been any in nine generations, and what the four of us ate between the fourth and the fifteenth of a cold came out of eleven kitchens, and about four households gave us salt, and not one household was asked twice and not one household was told what it was for.”

“You have not cost it.”

“I have not cost it and I have had a fortnight to cost it and I have not, because there is no figure in this valley and a figure is what you would start with, and I have been waiting for a man with a notebook to say one and he has not and I am going to ask him.”

“There is one,” said Aren Kest, and got the notebook out, and it is a book he carries in a coat and has not written a page of in since a cold week on a bank in the ninth week of this valley, and the reason he has not is in the book and in four books behind him and nobody in this room is going to see it.

He turned to a clean page and wrote the sum in a county's money, in the county's money and not in this valley's, and he wrote the addition out, because his hand does that and because a sum that is only a total is a thing a person can be argued out of.

*One man, one day, at three pence.*
*Sixteen men: three, sixteen times over, is forty-eight.*
*Forty-eight a day for eleven days: forty-eight eleven times over is five hundred and twenty-eight.*
*A barrow of salt at fourpence.*
*Five hundred and twenty-eight and four is five hundred and thirty-two pence, which is eleven marks and fourpence.*

**“That is the cost of the asking in a county about four hundred miles off where a day of an unskilled man is three pence, and it is eleven marks and fourpence, and it is a figure in a money that does not exist in this valley and cannot be changed into anything that does, and I am not going to try, and the reason I am not going to try is the reason I have given in four other rooms since the ninth week and I am not going to say it again in a room where the man of fifty-eight is keeping a book.”

“The valley's own price of that fortnight is eleven kitchens and about four barrows of salt and nobody's figure,” said Iven Sarr, “and those are the two and I am not putting one of them on a page with the other one and neither is he and it is not because it would be wrong, it is because there is no way on this earth to add them and a person who tried would be doing arithmetic on a thing to make it go away.”

**And that is the first distinction anybody in this valley has ever drawn, and it took two men and a stub of chalk and about forty seconds: a figure of people goes in the book and a figure of money goes on a trestle in chalk, and both of them are figures, and one of them is about nine generations of people and the other one is about eleven kitchens, and if the second one is still legible in four hundred years then this valley will have been a place that cost something to ask a question in, and if it is not then the asking will have been free, and it was not free, and there is a man with a limp who knows the price of it in a county that has an office in it and has told nobody in this valley a single word about it.**

He wrote it on the end of the trestle in chalk, in a county's money, about eleven inches high, and the trestle has been the same trestle for nineteen years and the chalk will be off it inside a year and neither of them wrote a second copy of it anywhere, deliberately, on purpose, and the first of them said why and the reason was a rule about a thing written down twice.

**And then they got to the arithmetic that was in the page and not in the book, and it took four minutes, and it is the only arithmetic anybody in that valley has ever done on the people in it, and the answer was wrong, and it was entered anyway, and that is the whole of what the twenty-fourth day of the second winter of a cold in a ninth generation came to.**

“Eleven of the ninth went over the pass on the fourth day of this cold,” said Wystan Roole. “That is not a household figure and it is not a family figure and it is eleven people, and they are the only part of the two hundred that the sixth thing did not reach, and I have been to about nine hundred doors in eleven days and I have not been to one of them, and neither has the man of thirty-four, and neither has you, and it is a week to the top of the pass and then they are gone and about four of them come back in the first warm week.

“So the figure of the standing is not two hundred. It is a hundred and eighty-nine, and I have got that by taking a number off a number and neither of the numbers is in a book that anybody can check, and the second one is yours, and I would like somebody to write down that the standing of the ninth generation in a valley of about four hundred people has a figure of a hundred and eighty-nine in it and that nobody in this valley will ever be able to say whether it is right.”

“About four come back,” said Iven Sarr.

“About four come back and seven do not, and that is the figure of the ninth generation a valley can actually get to, and it is the answer to your question and the answer is that it cannot be done at all, because the only people who can agree to it are the two hundred and seven of the two hundred are not in this valley and will not be in this valley for about nine weeks, and there is no road out of here to go and ask them, and there has not been a road out of here for nine generations, and the seven of them are the only people in the world who could say no and have not been asked.”

“Then we wait for the warm week,” said Iven Sarr.

“No we do not, and I want it noticed that I did not say that, and I want somebody to notice that a man of fifty-eight who keeps the only book in a valley of four hundred people has just been told in his own room by a bridgekeeper that the thing cannot be done for about nine weeks and has gone straight to the only thing he knows how to do with a thing he cannot do, which is to read out what is in a book.” He put the stick down against the trestle. “And I am not going to tell either of you what the answer is, and I have had it in my mouth twice in four days and I have not said it either time, and a man of thirty-four who has felt a thing in a road since the first winter and cannot name it and a man of fifty-eight who has read about four hundred books and cannot name it are in a room with a young man of twenty-two who has said his own name once, and none of the three of us is going to name it, and I would like it noticed in about nine years that we were all in a room and not one of us said it.”

**And then the page, which is the last thing that happened in that room in the second winter of a cold in its ninth generation, and it is the first page he has given to anybody in four years and a half.**

He tore a leaf out of the back of the notebook. That is a thing nobody does with a book that is the only record of a crossing, and he did it in front of four people, and he folded it once and put it on the trestles and squared it with his hand the way a man of fifty-eight squares a book and not the way a man of thirty-two squares anything.

“That is nine lines and it is the only paper in four hundred miles that anybody can be given, and I am giving it to you, and I have not given anybody a page since a year in a county that has a stone in a field in it, and I am not going to say anything about why I am doing it tonight instead of the other nine nights of the last fortnight.”

“What is on it.”

“Six things that were said at about nine hundred doors in the same words in the same order, and the count, and the three households, and one line about a word.” He put his thumb on it. “And the line about a word is the reason it is not the only paper about a word in the world, and I am going to read it out because I have written it and I have been looking at it for four minutes and I would like to hear it in the room.”

He read it.

“The question was asked in a word that means a bridge in this valley. I did not put the other word on it. I have been in this valley thirteen weeks and I did not put it on it, and the reason is that every thing that has happened to me here has been done in a word that is not mine, and I have understood about nine of those words and got four of them wrong, and I am not going to be the first person in the history of this valley to say a thing in my own language and have it be the thing on the page.”

He put his hand off the paper.

“That is on a page now, and I know what it is, and I have thought about it for four days and I am still going to do it, and I would like a man of fifty-eight to tell me it is a bad idea.”

“It is a bad idea,” said Iven Sarr, and then he sat down on the end of the trestles and did not pick it up for about nine seconds, and then he picked it up and read it again himself, which he did not do in front of anybody for the four hundred books under the boards and he knew it and neither of the other three said so.

“It is a bad idea and there is one reason it is worth doing and I have not got it for about four minutes and it is this. A rule about what is written down is not a rule about what is done, and a man who has written down what a thing means can still get it wrong, and the two are not the same mistake, and I have spent nineteen years in the second one and I have never once been in the first, and I do not know which of them this valley is going to need in nine years and neither does he, and it is the first thing anybody in this valley has ever been able to hand forward that is not a day or a mark or a hand.”

He stood up with it in his hand.

“I am not going to put it in the book. A book of this valley is a day and a mark and a hand and I have been telling him that since the ninth week and I have not been able to say it in about nine years and I said it to a man of thirty-two in a cold room in a market and I did not know I had said it. I am not going to put a meaning in this book. So it goes in the room, and it is not in a book, and it is not on a slate, and it is not on a wall, and there is nowhere else in this valley for it to go.”

---

**And it went on the seat of the bench at the back of the right-hand row, folded once, and there are twenty-nine benches bolted to the floor of that room in four rows with a number cut into the end of each one, and a hundred and forty cut into the end of that one, and the hundred and forty is a count of the times anybody has been asked to stand in that aisle, and nobody has ever been struck off one of them, and there is not one thing on any of the twenty-nine that has ever been put on one, and there is one now.**

Nobody went and looked at it. That is the arrangement of a room like that and it is an arrangement older than anything anybody in that valley can put a hand on, and four people were still in the room when he put it there, and the third-generation's house is two steadings up the bank and there is a mark cut in a wall in a corner of a byre yard with a stroke through it, and the ninth spread of the only roll in a valley of about four hundred people has three figures at the foot of it in a hand that varies, and eleven people of the ninth generation are at the top of a pass or beyond it and cannot be asked and have not been asked and will not be able to be asked for about nine weeks, and four of them will come back and seven will not, and a girl of about nine with a barrow she has not gone and got out at the ford for three weeks was at the top of the outside stair with her hands in her sleeves and had not knocked and was not going to, and the fourth tread said so under anybody who went up it, and the light in the room over the cross does not get dark at any hour anybody keeps in this valley, and nobody has ever asked why, and there is no hour here, and there has never been an hour here, and the form on the floor of the room at the back of the byre has been under a squared stone for about two hundred years and it has been answered by nobody and it has been withdrawn by nobody and the stone is on the diagonal because a man of fifty-eight could not get the heel of his hand out from under the far edge of it in the fourteenth week of a cold, and nobody in that valley is going to know that either, and the ninth spread of the book and the form under the stone are four steps apart and about two hundred years apart and there is a door between them and it is shut on its frame.
