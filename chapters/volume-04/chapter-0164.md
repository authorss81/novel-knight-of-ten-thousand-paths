# Chapter 0164

A retaining wall is not a difficult thing and there is nothing clever in it, which is why a man from four hundred miles off can stand at the corner of one at about the fourth hour of a morning and have nothing to say about it for a quarter of an hour.

He had a footing to look at. The footing was a foot wider on each side than the wall, and it was one course deeper than anybody in that yard expected, and it was laid in the only stone that is any good for a footing, which is the stone that is not flat, broken out of the old field wall with a bar and not with a hammer, and about a fifth of what came off that wall had gone down the bank on the first morning and the rest of it was in the footing with the beds sloping back into the bank, because a footing that sheds its water is a footing and a footing that holds its water is a hole with a wall on it.

The wall was a double face. Two skins of stone, laid to the same line, with the hearting packed behind them in lime and rubble, and a batter on it, which is to say that the face leans back into the bank as it goes up, and a batter is not decoration, it is the difference between a wall that stands forty years and a wall that stands eleven.

“You are going to lose that line on the fourth course,” said Aren Kest.

“I am going to lose that line on the fourth course,” said Wystan Roole, “and I am going to have it back by the eighth, and the man who does the fourth course is the man who did the second, and he is the only one of the sixteen I would let within nine feet of a string, and his name is a thing he tells people.”

**And the string came down on the sixth course and went up on the ninth, and it came down and went up four times in three days, and every time it came down it was a man on a ladder with a plumb bob made out of a bit of wrought iron and a length of string, and every time it went up it was the same man and the same bit of iron, and the bit of iron is one of the two things the man off the road recognised in that valley in the first six weeks.**

The other thing was the coping of the byre, which was a single course of flat stones set on the wall and was about a hundred and forty years old and was done in lime, and it was better work than anything that had been built at that byre in nine generations and it had been done by a woman, because it is the kind of thing a woman does who has been married to a man in a valley for a long time and has had to make the top of her own byre not let the water in.

The woman of about sixty-six with four looms came up the bank at about the second hour of the second day with a set of slates in a string bag, and put them down at the end of the line, and would not be talked out of them.

“Stand on those,” she said. “You are all going to stand on those by the fourth day and none of you are going to be told to.”

“Why slates.”

“Because it is a wet week and it is a cold bank and you are going to be on your feet for nine hours and I have stood on a board for fifty-one years with my feet in a bucket of nothing and I know what it does to a person.” She put the bag down and turned round. “That is all of the reason. I am not going to stand here and be thanked for it.”

She was not thanked for it, because nobody in that yard thanked anybody for anything all week, and about four days later a man of twenty-four who had never had a job in his life took a slate round to the field wall to fetch more stone and put it down and did not tell anybody he had fetched it on a slate.

**And the sixteen men are the whole of what nine generations of a trade comes to when nobody will let it do the thing it was learned for, and about a third of them are the ninth generation, and one of those is twenty-four and has never had a job.**

They came at the fourth hour because the fourth hour is when the light comes off the ice off the top of that bank, and they came in ones and twos and threes across about half a mile of ground with no road on it, and they came on the fourth morning in a line, which is what you do, and nobody arranged the line and everybody stood in it.

There are about thirty of them under the cross and sixteen came, and the fourteen who did not come are not bitter about it. Two of them are over sixty and are not asked to stand on a wet footing. One of them is the mill man and cannot leave the wheel. Eleven of them are the ninth generation and they are twenty-two and twenty-four and twenty-six and they have a skill that is nine generations old in this valley and that nobody in the valley has ever had an occasion to use, which is the specific and peculiar shape of the trouble, because a man who has a skill and no occasion does not stop having the skill. He goes and does the nine other things.

The twenty-four-year-old was the worst at it on the first morning and the best at it by the fourth, and the reason was not that he was quick. It was that he asked. He asked the man doing the second course how deep the bed went and he asked it on the first morning in about a voice you could hear from the other end of the line, and the man told him, and the twenty-four-year-old went back to his own footing and put his course in half an inch deeper than the one before, and the man watched him do it and did not say anything at the time, and said about four hours later, in a voice exactly as loud, that it was right and that nobody had asked him that in six years.

That is the entire of what a trade is in a valley with no work in it. It is the sum total of what a man can teach another man when there is nothing to teach him on.

The bank behind that byre has been going for nine years and it is going because there is a drain behind it and the drain has been coming out under the wall of a different building for about sixty years, and the ground behind the byre is what the water has been eating, and about every third year somebody has put two or three barrow-loads of stone into the place where the ground has come away and it has gone again by the next spring, and nobody has ever said the word *retaining* in this valley because nobody here has needed to.

In about four years it will take the back wall of that byre with it, and when it does it will take a byre of about eleven cows and about a hundred and forty sacks of barley that a family put in there in the warm week because there is nowhere else in this valley to put a hundred and forty sacks of barley in the warm week, and it will do it in about a day, in the thaw, at night, with nine or ten people asleep in the low end of a building.

That is the whole job. Sixteen men, eleven days, about four hundred feet of wall and a hole in the bottom of it, and every one of those figures came out of the mouth of a man of thirty-four on a bank in a cold week, and none of them was asked for by anybody, and the woman whose cows they are has not been told any of it.

The woman of the Low Holm came out and looked at the wall on the second morning, and stood at about the end of the line for a while with her arms in her sleeves, and did not come closer than that, and said two things.

She said that the batter was right, which she knew because her own grandfather had built the coping and the coping has a batter in it, and she said that she had not asked for it, and that she was not going to say thank you for it, and that she would rather somebody did it than nobody, and that these were four separate opinions and she had held all four of them since the middle of the week and had told none of them to anybody until the wall was four courses up and she could see that it was going to be a wall.

Then she went and got a pail and moved it four inches to the left, so that a man standing on it would not be standing in the wet place, and did not explain that either.

**And the man of eighty-one came up the bank on the third day with his stick across his knees, in the sense that he carried it and then held it, and he sat down on the offcut of a byre roof two steadings up that had come off a roof with a hole in it, and he stayed about an hour.**

Nobody stopped. Nobody told him anything. He looked at about nine feet of wall, at the top of it, where the batter was and the string was eleven inches out because a man of twenty-two was leaning on it, and at the man off the road sitting on an upturned pail with a stick across his knees that he was using for nothing, having brought it four hundred miles for four hundred yards.

“You brought that to work with,” he said, eventually.

“To get about in.”

“There is no road.”

“There is no road,” said Aren Kest.

“Then you have brought it a long way for it,” said the old man, and went down the bank on his own, and it was the only thing he said to anybody in that yard that week.

**And on the third day a man of about thirty-four with sixteen men and no work came off the line, wiped his hands on his coat, and said a thing to a stranger on a bank in the eleventh week of a cold in its ninth generation, and he said it once, and he did not say it again to anybody in this valley ever, and no person in that valley has ever repeated it, and it is not a sentence anybody can hand on to somebody else in a book because it was not said in a room.**

He had it ready. That is on the page because it was on the bank: he had had it for about four years, and the only person he had ever said any of it to had been a bench that had told him twice there was no road to build, and the reason he had waited nine years was not that he was shy.

It was that the last four people he had tried to say it to had all been the wrong shape for it. Two of them were the kind of man who agrees with you. One was the kind of man who disagrees with you. And one was a woman of about thirty with a slate who does the births in this valley and who had once, about two years back, corrected a figure of his out loud in a yard in front of nine people and had been right, and he had not spoken to her about it since.

“Then what did you do about that,” said Aren Kest.

“I wrote the figure down she gave me and I have carried it since and I have never used it and I have never said thank you to her, and if you have a better way of doing that I would like to hear it and I do not expect to.”

---

He had it ready, and he got nine lines into it before he stopped and started again at the beginning, and he did not apologise for that either, and the man off the road on a bank with a stick in his hand and nothing to do with except be there did the thing that is the whole of what he is good at, which was to stand on a cold bank for nine hours and look at eleven feet of wall.

He said:

“Right. I have laid about eleven hundred promises in this valley in my time. I do not know that I have laid all of them. I know I laid them. And every single one of them needed doing again, and that is not a fault in them, that is what a promise is, and anybody in this valley who tells you a promise holds is telling you about a bridge and not about a promise.

“A wall is a different animal. A wall you build once. It goes in the ground and it stands there in the dark at three in the morning doing nothing at all, and it has never once asked anybody for anything, and that is the whole of why a wall lasts and a promise does not. It is not better. It just does not want feeding.

“And that is what is under your winter, and I have never seen it and neither has he and neither has the man of eighty-one, and it is not a promise, and it is nine generations old, and it does not want anything, and that is why nobody in this valley has ever found a hole in it and that is why there is a room over a cross where a person stands up in the cold once a generation and asks this valley to keep the winter, and it has never once been refused.

“So here is the part I have not said out loud in nine years and I am saying it once and I am saying it to you because you are the only person on this bank who builds anything.

“A promise has to be renewed or it stops. A working does not. And the reason we go and ask the ninth generation to keep the winter is not that the asking holds anything up. The asking is what we pay for the privilege of it being a promise instead of a working. The price of the working being a promise at all is that somebody has to keep saying it out loud, and we have been saying it since the first of them, and not one of us has ever costed it, and I have been in the room for it and I have counted the rooms.”

He looked at the string, which was about eleven inches out at that moment because a man of twenty-two was leaning on it.

“I have counted the rooms. There is one at the top of the cross and about forty people stand in it and I have been one of the forty for nine years, and I could not tell you in front of them what we are paying for, and that is the whole of what I have got.”

**And he said the rest of it in the middle of the afternoon, to nobody in particular, with his back to a man of twenty-two who was bedding a stone and who heard all of it and has never repeated a word of it.**

“It is the same reason I am putting a wall up at a byre I have not been asked to put a wall at. There is a thing in this valley that needs doing and there are thirty men in it who know how to do it, and a wall is a wall that needs doing, and if the wall stops then the thirty men stop being thirty men and this valley goes back to being four hundred people with a book in it. So I am going to keep the wall going, and I am going to say the other thing out loud at the first meeting where anybody asks me anything, and I do not expect to be asked, because nobody in this valley has ever asked me anything, and that is not a complaint, it is the arrangement, and I have been in it for nine years and I am the only man in it who has ever read anything about it, and that is not a thing I am proud of.”

Then he picked up the plumb bob, and looked at the string, and found it out by about a quarter of an inch, and said nothing to anybody about it.

**And then he turned round and put his hand on the top course and pushed it a quarter of an inch, which is a thing you do to a course to find out whether it is bedded, and the course was bedded, and he said one word to the man nearest him, and the man nearest him went and got a board.**

The snow came off the coping of that byre at about the fourth hour and went into the lime, and a man of twenty-four looked at it and looked at Wystan Roole and did not ask, and the man of fifty-eight came up the outside stair of the room over the byre with a yoke on his shoulders in the middle of all of it, because two pails of water do not stop for a wall, and looked at the line of the string from the top tread, and went back down, and did not say anything to anybody about it, and that was the eleventh week of a cold in its ninth generation and it was not the first morning anybody in that yard had gone to look at a thing somebody else was doing.
