# Chapter 0175

The third generation's house is two steadings up the bank from the Low Holm and it is a house and not a steading, because there are four houses in this valley out of about eleven steadings and the four of them are the four that have been here longest, and this one has a byre with a standing door frame and a barrow wheel propped against the end of it and a hurdle leaning in a corner of the yard that has been there long enough to have gone the colour of the wall behind it.

They went up at the fourth hour of the fourteenth week of a cold in its second winter, and the light in this valley does not do what light does in a county that keeps its hours, and a man from a county that keeps them walks beside two people at a pace that is wrong for both of them and finds out about it twice.

The woman who keeps the house is sixty-eight and has been married into it for forty-eight years and there is a man of the seventh generation in the byre who does not come out, and the woman was the one who came to the door, and she did not ask them in, she stood aside.

“There is nobody under this roof who is going to be told anything by a man off the road,” she said, “and there has not been one in nine years, and you are the first to come up the bank and it is the fourteenth week, so you have chosen a cold week on purpose, and I have a fire and I am sixty-eight and I am going to let you in.”

“Thank you.”

“Do not thank me. I am going to ask you four things and I am not going to help you with any of them.”

---

**And the first of the four things she did not help him with was the mark in the wall, and the girl of about nine found it before either of them did, because the girl is nine and has eyes for nothing else and was in the yard with a barrow she had not gone and got out at the ford.**

“There is a stone in this wall with a thing on it,” she said, from six feet off, and pointed with her whole hand, which is how anybody under ten points at anything over their head. “It is not one of the ones I counted at the Low Holm. It has a bar on it.”

“Show us,” said Osgar Vell.

She took them round the side of the byre to the corner where the wall is oldest and the lime is oldest and the corner has had a roof come off it twice. There are nine stones in that corner with a mark cut on each one, and the mark is a T with a bar across the top of it, cut in the same way in each case by four different people over ninety years, and one of the nine has a stroke through it, cut from the top, and it is the only one of the nine in that corner with a stroke, and it is also the sharpest, which means it is the newest, which means the man it is about has been dead for less time than the others.

“That is a T with a bar on it,” said Aren Kest, “and it is a barred T, and it is a mark. And the stroke through that one is a different mark, and it is the same stroke.”

“It is the same stroke and it is the one in the book,” said Osgar Vell.

“It is the one in the book,” said the woman of the house, from the byre door, and neither of them had said anything to her about the book. “Sit down and eat something and I will tell you about it, because I have been married into this house for forty-eight years and I am the only person in it who has ever said anything about that mark to a person who was not in the family, and I am not going to do it standing up in a yard in the fourteenth week of a cold.”

The story is four generations old and it is the version every family in this valley has of anything, which is that it happened to somebody and it was raining.

The man of the story was the man of the house's elder brother, and he went out of this valley when he was seventeen and his brother was eleven and there was no word for what he was going to do and no count of it and no form, and a hundred and forty years later a man of fifty-eight would find one under a floor four steps down, and the form was not about him and was never about anybody in this house, and the family has been telling this story for three generations and has never once wondered what a form is. He was gone fourteen months. He came back in the warm week with nine beasts that were not this valley's beasts and with a word in the low country's language, and the word was a word for a man who has been on a road and come off it, and the man who had said the word to him had not asked his name and had not wanted to, and had gone on down the valley the same afternoon, and had never come back up it.

“And who said it,” said Aren Kest.

“A stranger with the beasts,” said the woman of the house. “That is what the family says and I have heard it since I was nineteen and I have never had it out of anybody. Not a stranger. Somebody in this valley who had it off a stranger. That is my husband's grandmother's correction, made forty years ago, and nobody has ever been able to say she was wrong, and I married into this house at twenty and I heard both versions inside a month and I have never had it out of anybody since and I am sixty-eight.”

“And the word is the word this valley uses.”

“It is a word,” she said. “I am sixty-eight and I have been in this house forty-eight years and I have never once heard one word used for one thing in this valley. There are eight of them and I could not tell you how many because it depends which house you are in, and the woman at the mill has one and my husband's brother had one and the old man at the upper water has got one that sounds like owing somebody money, and there is not one house in nine miles where two people use the same one, and there is not one household in nine miles where a person of my age and a person of twenty-two would choose the same one out of the eight.”

Nobody said anything for a moment. There was a fire in the room and a wind somewhere in the roof that had been in that roof for a hundred years and had been looked at and not mended.

“That is the finding and it is about the word and not about the wall,” said Aren Kest, “and I am going to say it in four seconds and then I am going to sit down, because a historian of thirty-two with a limp has been walking up a bank at a pace that is wrong for him for a quarter of an hour. Nobody in this valley named you. You have been named it since the day you were born by about nine people who could not have told you where they got it, and the reason they could not tell you is that there are two accounts of where it came from and one of them is in a room under a byre four hundred miles from a county that has an office in it, and the other one is in this yard, and the two of them have never been in the same room and there is no instrument in this kingdom that could put them in one.”

“That is a thing about two accounts,” said Osgar Vell.

“That is a thing about two accounts. I am the only person I have met in four years and a half who could check either of them, and I cannot check a word out of a man's mouth, and I have been telling you that in four words in four rooms since a market day in the ninth week of a cold, and I am not going to do it a fifth time with a different face on.”

“You have told me four times and I have counted them and the fifth one was nine minutes ago, and you have not said it once the same way, and I have written none of them down.” He sat down on the end of a bench that was not the right length for two people. “I will tell you the one I have not written down, and then I want to say something to her and not to you, because you have not asked me anything since the market and you have not asked me anything now, and I have worked out that a man who does not ask is the only kind of man in this valley who is going to be the last one who is doing it.”

**And then he said everything a carrier was, in a room over a byre at the fourth hour of a cold, and it is nine generations old and it is a knife in a yard and it is the texture and not its argument.**

“A carrier was somebody who went out of this valley and came back,” he said, “and it was not given to him by the valley. He was named for it by somebody who had not been asked to name him and would never have known what to call him otherwise, and then his brother put a stroke through a mark in a wall with a knife and a cold chisel off a roof slate, in nine minutes, in a wet week, because a family cannot say a thing like that out loud and a wall will hold it for a hundred and forty years, and a knife in a yard is the only instrument in this volume that anybody has ever used to say a true thing.”

“And your family has held it for fifty-five years,” said the woman of the house, “and the book has held the same stroke for a hundred and forty, and it was cut in this yard by a man who is still alive and is the only person in this house who has ever seen it done, and the one in the book was cut with a pen by somebody in a place I could not walk to in a year, and neither of them was a person who lived here. I have been in this house forty-eight years and I have never once seen that book and I have not asked to see it and I am not going to ask, because I am sixty-eight and by the time I did somebody would have had to decide whether I was allowed, and I have had four daughters-in-law and not one of them has ever asked me a question about anything at all.”

“Was there ever a rule about it.”

“There is not. There is a book and a room and a keeper and there has never once in my lifetime been a rule I could find out about.” She put her hands in her sleeves, which is the gesture of nine women in this valley and is not a thing any of them has agreed on. “I have been in a room in a winter with a man in it and I have been the one holding somebody's hand and I have never once been asked to hold a book, and that is not a complaint either. I am going to say the thing and then I am going to put another stick on.”

“Yes.”

“The thing is that the form on the floor of that room is not about us.” She looked at the man of fifty-eight. “I have known there was a form under that byre since I was a girl, because I have married into the eighth generation and the eighth generation has known it for four generations the way you know a bad step. I have never read it and I am never going to read it, and the reason I have never asked about it is that I have understood something about it that nobody in this house has ever said out loud, and I have never said it out loud because I am the wrong person and I have four daughters-in-law.”

She put a stick on the fire and it went in whole and stood up out of it a hand's width, which is the one thing on that hearth that a person of sixty-eight did without any instruction from anybody.

“It came to us and nobody answered it,” she said. “It has been in that valley since before the first of the nine and it has been in that house since before the first of the nine and nobody has ever sent it back and nobody has ever been asked for it, and I have never in my life known what it was asking for, and I am telling you that I do not know, and I am the wrong person and it is the right thing to say, and if you write a figure on it, whatever figure it is, then I will have been wrong about this for forty-eight years and there is nothing in nine miles that can put me right, and that is not a threat. It is a woman of sixty-eight in a kitchen telling four people what happens in a kitchen.”

Nobody wrote a figure on it.

**And then the young man of twenty-two said the eighth one out loud, because he had come up the bank knowing he was going to and had not told anybody which part, and he had it in his mouth before the woman of the house had put the second stick on.**

“There is no eighth word,” he said. “That is what I have got wrong and it has taken me nine days. There are seven ways of saying it in this valley and then there is a thing on a sheet of paper in a room under a byre, and the thing on the sheet is not a word. It is a description. It says *the number of men going out of the quarter*, and if you want the person instead of the figure then the sheet has another line for him, and that line asks for a name.”

Nobody in that room said anything.

“So there are seven words in nine miles and one description and one request for a name, and the description is the only one of the nine that is true about anybody in this house, and nobody has ever used it, and the reason nobody has ever used it is that it is a description of a thing that happened to a man and not a name for a person, and my mother has spent nineteen years calling me by a name for a person and the name she uses is the name of a thing that happened to her husband's brother in a wet week in this yard about fifty-five years ago, and she has never once asked where he got it, and she is not going to now, and she told me that a minute before she told me the rest of it.” He put his hands in his sleeves. “And that is what the stroke in your wall is for, and I have been in this room a quarter of an hour and I have worked out that the stroke has no other reason anybody here can give for it, and the three of you have all lived here your whole lives, and not one of you can tell me who put the stroke there or what the stroke was for.”

The woman of the house put the second stick on the fire and it went in whole.

“It was cut in about nine minutes in a wet week in this corner of this yard,” she said, “and it was cut with a knife and a cold chisel off a roof slate because there was nothing else in the house, and I was in this house when it happened and I asked one question about it in the same week and I was told *so that it is different*, and I have been in this house forty-eight years and I have never once worked out what anybody meant by different and I have never asked a second question about it, because the answer I got was nine words long and the whole of what I have got out of this house in forty-eight years is nine words at a time.”

“Nobody has ever put the two of them side by side,” said Aren Kest, “and I have now done it in nine seconds with a young man of twenty-two in a room with a fire in it, and I would like it noticed that I did not do it and that I have said so in the room.”

“You did it in your head about nine seconds ago and then you waited for him to do it, which is a different thing and is the only way a man of your trade gets a thing said out loud in a room like this,” said the woman of the house. “And you are wrong about one half of it and right about the other, and I am going to say which is which and then I am going to sit down, because I am sixty-eight and I have been standing in my own kitchen an hour and a half talking to three men about a stroke in a wall and that is the longest conversation I have had in this house since my husband died.

“The right half is that nobody has ever put them side by side. The wrong half is that you said nobody in this house can tell you who put the stroke there, and I can. I have known who put that stroke there since I was about twenty and I have told nobody, and I am telling it to a young man of twenty-two who says eight words for a thing, and the person who put the stroke there is sitting at the table by the fire with a cup in his hand and he has not said anything for an hour, and he is seventy-four, and he has been in this house since I was twenty, and he is the reason I stayed in it, and I would like him to have been asked, and I would like it noticed that I have been asking people questions about things for about nine days and it has taken me forty-eight years to get to this table and I got here because a man of thirty-two asked me four things at a door and I am not going to pretend that is not the reason.”

Nobody looked at the table. The man by the fire had a cup in his hand and had not said anything since the two of them came up the bank, and he put the cup down on the table and did not look up.

“I was nineteen,” he said. “And I had a brother who went out of this valley and came back, and I cut a stroke through his mark in a wall with a cold chisel off a roof slate in nine minutes in a wet week, and I did it because I did not know what else to do with a thing I could not say out loud, and I have been seventy-four for about a year and I have never told anybody that and I have never said why.

“And a man of fifty-eight who keeps a book has spent nineteen years not saying why, and a man of thirty-two has spent thirteen weeks not asking, and the two of you are the first two people in this valley who have ever been in the same room as a man who did not know what else to do with a thing. There were three of us that week. I was the youngest, and my brother was home, and the two people who have cut a stroke in a wall in that valley in a hundred and forty years had between them not one idea.”

---

**And the girl of about nine went out to the corner of the byre again and counted the marks on the stones, and it took her four minutes, and she came back in and stood in the middle of the room and did not say anything, and Osgar Vell waited.**

“There are nine stones with a mark on them,” she said, “and eight of them have got the bar and one of them has got the bar and the other thing, and the one with both of them is the newest and it is the only one you can tell has not been there as long as the others, and there is a tenth one at the corner by the gate but it has not got a bar on it at all, it has got a T and nothing else, and I have counted it four times and I have not asked anybody about it because then I will have to be told, and I have counted the steps on the outside stair at the Low Holm as well and that is nine and the fourth one is the only one that talks.”

“That is a T with a bar on it and a T with nothing on it,” said Aren Kest, “and there are two hundred of the second one in the ninth spread, and the first one is everything nine generations of this valley have believed about itself, and you have got both of them in a corner of a yard in four minutes, and I am not going to put them anywhere near each other, and you have.”

And the man of fifty-eight got up off the bench he was on, and did not say the thing he had come up the bank to say, and went out first, and stood in the yard in the light that does not do what light does, and looked at the barrow wheel propped against the end of the byre, which has no use in it and has not had a use in it since before the man of eighty-one was forty, and the girl of about nine came past him with her barrow and he stepped out of the way of a barrow with a man in his hand in a valley with no road in it, and neither of them said anything, and that is the arrangement, and it had survived four months.
