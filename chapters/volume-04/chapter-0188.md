# Chapter 0188

**The fourth of Harvestmonth, YR 314, a Saturday, was a market day in that county for about four hours and a Saturday sitting for about five, and the bench of nine sat in a long room over a wharf with the tide under it, and a man of fifty-five who had come up two flights of a stair with a waggon-book in his coat and a figure in it asked the bench of nine to fund a road to a valley four hundred miles off, and the bench said no, and the no was correct, and the reason it gave was eleven words long.**

And it was not the fifth asking. The fifth asking is in this county's minute already and its answer is in the minute too, and the number of times this bench has been asked the same thing is five and the number of yeses is four, and both of those figures are on a page under a stair in this hall where anybody coming up may read them. This was a sixth thing and it was about a different subject, and the clerk of the bench entered it as the sixth, and no person in that room called it the fifth, and the reason nobody called it the fifth is that the fifth was about a crossing four hundred yards up this town's own road and this one is about a county that is not on any map, and the man who read the second out loud said so himself before anybody else could.

The man who came up two flights was a carrier of about fifty with a waggon of his own and a firm of two and a rate on about a hundred and forty miles of haulage in four counties, and he had been up the low country twice in eleven weeks, and on the second of those he had gone nine days off a county road on a hired horse and come back, and he had been to a valley.

He did not put a map on the table because he had none and he said so before anybody asked. He put a page of a waggon-book on the table with a figure on it and the addition done out, and the addition was done out because his own firm does it that way and because he had been told by two men in the same week that if there is one thing this bench will take seriously it is arithmetic, and because he wanted the figure to be the first thing in the room and not the fifth.

And then he told the room what he had seen, because the bench-clerk had asked him to and because a man of about sixty who keeps a minute for a bench that is not a bench had never in twenty-two years been able to put a thing in a minute that he had not heard said out loud in front of him, and it took about eleven minutes, and about four of the nine did not believe any of it and one of the nine wrote it down word for word, which is not what a minute is.

“There is no road in it,” he said. “I have been on a road in every county you can name and I have been in a valley with no road in it and I did not understand it for about a day. I got off the horse at the top of the pass because there was nowhere for the horse to go, and I walked nine days, and the first two of them I kept stopping, and I want to say why, and it is because I kept looking for a gate.

“There is not a gate. There is not a wall. There is not a road and there is not a cart and there has not been a cart in nine generations, and there is a man there of thirty-four with sixteen men who can build a road and have had no work since he was twenty, and he has asked that valley twice for a road and been told twice that there is no road to build, and he told me himself that he worked out in about a week that no council had ever decided not to build one, because four hundred people had never once wanted to be anywhere.

“And he has built four hundred yards of ground from a gate down to a ford in nine weeks, and the only use it has is a barrow, and he has said so in a yard in front of nine people, and about eleven of the people in that valley have said out loud that it is the best ground anybody has ever seen there and that it is not a road.

“They have put a stand across four feet of a beck in four days. That is the first bridge in that valley in nine generations and it is about four feet long and it has a nail in the coping, and the nail is there because a man who makes nails put it in, and a man of thirty-four told me that a nail man is the first customer a nail has ever had in that valley and that he is not going to charge him for it.

“There is a mill at the bottom with a wheel on it that has not turned in nine years and came off four weeks ago, and a woman with a yoke who crosses a stand four times a day and used to put a pail in four feet of water. And there is a room over a cross with twenty-nine benches bolted to the floor in four rows and a number cut into the end of each one and a hundred and forty cut into the end of the one at the back, and nobody has ever been struck off one of them, and there is a fire in that room about forty times a year and nine people turn up to it, and I have been in about nine hundred rooms in this county and eleven weeks of them, and that is the one I have not stopped thinking about.”

He put his hand flat on the waggon-book.

“And the thing I have not been able to get round, and it is the reason I am standing in this hall, is that nobody in that valley has ever been asked anything by anybody, in nine generations, and they have a book under a byre with about two hundred of them in it from the day they were born, and there is no form in that valley on which to enter any of it, and the man who keeps that book is fifty-eight and has kept it nineteen years and will not let it out of the room. And they asked about two hundred of them at nine hundred doors in eleven days in the winter before last, on foot, because there is nothing in that valley that can ask anybody, and a hundred and eighty-nine of them stood to it and three households said no, and nobody wrote any of it down, and there is a figure of four hundred on a page at the bottom of that book in a hand that varies and no name against any of it.”

The room over the wharf was quiet for about four seconds.

“I am not asking this bench to like any of that,” he said. “I am asking it for thirty-one miles of road and a turning ground, and I would like the figure in the book and I would like the account of the valley that goes with it in the same hand, and if the minute of this bench can hold a page of arithmetic and a page of a valley nine miles long, then it is a minute. If it cannot, then it is a subscription book with a clerk in it and I am asking the wrong body and I would like to be told so and go down the stair.”

**“A road from the end of the county road at the low-country end to the head of the pass, thirty-one miles, and a piece of made ground at the top of it about two hundred yards across for turning a waggon. That is all of what I am asking for. Not the road into the valley. **Not one yard of anything inside the valley, and not one penny of it spent there, and not one person in it asked for anything or agreeing to anything or being told about it.** The head of the pass and a turning ground and the county road, and the county road is in your county and the top of the pass is in another one and the nine days between is not mine.

“I am asking this bench for it because a bench that keeps a road is a bench that knows what a road costs, and this hall keeps a road that has had no glass in its sill for two generations and two marks in a chest since the ninth of Fallowmonth, and I would rather be told no by nine men who know that than yes by nine men who do not.”

**He read the figure out, and he read the addition out, and the addition was on the page in front of him and the page was on the table before he opened his mouth, which is a thing a carrier does and not a thing a petitioner does.**

*Six men, thirty-one days, at fourpence the day.* 4, six times over, is 24. 24, thirty-one times over, is **744**.

*Two hundred feet of plank for a culvert at the beck, at three pence the foot.* 3, two hundred times over, is **600**.

*Forty pounds of powder at three pence the pound.* 3, forty times over, is **120**.

*A mason, four days, at a shilling the day.* 12, four times over, is **48**.

*Cartage of the tools out and back, four loads each way, at eighteen pence a load.* 18, four times over, is 72. 72, twice over, is **144**.

*A waggon and four horses from the county road to the head of the pass and back, at eleven shillings the turn.* 11 shillings is 132 pence. 132, twice over, is **264**.

“Six hundred and forty-four is the fifth line and there is nothing else in it, and I have left off nothing, and a man who leaves something off a figure is a man who has come up two flights to be asked a question he already knows the answer to.

“Six and six: 744 and 600 is 1,344. 1,344 and 120 is 1,464. 1,464 and 48 is 1,512. 1,512 and 144 is 1,656. 1,656 and 264 is 1,920.

**One thousand nine hundred and twenty pence, which is forty marks, and it is forty marks, and I have had two of them sent to this town on account of work and one of them came back.**”

---

**And then the reason the bench gave was said by the man in the chair, and it was eleven words long, and it is the shortest thing that has ever decided anything in that hall, and it is the reason the no is correct.**

“There are two marks in the chest and the ask is forty.”

Nobody in that room argued with it for nine seconds, and the nine seconds were the shortest of the sitting, and about four of the nine afterwards said in about nine years that those nine seconds were the only time that hall had ever decided anything.

The carrier put his hand flat on the waggon-book.

**“That is correct,” he said. “I have come two hundred miles to be told it and I am not going to argue with it, and I would like it entered that I said so before I said anything else, because I have been in about nine rooms in eleven weeks about money and in eight of them the money was the argument and in one it was not, and I would like the one where it was not to be the last I remember.”**

And then the reeve said the half of it that was in her own mouth, and she did not vote on it and has not voted on the guild's own matter in nine years and did not vote on it, and she was there for about eleven minutes and a third of what she said was the reason the no is the right no and not the easy one.

“Then it is a no, and I am glad it is a no, and I want the minute to carry that I said that, and I want the man who asked to have the reason twice, because a bench that says no for money has refused a thing it could not afford, and a bench that says no for anything else has refused a thing it does not want, and this county has never in nine years got the first and I am not going to let it put the second down in a book under a stair and have it read in nine years as the first.

**“There is a fair copy nailed to a board under that stair that anybody coming up may read, and it is a county's own account, four hundred and eight words and a working on a second sheet, and the first of its six terms is that a road is not to be made to carry a person. Read it. I have read it standing on that stair at the same rate this carrier has, and it is the only document in this county in nine years that anybody has put where anybody can get at it, and it came up here on a barge because a man of twenty-seven stood in the rain and read it and asked for a copy, and nobody had to be asked.

“And the reason this bench is the wrong body for this ask is on that board and not in anybody's head, and it is that a road that is not to be made to carry a person does not get built in a county where nobody has asked for it, and this one has not got a person at the end of it to be carried, and there is nobody at the far end of that pass to be hurt if we do not, and there is nobody at the far end of that pass to be helped if we do.

“And I have said the word *and* in that sentence twice and the second one is the argument and the first one is habit, and I would like the minute to carry that I said it twice, and I would like whoever reads it in nine years to notice that the habit was first and the argument was second, and that I got there by saying the wrong one first, and that a reeve of this town has been saying the wrong one first for nine years and it is the only thing in her office she is good at.”

Nobody in that room said anything about the second one being habit, and four of the nine had known it was habit before she said it, and one of those four is a man of forty-seven on the roll with a vote and not an office who has not used the vote in nine years and did not use it then and has said in this hall nothing about that and is not going to start.

---

**And then a magistrate of thirty who had come up about nine minutes before the carrier and had sat down at the end of the long table asked the one question nobody in the room had asked, and she is the reeve's sister and has been in nine years and has beaten two counts of persons with the form's own construction and is frightened of her own answer.**

“Then what is the road for.”

Nobody said anything for eleven seconds.

“That is not a hostile question and I would like it minuted that I asked it before I voted and not after, and I have voted and I voted with the bench and I am not going to pretend I have a difficulty that I have not got.” Lysa Fenn had her hands flat on the table. “A carrier has come two hundred miles with a figure in a book and has described a road that does not go into a valley and does not spend a penny in one and does not ask anybody in one, and that is the most careful proposal I have heard in nine years, and I have spent nine years being careful, and I am telling this bench that carefulness is not the same thing as there being a reason, and that I have known that for nine years and I have not said it in this hall because there has never been anything in this hall that needed it.

“If we fund it, then in nine years there is a road to the head of a pass, and at the bottom of the pass is a valley with about four hundred people in it that this county has no roll of and no writ for and no name for, and somebody from this county will go down that road. Not to carry anything. There is nothing to carry, the carrier has said so. Somebody will go down it, in about nine years, because a road is a thing people go down, and this county has no office that will be told about it and no minute it will be put in and no way at all of stopping it, because the only thing this bench does is decide things and nobody has ever asked it to decide a thing that was going to happen anyway.”

She took her hands off the table.

“That is not a reason for yes and it is a reason for the minute. Put the reason in the minute.”

And then the carrier asked the one question he had come two hundred miles to ask, and he asked it at the door of the hall on the stair, and the answer is in the minute because the clerk of the bench entered everything that was said on a stair on that day, and has entered about nine times as much as the standing orders require and cannot be got to stop.

“Who asked you to ask it.”

Nobody said anything for four seconds.

“**A man who makes nails,** said the carrier. “Nine days down the low country and he had a stall at a market and he would not sell me anything and he talked for about a quarter of an hour and then he asked me what a road cost, and I told him, and I told him what a waggon and four horses cost to the top of a pass and back, and he wrote it on the back of a barrow with a bit of chalk, and he is a nail man and he is the first man I have met in nine days who has said out loud that he wanted to be somewhere else, and I would like to know whether that is a trade of yours or a thing in a valley, and I would like an answer, and I will take no.”

The clerk entered it. The carrier went down the stair and out into a market day that had about four hours of it left in it, and there is a board under that stair with a county's account on it that anybody coming up may read, and there is nothing on that board about a valley nine days off a pass, and there is a man of thirty-two who went out of this county on the eighth of a month this county keeps at the fourth hour and who has not been heard from and who was not in this hall and who is not in this chapter, and the distance between this town and the place he is in is about four hundred miles and there is nobody in this county who knows he is in a valley at all, and the reeve of this town has four names in a subscription book at a market four times a year and two of them are the same man twice and she knows the figure and the man does not.
