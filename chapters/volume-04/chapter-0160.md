# Chapter 0160

The ninth week of a winter in its ninth generation begins on a morning when the Beck comes up, which the man of eighty-one said would happen in the fourth week and which happened in the ninth, and the whole valley treated that as the man being wrong and nothing else, and he is eighty-one and has been wrong about the water before.

Aren Kest went out at first light because he has gone out at first light every morning since he came and because a leg is better at the top of a morning than at the bottom of one, and he went as far as the ford and back, which is about two miles, and the ice on the edges of the Burn was thin and grey and there was water under it doing four feet a second.

Coming back up the bank he put his right hand flat on the lintel over the door of the room over the byre, the way he has done about forty times, because a lintel is a dressed stone four feet long with nothing on it and a man cannot help touching a thing like that.

> *You are expected.*

He did not write it down.

He has had four of those in four years and has written one of them down, and the one he wrote down has no hour against it and no name and he cannot account for where it came from and nobody four hundred miles off can tell him, and this one arrived while his hand was on a stone in a valley that is not on any map, and there is nobody in this valley he could ask, and he did not ask the woman over the byre, who was in the yard with a yoke on at six in the morning, and he did not ask the girl at the gate, who was not up yet, and he did not write it in the notebook, and that is three not-doings on one morning and none of them was a decision and all of them were.

---

**And the woman of about sixty-six said the word at about the fourth hour of that morning without having decided to, and then did not use his name for the rest of the day.**

She is at the weaving house two steadings down the bank from the Low Holm, and she has been at that trade for fifty-one years, and there are four looms in a room the size of a cart and there is nobody in this valley under forty who can set up a warp, and her name is not used in this valley except by three people and none of them are her grandchildren, because her grandchildren call her the old woman with the looms, which is what she tells them to call her, and it has been a name for about thirty years and she likes it.

She was at the gate of the weaving house when he came past with the stick, and she was holding a warp-weighted bar in one hand, and she looked at the leg.

“You are the carrier off the road,” she said.

He stopped.

It came out the way a word comes out of a person who has been saying it all her life and has never once had to think about which of three things she meant. She had not decided to say it. He could see that she had not decided to say it, because the sentence it was in the middle of had been about something else entirely, and the word had gone into a gap in it, and a person says what goes in a gap.

“You are the carrier off the road,” she said, “and you are going slower than a carrier should go, and if you have come four days on that leg you should be sitting down at the Low Holm and not the whole bank, and the woman over there shuts her gate with her hip and has done since she was nine, and you should tell her to sit down, and she will not, so tell her anyway.”

“I am not a carrier,” said Aren Kest.

He said it before he knew what the word meant. That is on the page and it was said in about a second and a half and it was the first thing he had said in six weeks that he had not thought about first, and he heard himself say it and could not have said afterwards what he had heard.

The woman looked at him.

“Right,” she said. “Then I will not say it again and I will not call you that. And I am not going to ask you what you are, because you are somebody who has come a long way on a bad leg and has not asked me a single question in six weeks, which is more than the last four men off this road managed between them, and a man who does not ask is either polite or frightened and I do not need to know which to shut my gate.”

She went in. The door was not shut behind her. It is never shut behind a man who is going past in a valley that has not had a man off the road since before the oldest living person was born.

He stood at the gate for about a minute with the stick in his hand and did not ask.

That is the thing on the page and it is a small thing and it is not going to be small by the end of the day, and the reason it is not going to be small is that a man who has a word used about him and does not ask what it means has done the same thing as every family in this valley has done to about two hundred people, except that he has done it in a day rather than in a generation, and he did it with a word he had never heard, and nobody asked him a single question and nobody entered anything and nothing at all has happened yet.

**He did not know what it meant. He had heard it eleven times in nine weeks, in a doorway, in an argument about a gate, and every other time it had been about a person and not about him, and the not-asking is not a thing he decided. It is a thing he did, and then, about a quarter of an hour later, he noticed that he had done it, and noticing it afterwards is not the same as not having done it, and that is the whole of what he has learned about this valley in nine weeks and it has cost him nothing and it has cost him a day.**

---

**And by the sixth hour of that morning the word had become a name for him, and no form in this valley has a column for it.**

It was the girl at the gate first, coming down with the barrow, who said to the woman of the Low Holm, without stopping, that the carrier was going up to the mill, and the woman of the Low Holm said, “He says he is not,” and the girl said, “He told me the same on the day I first saw him,” and went on.

Then the man of about thirty-four came down from the room over the cross and said to a man with a barrow, “The carrier has been at my floor again,” and the man with the barrow said, “What carrier,” and he said, “The one off the road,” and did not stop either.

Then the woman of about sixty-six, at her gate, to a neighbour, said, “The carrier off the road is the one with the stick,” and the neighbour said, “I had him down as a stone-cutter,” and she said, “He is not a stone-cutter, I have watched him shut a gate for four hours.”

By the sixth hour of the ninth week of the ninth generation of a winter, there were four people in this valley using a word about a man that none of them had heard before the fourth hour of that morning, and not one of them had asked him anything, and none of them would, because the word is not a question and a word is the cheapest thing in a valley to give away.

There is no form in this valley. There is no book with a column for it, and no bench, and no clerk, and no writ, and no bell, and no office, and the only roll in this valley is on a board on trestles in a room over a byre and its columns are the day and the mark and the hand, and a name in a fifth column on the older spreads, and **there is nothing in it for a word somebody said at a gate**, and that is not an oversight. That is the design. A roll of this valley is not a list of anybody to be struck and it is not a form and there is no form on which to enter a danger, and that is why it has gone on for nine generations without anybody standing up in a cold room to say what happens to a person about whom a thing has been said in a valley like this.

In about nine years a man who has never been to this valley will find a woman in the market who will remember that a stranger came up the bank in a winter and that somebody called him a word, and she will not be able to say which word, and nobody will be able to tell her, and there is no instrument in this kingdom that could go and look.

---

**And on the same day he went to the market, which is a wide place in the middle of the valley with a cross and a smithy and a room over the cross, and there is no made road in this valley to bring anybody to it, and the market is on a day that is a market day.**

He had been up there twice in forty days and had not understood either time that the thing he was looking at was the absence of carts. There is no cart in the market. There is a barrow and there are three sledges on hard ground under the cross and one of them belongs to a man who is seventy and uses it for wool, and there is a handcart, and that is the whole of it, in a valley about nine miles long and four hundred people in it.

The wool was there. Barley was there, and a salt, and a great deal of one kind of cheese, and about nine pairs of gloves, and a man who makes nails and cannot sell them because there is nothing in this valley to put nails in, and he was there anyway, and he had been there the last four market days, and nobody had told him to stop.

“Eleven of the ninth went over the pass on the fourth day of the cold,” said the man who makes nails, without being asked, because nobody in this valley has ever in forty days asked him one question and he had got to the point of offering. “Eleven the winter before, and ten the one before that, and I keep a tally on the back of the stall because there is nowhere else to keep a tally and my wife says it is not a market day for a nail man.

“They go on the fourth day of the cold and they go in a line and they carry what they can carry and it takes a week to the top of the pass and then they are gone, and about four of them come back in the first warm week and the other seven are somewhere else. And I will tell you the thing that I have never been able to say out loud to anybody in this valley, and it is that a nail man does not care one bit, and that is why I am the one who says it and the rest of you do not.

“A man who has not been anywhere in nine years and has a stall in a market knows exactly what a road is for, and he knows it is not for him, and he has never once been in a room where anybody argued about it, and the reason is that there is nothing in the room to argue about. There is no road. There is a line of men walking over a pass in the cold with what they can carry, and that is a road, and it is nine generations old, and it costs about nothing, and it is the best thing that has ever happened to this valley, and about eleven of the two hundred people in it are the ones who pay for it by not being here, and they are the ones nobody argues about.”

“Where do they go.”

“I do not know,” said the man who makes nails. “You would have to ask somebody who has been, and about four of them come back and about seven do not, and the four who come back will not say, and I have asked two of them and the second one asked me not to ask him again, and I have not asked anybody since.”

He put the tally back the other way up on the back of the stall, which is a thing a man does when he has said more than he meant to.

“Your leg is not that bad,” he said. “I have watched you come up this bank four times this month and you have got a stick and you are using it wrong. You are putting the weight through the wrong hip. You want to put it through the rail and the rail is your leg, and a man who goes out over a pass for a week wants a leg that is somebody else's problem on the fourth day.”

He got the stall right for the afternoon, and the barrow went out at the ford, and the sledges under the cross had their chocks put under them, and there is no made road in this valley and there are eleven men going over a pass in the cold in about a month, and no one in this valley has ever been asked whether that is what the winter is for.

---

**And he went up the outside stair at about the seventh hour of the same morning with the ninth question of a road in a notebook in his coat, and Iven Sarr had the book open on the trestles with his hand flat on it, and the man of fifty-eight had been waiting about an hour to say a thing and had not said it, and he said it now.**

“You have been at that lintel every morning since you came,” he said. “Forty days, near enough, and my wife's grandmother cut that stone and there is not one mark on it, and you have not once asked me what the room is for, and I have been waiting to be asked every one of those forty days, and I am not going to be able to get through the rest of it if you do not ask me one question that is not about the book.”

“I have asked you two,” said Aren Kest.

“You have asked me two about the spreads. That is not a question that is not about the book. That is the book with a question in front of it.” He turned his hand over and laid it flat the other way. “Ask me what I do all day.”

“What do you do all day.”

“Ten hours,” said Iven Sarr. “I put the day against a line. I do not read the book, I do not check it, and I have not checked a hand in nineteen years, and the mark against a line is mine and the day against a line is mine and the hand against a line is not. That is what a man of fifty-eight is for in a valley like this. You want to know whether I have wanted the book read for six years because I do not trust the entries, and I do not trust the entries, and that is not why.

“Nobody has ever asked me whether it is wanted. You have been here forty days and you did not ask an old man of eighty-one that, and I would have told you if you had, and he would have told you the rest of it as well and I would have had to sit here and be told you were told, and I am not going to be able to be told that by you, which is why I have not told you to go and ask him.”

He squared the book on the trestles with both hands.

“And you may as well know what you have not asked, since you have not asked it. Everybody who has ever read this book has read it to see something in it. Four readings in nine generations. Two of them were mine and one of them lasted a night. And the word for a man who has read it and got nothing out of it that he was willing to write down has not been made yet, because nobody here has ever had to make it.”

He shut the book, which he does not do in front of a person, and put his hand flat on the shut cover.

“Now go and do the four men’s work in the room over the cross, and do not come back up this stair tonight, and if you come up tomorrow I will tell you the fifth thing, and it is the last thing I have got, and I have been keeping it for a man who asked.”

That is the end of forty days, and the woman of about sixty-six went on not using his name, and the girl at the gate had a barrow to get out at the ford, and the man of thirty-four was waiting for four men in a room over a cross who had not had a job in this valley since before he was twenty, and the Burn went over its stones at four feet a second with thin grey ice on the edges of it, and the lintel over the door of the room over the byre had nothing on it at all, and there was a word in this valley that he had not asked about and had not asked about in a place where a man who asks is the ordinary thing to do.
