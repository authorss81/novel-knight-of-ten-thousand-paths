# Chapter 0085

Garrick Dene has swept the coping of the second span of the Ninebridge for forty-one years and there has never been anything over it, and the reason he is the only man in the County of Ash who has ever noticed is that he is the only man in the County of Ash who sweeps.

He is sixty-four. He came into this county at twenty-three with a broommaker's certificate his master's name on it and a place on a bridge gang at Kilnholt, and he has swept nine bridges in his working life and four of them were in counties that have words for things this county has not got, and he is not a proud man and he is not an unhappy one, and the thing he wants out of the eighth month of the year in which a stranger is living in this county is to be allowed to lift a thing off a coping and sweep under it and put it back.

Not to take it away. To lift it.

"There is a difference and it is the whole of what I have come to say," he said. "You take a thing off a coping you have thrown it away. You lift a thing off a coping you have done your job. Forty-one years I have done my job round it and not under it, and there is not one thing wrong with the road and there is not one stone moved and no water stands on that span in a wet morning any more than it did when I came here, and I would like to go down on my knees on that coping with a broom in my hand and do the eight inches under it, and I cannot, because it is not mine to lift and the coping is the county's."

---

The eighth month is the month the Kiln comes back up, and the coping of the second span is the driest place on the Ninebridge, and the sweeping is done before the first hour and it takes a man about two hours a span on a good day.

"You stand on the dry side because the wet side has a parapet worth a man's height and the dry side has a coping you can walk," he said. "That is the whole of the technique and there is nothing to learn. I have taught it to four men and two of them are dead and one of them is the kiln-hand at Kilnholt and one of them went off to a coast. The thing about a coping is that everything that is going to hurt you arrives on it from about four feet away and never from underneath, because the underneath is the only part of a bridge that is not the bridge's fault. Everything that is going to hurt you on a bridge arrives in from the weather or from a wagon. Nothing arrives out of the structure. **A structure that has been built by anybody who can be asked about does not come up and get you.**"

He said it the way he said everything, which is the way a man says a thing he has been saying for forty years and has never once had anybody argue with.

---

"There is no bell in this county," he said, "and there has never been one."

He said it at about the fifth hour on the dry side of the second span with a broom in his hands, to a man who had come up the steps with two boards under his arm, and he had not been asked about it, and he had been waiting about nine months to say it to somebody who would hear it.

"Nine spans and not one bell on any of them, and not a bell-cote, and not an iron stock, and not a staple, and not a hole in the parapet where one used to be. I have swept under that place four times a day for forty-one years. There is nothing over it. There has never been anything over it, and there is not a mark on the stone of anything having been over it, and I want you to understand what that means, because it does not mean it fell down. **When a bell is taken off a bridge there is a mark.** I have taken the marks off four bridges in my working life. There is a socket scar, or there are two bolt holes and a paler rectangle of stone where the soot stood, and you cannot sweep a place clean for a year and you do not need to because you can see it from the road, and every man in the county sees it and they all say nothing because there is a reason and the reason is a man in a coat."

He put the broom down.

"There is no mark. That is the whole of what I have to say about it. There is no bell in this county and there has never been one and there is nothing to say about it, and I am sixty-four and I am the only man alive who has swept the place where it would have hung, and about a hundred and forty people cross that span on a market morning and not one of them has ever looked at the wall above the coping on the dry side, and I have swept it four times a day for forty-one years, and they are not looking because there is nothing to look at."

He picked the broom up.

"**That is what it is like to sweep a bridge in a county that has never had a bell on it.** You get used to it. You do not stop noticing. You just stop having anybody to tell."

---

"I came off a bridge at —— , and I will not tell you where, because there is a woman there who has swept longer than I have and she does not need a stranger from a clay county."

He said the shape of it and left the name out and did not notice himself doing it, which is a thing men of his trade do and which the man with the boards did notice and did not say anything about, for the reason that is going to cost him one sentence in about a page.

"There was a bell on the middle span. It went for a fire and it went for a death and it went for a refusal, and I want to say that last one properly because I am going to say something about this county and I am not going to do it by pretending there is nothing here.

"**A bell went when a town said no.** Not a no to a road. A no. There was a bridge and a crew and a bench, and a man came with a paper, and the bench read it out in a hall and the hall said no, and about a week after that the bell went on the middle span and it rang once and it was a long stroke, and a man came out of the toll-house in his coat and made a minute of it, and the minute is in a book. I have never seen the book. I have been told there is a book and I have believed the man who told me and I have not asked to see it, because a book is a thing you are shown and I am forty-one years on a bridge and I do not need to be shown a book to know a bell went for a refusal."

He turned the broom over.

"So a county that has had that has a word for it. This county has got a word too, because every county has got the words for everything, it is only that some of them have been used. **And the word this county has for a thing that was asked for and not given is the word for a thing that got lost.** It is one word for both. I have worked it out over about nine years and I have never once had anybody to say it to, and I have said it to two men, and one of them said *I did not know that* and the other one said *that is not a thing anybody can do anything about*, and both of them were right, and they are the two most useful men in this county and one of them is standing on this bridge with two boards in his arms at about the fifth hour of the eighth month because he came off a road."

---

He looked at the hand of the Ninebridge, who was eleven spans away with a roll of tools at her hip working a kerb, and he did not lower his voice and he did not raise it, which is a thing a man of sixty-four does on a bridge.

"They call her the hand and I have heard the word and I have never once heard anybody explain it, and I have been working a bridge with that woman for nine years, and I will say what I think it means, and you can carry it to her or not.

"Where I came from there is a word for the man who is answerable for a bridge. The word has a duty inside it. It does not mean he is proud of it and it does not mean anybody has asked him anything. It means **there is a thing about that bridge that somebody is entitled to ask him.** It is a word with a question in it. That is the whole of the difference between a word and no word.

"And I have stood on that coping for nine years and I have watched a road be kept in this county and I have worked out what the word *the hand* is for, and it is for this: **there has not been one single thing on this bridge in nine years that anybody in this county could have asked anybody about.** The road is a road. The kerb is a kerb. There is not a writ in this county, there is not a schedule, there is not a Crown's man, there is no dispute about this bridge because there is nobody to have a dispute with. So there is nothing to answer for, so there is nobody to be answerable, so you cannot have the word, so you have got a good word instead, and the good word means a hand, and a hand is a thing that holds on and a hand is not a thing that answers.

**A man who is answerable for a thing that cannot be asked about is not a man. He is a shape. And they have got the right word for her and it is the right word, and I have been trying for nine years to work out why it is the right word and it is because there is not another one to use.**"

He stopped, and looked at the boards under the man's arm, and then he went and swept about nine feet of coping without saying anything, because a man who has made a speech on a bridge is a man who then sweeps.

---

"And that is the long wind out of me," he said, at the end of it. "You did not ask me one question and I have given you about eleven minutes and there was nobody on this bridge but a man with two boards, and that is not a complaint, because I have been waiting nine months and the only man in this county with the trade to understand a word of it is a stranger who has been here eight months and is not staying. So now you have got it and I have got nothing out of it, and the arrangement is fair."

Then he asked, which he had not planned to and which surprised him.

"I want to ask you one, since you are the only man on this bridge who has ever heard the word *mopper* and not asked me what it is."

"Go on."

"**Can I lift it.**"

---

The thing on the coping of the second span is six inches by four and about the thickness of two fingers and it is fired grey clay, and it has a rope on it hanging in two loops with no yoke on either loop, and the rope is about a year old and the clay is not a year old, and it is on the dry side of a working coping on a bridge of nine spans in a county of nine hundred and forty-one hearths, and it has been there for the whole of Garrick Dene's working life and before.

He has called it the old mark. He is the only person in the world who has a name for it and the name is a workman's name and it is not going to be written down anywhere.

"There is a stamp on the face of it," he said. "I do not know what it is a stamp of. It is not a stamp off any die in this county. I have swept around about forty pressed tiles in my working life and I would know a Kilnholt mark or a Ninebridge mark or a north-village mark in the dark, and it is not one of those, and it is not a mill mark, and it is not a guild mark, because this county does not have a guild that presses tiles. So it is a stamp off something this county has not got, and I have known that for about thirty years, and I have never told anybody, because there is nobody to tell and because I would not have known what I was telling them."

"Is there a reason it's here?"

"I have asked. I asked when I was about thirty and I asked a man who was about seventy and I asked him twice over about two years, and he told me the first time that his grandfather put it there, and the second time he said he did not know, and the man is dead. **That is the whole of what there is.** I have had forty-one years of sweeping and there is a thing on my coping that a dead man's grandfather may or may not have put there, and I have not been frightened of it once, and I am going to tell you why, and then you can do what you like with it."

He put the broom against the parapet.

"Fear is what you do with a thing you think somebody is going to ask you about. **A thing you have to work round every day for forty-one years is not a frightening thing. It is a shape.** I have had a stone on the shoulder of that span since I came here that the county put there in my first year, and it is not straight, and it is four inches proud, and it has been in everybody's way since and everybody walks round it, and I have never once thought *that stone might mean something*. I think about that stone about four times a year and every time I think *somebody ought to come and knock that off and put it in true.* That is the whole of my feeling about the old mark. I want it done and I want to do it and I am not able to, and I have wanted it done since I was thirty."

---

The man with the boards put them down on the dry side of the coping, which is not a thing anybody does, and knelt, and looked at it from about eleven inches, and did not touch it, and was quiet for about nine seconds, and the whole of the Ninebridge was quiet, and the Kiln made almost no noise.

**"I don't know," said Aren Kest.**

And the hand of the Ninebridge, who had been eleven spans away with a roll of tools at her hip and who has told nobody in this county what she was told in the ninth month of a previous year by a man with a sett-cutter's bag, would have given about a year of what a man in this county can earn to hear him say that sentence a third time in a place where a person could hear him.

"It is in that book of yours," said Garrick Dene. "I do not know what book. I do not know where a man off a road keeps one, and I would not look if I did. But it is in there, and you have just told a man with a broom on a bridge that you do not know, and you have told him on purpose, and you did not have to."

"No," said Aren Kest.

"Right," said the man with the boards, and picked his boards up.

And that was the sentence. There is not a great deal to it and it cost more than anything else he did in this county, and the price of it is this, and he wrote it down that night in the flat hand at the back of the notebook and it is the last thing in the third notebook entry and he did not add to it:

*In the eleventh month she asked me what it was and I said I did not know and she said Good, and in the twentieth day I told her it was a good tile and she knew I was lying and she thanked me for the answer and said she would like it to go on being the whole of it. That was a protection. A man who does not know a thing cannot be asked what he has found out.*

*Today a man of sixty-four who has swept round that mark for forty-one years asked me one question and I answered it truthfully, and I am going to be a man who has said I do not know about a thing three times in one county, in front of three people, and three people in one county is a county, and in nine years the question is going to be what a man off the road said on the coping of the second span, and there will be two answers, and one of them is a lie.*

*She gave me that in the eleventh month. **A thing said out loud and believed, and believed wrongly, is a debt and not a release.***

*I have said the true thing to a third person and I have not said the other thing to anybody, and the two of those are going to be a pair, and I have bought a protection with a debt and I do not know the rate.*

Then the notebook, and then the bottom of the rack, and the eighth month went on, and on the coping of the second span of the Ninebridge the old mark stayed on the dry side with a year's rope on it, and the eight inches under it were not swept that day, and Garrick Dene had asked a man off a road one question in nine months and had got a straight answer and had made no complaint about it and did not expect one.
