# Chapter 0054

The reeve called it on the fourth of Embermonth, which was a Saturday and a market day, and she called it in the long room over the wharf because a bench that cannot meet about a bridge in a room can meet about it in a room with forty people in it who are not a bench.

That was her argument and she made it out loud before she made anything else, which is a thing she has done for nine years and which is the reason a room full of angry men listens to her for a minute and a half.

"I am not going to ask this bench to decide anything this morning," said Reeve Aldis Fenn. "There is a Crown's form at the yard with a space for a figure in it and the figure asked for is persons, and a bench cannot answer a form and this bench has been shown that it cannot answer a form once already in Longlight and it did it very well. So this morning I am going to ask the room a question and I am going to write the answer on a sheet in my own hand and I am going to read the sheet out and that is all. Nobody is on oath. Nothing here binds anybody. And I want it understood by whoever sent that form that the reason I have called this is not that I have anything to give you. It is that I have found out something about this town and I do not like the shape of it."

She put her hand flat on the table, which she does, and which her sister does not, and the room noticed and neither of them said anything about it.

"The question is this. How many persons in this county can say no to the Old Toll crossing — not to a bench, not to a letter, to the crossing itself, to the thing being carried over that bridge, to the arrangement as it stands — and be believed, and go on living in this town afterwards?"

Nobody spoke for about as long as it takes to count eleven.

---

The second speaker was Captain Rell, and it cost him something, and the room could see it, and he did not pretend otherwise.

"Nineteen years," he said. "I have filled in nine hundred forms in this county and I have never once been asked what I thought a thing was. I was asked on the sixteenth of Mudmonth to take a bell off a bridge and I did it, and I found out three days later that the paper was dated the sixteenth and the bell came off the nineteenth, and there is no column on any form in this kingdom for the two days and I have wanted one for eleven months."

"And can you say no to the crossing?" said the reeve.

"I can say no to the crossing," said Captain Rell, "and I have said it twice in eleven months in rooms with clerks in them, and I have been right both times, and I have been *picked* for it, and there is a notice nailed to my own wall with a seal on it and I read it every morning. That is what my no is worth. It is worth a nail in a wall and it is worth being right. It is not worth a bench, because a bench has never asked me and I have never asked one."

The third was the guild's man with the levels, who is not named on any roll in this county and who has a quarter of an inch of the Sedge Reach bench mark in a leather tube and a refusal in his own hand.

"I can say no to that crossing and be believed, and I will tell you why, and the reason is not courage." He did not put his hands in his pockets and he did not sit down. "Because I will not sign this guild's roll. I have refused it once and I have written the refusal down and dated it, and a thing of mine that is in a book is a thing that can be looked at, and the Crown has been sending instruments in this county for eleven months and has had not one of them laid on my figures. So my no is worth exactly as much as my figures are worth and my figures are worth a quarter of an inch and everybody in this room knows which way that quarter of an inch points."

He looked at Lysa Fenn, who was at the back, and said: "That was rude, and I am not sorry, and you would have said the same."

"I would have said it shorter," said Lysa Fenn.

The fourth speaker was Cresset Vaine, who is thirty-three, makes stockings, and has never been asked a question in a room in her life.

"They will think I have come up here to be important," she said, "and I have come up here because I sell stockings to nine families in this town and every one of them has been asked something by somebody in the last four months and none of them could tell me what."

She put her hands on the table and took them off again.

"My want is not to be admired. My want is for this town to know how many of its own people it can be told *no* by, in a figure I can say out loud in a market, so that I have got something to say on a morning when a man in a good coat is in Merefen and wants the road. I have been losing that argument at three markets a month since Mudmonth with nothing in my hands except my own face, and I would like to stop."

---

The answer was eleven.

It was eleven and it was not four, and the four had been true in Greenmonth and had been written down in a room with a clerk in it, and it had taken five months and two instruments from another office to get from four to eleven, and not one of the seven had been asked anything to do with it.

They read them out in the order the sheet was in, and the sheet was the reeve's own hand, and she read the trades and not the names first, and then the names, and every name was a name in this town.

The road warden, who is a man with a bar and a scar and no office.

The carpenter of Weir Street, who is not a member of the bench and has not voted in it since he was twenty-one.

The watch captain, who took the bell and put it back.

The keeper of the records over the weigh-house, who is the only person in this county in a provincial file by name.

A magistrate, who is the watch captain's own trade and the reeve's own sister and who has said in a public room that her court asked an office for its authority and was not answered.

The reeve herself, who does not vote on the guild's own matter and has wanted to for nine years.

The man who weighs salt, who is sixty-eight, and whose second floor the Archive is in, and who was asked for it back in Longlight and has not asked again.

The holder of the tolls of the Old Toll crossing, sixty-one, who bought them from an abbey two years ago and who obeyed a suspension in writing and wanted the obedience on the chalk and is owed a hearing like anybody else.

The guild's man with the levels, who has refused his own guild's roll.

A man of forty-seven who was a watch sergeant in April and wrote down a true thing at his own table and brought it in unasked and lost his post and nine months and three weeks' accrual on the same morning and has not been paid for anything since and has not been promoted to anything and is not going to be.

And a stocking-maker of thirty-three who sells to nine families and has just told a room what she came up for.

Bram Ottery stood up when his trade was read out, at the back, in a coat that had been good in Greenmonth.

"That is me," he said, "and I want it said in this room that I have not been paid for anything since the fourth of Greenmonth, and that I am not going to be asked to make a speech about it, and that I would like the eleven to be on a sheet in a book and not in a roll, and I will tell the room why and then I will sit down."

"Go on," said the reeve.

"Because a roll in this town has a column headed *Keeper* on it now," said Bram Ottery, "and a man who is on a roll with a name under a column headed *Keeper* is a man who is an officer of something, and I have been one and I know what it costs, and I am asking the reeve not to enter the eleven on that roll."

The reeve entered the reason and read it back and entered the hour, and then she said the thing that made the morning.

"And you are right," she said, "and I am not going to do it, and I want it minuted that I am not doing it because a bench clerk asked me in a corridor on Thursday whether this was going on the roll, and I told him no, and he said *then it isn't anything, is it*, and I said no it isn't, and he went away satisfied, and I have thought about it for two days and he was not being cruel. He was asking the ordinary question. A thing that is not on a roll is not a thing a court can be given."

"Then where does it go?" said the man at the shave horse, who had come along and had no business there and had come anyway.

"It goes in a day-book over a weigh-house, against no person, with the hour on it," said Mara Vey, "and I will take it, and I will tell the room exactly why, and then I am going to say one sentence that I have been wanting to say since I came up the stair and I am going to say it once and badly."

---

"Nobody writes a count down in a filing room in this county," she said. "I have been a keeper of records for four years and a half and I have refused eight things and one of them was a good offer. So the room will believe me when I say that the reason I am taking this is that a count of persons is a list with the names taken off. That is all a count is. It is a list that somebody has had the names taken off, and a list with the names taken off is the one document I have spent four years refusing to make, and I have refused it in a room with a marshal of a Crown in it and I refused him in ninety seconds."

"Then you will not take it," said the reeve.

"I will take it and I will say the rest of it." Mara Vey put her hand flat on the sheet and did not move it. "A count is a list with the names taken off, and a list with the names taken off cannot be refused, because there is nobody in it to refuse with. That is the whole of the trick and it is not a trick anybody had to invent, it is just arithmetic, and the office that sent that form to the yard on Friday found it out the way a person finds out that water will run downhill. And a thing that cannot be refused and cannot be tried is not a document, it is a *road*. And a road is the thing that this county has been surviving on for eleven months, and it is the thing this case is about, and I have written that sentence down twice in a notebook in a coat and I have been wrong about how to say it for eleven months."

She took her hand off the sheet.

"So the eleven goes in my day-book, and it does not go on the guild roll, and I enter the reason, and the reason is the reeve's reason and mine, and if anybody from an office comes up that stair with a form about it, they will be answered with a number and a date and no name in it, and that will be the answer of this office for as long as I hold it."

Reeve Aldis Fenn looked at her sister, who had said nothing at all for fifty minutes, and Lysa Fenn said, at the back, in the voice of a woman who is thirty years old and has not slept properly:

"I have nothing to add. I have a finding of the fourth of Greenmonth with five clauses and one of them asks an office for its authority and the asking is dated, and that finding is the only instrument I have produced in eleven months, and it is worth exactly what a piece of paper is worth against an office two hundred miles off that has never lost anything for want of me. I have been in a room with a Crown's man twice and I have been right both times and neither time it cost the Crown anything, and if eleven people in this county can say no and be believed, then the instrument is not a document. It is the eleven. And I would like it minuted that a magistrate of this county said that in a room with no clerk in it, which she is."

---

At the fourth hour the same day, in a filing room over a weigh-house, somebody came up the stair.

She was thirty-six and she had walked from the eastern counties and she was in the same coat she had been in at the end of Fallowmonth, when she had come up that stair with one sheet of paper and gone down it again with a receipt, and the receipt is in the day-book against nobody with the hour because a document received is entered whoever brings it, and that is the office's rule and not a kindness.

"You are the keeper," she said.

"I am."

"My name is Nell Tace. I am of Wickmoor. I came in Fallowmonth with a form about a fever and I have come again with the second part of it." She put a folded sheet on the table and did not let go of it. "That is a list. That is eleven names, and they are eleven households out of Wickmoor and two other villages, and they are the eleven the county proposes to take off the public roll."

"Why are you bringing this to me?"

"Because the first time I came up this stair a woman told me that the thing I was describing was not this office's war, and she was right, and I have thought about it for four months and she was right, and I have come back anyway, because I am not wrong about what it is, I am only wrong about which room it belongs to." She let go of the sheet. "And because the clerk at the assizes in the eastern counties asked me who I had shown it to, and I told him, and he wrote it down. That is the second reason and it is the reason I am actually here."

Mara Vey read the sheet. Eleven names in a clerk's hand with a date against each and a column headed with a word she did not know and had to look up, and the word was *removal*, and the column after it was headed *ground*, and eleven of the eleven had a line under it.

"And your name is on it," she said.

"My name is on it," said Nell Tace. "I am the twelfth. They have not got me on the sheet yet because I am still able to answer, and when I cannot answer they will have me, and that is how the sheet works, and the man at the assizes explained it to me in a very reasonable voice."

"What do you want?"

"I want my village's fever entered correctly," said Nell Tace. "I want it in a book with a date on it and not in a story. And I want my own name kept on the roll. That is the whole of it and I have no interest in your bridge and I have never heard of your marshal and if he walks up that stair I shall tell him the same two things and he may write them down."

Then she said the other thing, once, and she said it the way a person says a thing they have said to four other people and are now saying to a fifth because there is no other word for it.

"You do not have to worry about the Culling Order where I come from," she said. "We only ever hear the name. Nobody has ever come and told us who they are."

And the keeper of the Bellweather Claims Archive wrote the hour on the receipt, and entered the sheet in the day-book against no person, and told Nell Tace the truth.

"It is not this office's war," she said. "It is the eastern counties' and the assizes' and a physician's, and the only reason it is in this room is that you have carried it up two flights of stairs and I have a rule about documents that people bring up stairs themselves. I am not going to tell you that I can do anything about it, because you would find out in a month and I would rather you found out from a person."

"Then I will take it back down the stairs," said Nell Tace, "and I will come again if I have got anything else, and I will not come again for a favour."

"That," said Mara Vey, "is the correct arrangement and I am going to write it down as a term of it, and I am going to enter the eleven names in the day-book under their own names with a date, because they are eleven people and not a count, and I am not sending this to anybody, and I am not going to total them against anything."

She wrote the entry and read it back and the reading of it had the word *removal* in it three times, and the ground column, and the eleven names, and the twelfth that had not come yet, and at the foot of it she wrote, in the fair hand, with the hour:

*Entered against no person, on the office's own account, and not sent to any person, and the reason is that a man can enter a claim against this office and a county cannot be served, and the second reason, which is the real one, and which I am obliged to put down: this office has a claim in a county file two hundred miles off that has a woman's name on a separate sheet under a separate number, and that is the last thing in this business that was done to somebody, and I am not going to be the second thing in somebody else's book.*
