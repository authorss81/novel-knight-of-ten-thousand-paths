# Chapter 0094

The twelfth of Greenmonth was a Friday and it was not a market day, and Sabin Dain came down the county post at the fourth hour with a leather bag and a form in it, and she walked the last four miles because the post does not come to a lane with a door on it, and she had asked in advance for it to be fetched and the clerk of the county council had written *no carriage* in the margin, which is the most county answer there is.

She had been to this county town four times in eleven years. She had never been on foot to a keeper of records, and she had thought about it for a long time on the road, and what she had thought about it was not the sentence. The sentence had been ready since the second of Frostmonth. What she had thought about on the road was how to be asked.

**Being asked is the most frightening thing anybody can do to an officer of that office, and it is the only thing that makes an officer answerable, and she had learned it in eleven years by nearly being asked, four times, in three counties, and had spent nine months afterwards wishing in each case that somebody had gone on asking.**

So she did not wait to be asked. She walked up the stairs of a building over a court with a bag in her hand and said, in the first nine seconds, before anybody had offered her a chair:

"I am Sabin Dain, an aid to the register at the Office of Provincial Registration, and I am not a member of anything, and I have never been a member of anything, and if anybody ever tells you otherwise in a room you should ask them for the number of the list. I would like it entered that I have said that, and where, and the hour, and I have said it three times in three counties in eleven years and twice to your own post."

"Entered," said the clerk of the county council. "By whom."

"By me. In a fair hand. With the day and the hour and the number of the form I am carrying, which is the ninth of a series, and if there is a mistake in it I would like the mistake entered as well and not left for somebody else to find in four years."

Marda Oakes came in from the far end of a room with a wet coat on and stood still long enough for everybody in the room to look up, and then said, "You had better come in," and then, in the doorway, to nobody, "**Eleven years.**"

"I know what it is," said Sabin Dain.

---

**They gave her the room over a court, and they gave her a table, and she put the bag on it and took out a form and did not open it until the room had gone quiet.**

"You are going to ask me what I want," she said, "and I am going to tell you, and then I am going to say the other thing, and I would like the other thing said before anybody writes anything down, because after anybody writes something down everybody gets careful and the carefulness is not what I came for."

"Say the other thing first," said Marda Oakes.

"Right." She put both hands flat on the form. "I have been eleven years in that office and I have filled in about two hundred forms a year, and the returns come back every year with fewer names in them and more figures in them, and every year nobody can be punished for anything, because a figure cannot be refused and a figure expires for want of a party.

"**I want that no family in this country is ever again removed from a record by a name nobody entered.** I have wanted it since I was twenty-two. I do not care whose family. I do not care what they did. I want the name on the paper by the man who takes it off, in his own hand, with a date against it, and I want a column on the form where he has to put it. That is the whole of it. I have said that in three counties and I am not going to improve on it and I have been told three times that it is impractical, and I would like it entered that I said it here and that nobody improved on it."

"That has been minuted in this county before," said Marda Oakes, "by a magistrate, in a room, and it is the most carefully made document I have ever read and it has been read by four people and three of them work for me."

"I know what that document is," said Sabin Dain. "I have a copy of it in my bag. **It is a notice. It is a magnificent notice and it is addressed to an office that has never read it and is not going to and will not be made to, because there is no mechanism in that office or in this kingdom that compels a person to read a document.** Notices do not stop anybody. They only stop people who read them, and I have spent eleven years being one of the people who reads them and I have got very good at it and it has never once stopped anything."

---

**And then she said the sentence, and it was not a threat and it was not an offer, and she said it standing up, at about the fourth hour, in a room over a court, to about nine people including two of them clerks.**

"The office is not asking you to tell us anything.

"The office is asking you to confirm a figure. **A figure has no name in it.** A figure is a thing a person can put on a form and a clerk can certify and a provincial office can file, and it is lawful, and it costs nobody anything, and nobody in it can be punished for it, and it does not go stale the way a name goes stale, because there is no name in it to get old.

"In four years there will be a number in a file in a provincial town with no name attached to it, and that number will be about this county, and nobody will be able to say whose hand made it. Not because the hand has been concealed. Because a figure does not need one. **And that is the whole of what is coming, and it is not coming because anybody in that office has thought of it in a room with a clock on the wall. It is coming because it is cheaper than names, and cheaper than names it is, and there is not one instrument in this kingdom that can make it more expensive.**"

Nobody spoke, and the rain did the rest of it.

"May I put that in the minute," said the clerk of the county council, "and I am asking because I have never in twenty-two years entered an officer of a provincial office making a statement, and I do not know what a minute is for."

"**Yes,**" said Sabin Dain, "and I want it all of it, and I want the number of the form on it, and I want the hour, and I want my name spelled with the whole of it and not the short, and I want it in your hand and not in mine, and I want you to read it back and me to correct it."

He wrote it. He read it back. She corrected one word — *number* was not *figure* in the second sentence, and she was right, and he made the correction and read it again and stopped.

"You asked for it," she said, "and I want to tell you what it costs, because you will find out anyway and it is better that a clerk who has written it down knows.

"**Being recorded is the most frightening thing that anybody can do to an officer of that office, and it is the only thing that makes an officer answerable, and I have just asked a county body to record me and to keep me, and I did that because I would like to be one of the four people in this kingdom who can prove that in this month an officer of that office stood in a room and said a figure was coming, and said it four hours after the figure was answered by somebody else, and was wrong about nothing.**"

"You will be a witness," said Marda Oakes.

"I will be a witness in a book in a county that has a door and a number and is not in any register of any office in this kingdom," said Sabin Dain, "and that is precisely why I asked for it, and I would like the minute to say that I said that, and I will not be offended if the clerk declines to enter it."

The clerk of the county council entered it.

---

**The thing she came for, and had not said yet, was a question, and she asked it at about the fifth hour with the form still on the table.**

"You have had four instruments in this county in eleven months," she said, "and I have read all four, and I am going to describe them to you and you will tell me if I have any of them wrong, because a woman from two hundred miles off who can describe your instruments back to you is a woman who has read them, and that is worth having on the record too.

"**One.** A sealed order, dated, delivered, and a bell taken off a bridge, and a court that found the instrument lawful and said the court had nothing further to say about it at any time.

"**Two.** An instruction in force since the ninth of Longlight, with no law named, no court named, and no office named, and a town that was not required to be told.

"**Three.** A writ of enquiry that did not want to succeed, on a reeve's records, and was discharged, and the second of the two was discharged as well.

"**Four.** And this is the one I have been in a room about for nine months. A count. Returnable, dated, no party in it, and a column on it, and a figure where the names go, and the figure answers to nothing and expires for want of a party and cannot be refused without refusing a number."

She put a finger on the ninth of a series.

"That is the one that is going to cost this county something, and it is not going to cost it anything in a court, and I am not going to be able to warn you about it in a way that helps, because the whole of it is that there is nothing to warn you about. **All four of them are instruments and this one is an argument, and an argument is a thing that can be met with a counter-argument, and I have not got one, and that is why I am here and not in my office.**"

"Answer the question," said Marda Oakes. "You said it was a question."

"Yes," said Sabin Dain. "**Does this county intend to use the figure?**"

Somebody at the back said no, and somebody else said nothing at all, and Marda Oakes said: "No, and that is the correct answer, and it is also the answer that four years from now will be quoted as the moment it was decided, and I would like the minute to have my reason in it and not only the word."

"Your reason."

"That a figure is only a number until somebody needs it," said Marda Oakes, "and that the only thing that makes a number into a weapon is a person who is frightened of what is behind it, and there are about four of those in this county and two of them are standing in this room."

---

**Lysa Fenn came in at about the fifth hour and was made a chair and sat in it, and she had walked the eleven miles out of Merefen in the rain that morning and had said so to nobody, and Dain knew who she was the moment she came round the table, because a magistrate of this county is the only person in it who has been in the room that Sabin Dain has just described.**

"I wrote the finding on the ninth of this month, in this room, an hour after I said that," said Lysa Fenn. "It is three days old and it is in my book and it says that this county's instruments in the last eleven months are lawful, unanswerable, and four in number, and that the fourth of them is the one that frightens me, and that I can do nothing about any of the four, and that I have said so in a finding which is a document that four people in this county have read."

"And you have come to hear a provincial officer say it in a room," said Sabin Dain.

"I have come because a finding is the only thing I make and I make about eleven a year and every one of them is a notice," said Lysa Fenn, "and I have spent three days working out the following out of my own reasoning and not out of anything she said, because she is in this room and I would like it in a book somewhere in this county and not only in mine.

"**A witness is the cheapest instrument in the kingdom and it is the only one that cannot be withdrawn.** Every return expires. Every notice stops only people who read it. Every finding is a thing a court says about a thing and can be got out of a file by whoever has the file in about nine years. And a woman who stands in a room and says a thing out loud to nine people, and has it written down by a clerk, and corrects the clerk, is a figure in a book that answers a question for as long as the book is dry.

"That is all I have come for and I have got it, and I am going back eleven miles in the rain in an hour, and there is a market on Thursday at the far end of it."

"That is the cheapest thing anybody has said to me in eleven years," said Sabin Dain, "and I am not going to be able to use it, and I would like it in the minute."

"It is in the minute," said the clerk of the county council.

---

**She was on the stair at about the sixth hour with the bag over her shoulder, and Marda Oakes came after her, and caught her on the turn, and the two of them stood in a stone stair in the rain.**

"You said in there that a figure cannot be refused," said Marda Oakes. "That is the sentence of the year and it is going to be quoted for thirty years, and I have been a member of a council for eleven years and I have never once made a sentence in a room in my life, and I have just watched a woman from two hundred miles off make one in a room over a court and get it minuted without improving a word of it, and I would like to know what you do about it."

"I fill in the forms," said Sabin Dain. "That is the whole of what I do. I have been at it eleven years and I have never been in a room where it was a large thing to do, and it is going to be a large thing to do, and I have asked to be recorded, and I would like you to understand that asking to be recorded is not the same as being brave. **It is much worse than being brave. It is arithmetic.** A witness is a figure. I have made myself a figure in a book in a county I cannot serve, and in about four years somebody is going to read that minute and count me in it, and there will be no name."

"There is your name in it," said Marda Oakes. "You made them spell it with the whole of it."

"Yes," said Sabin Dain, "and it will be the only name in the file, and I will have earned it, and it will not help me at all."

---

**And the last thing she did on the twelfth of Greenmonth was at the gate of the court, in the rain, to a man she had not met, who was standing in a gateway four yards off with his hands loose at his sides and his weight a little back, doing nothing at all, waiting for somebody who was not coming.**

"You are the road warden," she said. "You are on the roll and you have a vote and you do not hold the crossing and you are not on the bench, and I have read the minute of the ninth of Frostmonth and there are eight pages of it in your county's press and I have read all eight, and I did not have to, and I would like you to know that I did not have to, because in about four years a man is going to use those eight pages and he is not going to be me."

"You did not have to," said Aren Kest.

"No," said Sabin Dain. "**I did it because I am frightened of unnamed things and there is not one of them in this county that I have not been able to put in a hand, and I am going to go back to a provincial town and fill in a form about this county with a figure in it and no name in it, and I have just spent a day writing down the things I am frightened of in a book that will be read by strangers, and I would do it again.**"

"You filled in the *Keeper* column," said Aren Kest.

"I filled in the *Keeper* column because the form says to," said Sabin Dain, "and I have filled it in about two hundred times, and I am not going to discuss it with a man in a gateway, and you did not ask me to."

"No," said Aren Kest.

"That is the whole of what I wanted," she said. "**Thank you.**"

She went out into the rain, and was gone down the county road on foot with a bag before the rain had properly started, and the county post is three days either way, and she will be at a desk in a provincial town on the sixteenth of this month with four months of returns in a file in front of her, and a man in a good coat is going to ask about that file one day.
