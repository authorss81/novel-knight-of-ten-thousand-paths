# Chapter 0076

The ninth of Frostmonth was a Monday and a market day, and by the sixth hour the market had done what it does, and the room over the weigh-house at the north end of the town had the particular quiet of a room in which a thing has been decided and nobody has said so yet.

The thing had been decided on the fifteenth of Wolfmonth, in a conversation, in about nine minutes, and the decision was a figure, and the figure had to be on a sheet by the sixth hour of today because the sheet said so.

There was one sheet. It was a return on the reverse of a form of the R.C. series — the same printing, the same shape, the same foot of the series in the office's small hand on the back, which nobody in that room had ever read out and which was not read out today either — and it wanted a county, and then it wanted a figure, and then under the figure the four words: *how the figure was compiled.*

It also had a ruled column headed *Keeper*, running down the right-hand side, empty, the length of the sheet.

Mara Vey filled it in at the sixth hour with a stub of pencil she had sharpened herself, because Sarn Oyelaran had told her once, in the first year, that a thing you sign in another person's ink is a thing you have not quite done.

*County: Merefen. Persons: eleven. How compiled: read out in a hall in this county on the fourth of Embermonth at the fourth hour, in the presence of forty persons, with a date and an hour, and entered in the day-book of this office against no person. Of the eleven, four appear in a file of that office and seven do not; the figure contains all four and names none of them. The figure is a figure. It is not a list, and it was not made by taking a list and stopping, and the order of the eleven is the order in which they stood up in that hall, which is not an order, and the office will not be able to do anything with it except add to it.*

She read it three times and then left the *Keeper* column empty, and she did not enter a reason, because entering a reason in that column is a thing the form's own printing tells a person to do and she was not going to do it and she was not going to write down in a book that she had not done it, and Sarn Oyelaran had said the same thing to her once in a different context and about a different piece of paper and had been right then.

"Post," said Sarn Oyelaran.

"By the hand to the county clerk and not to the office," said Mara Vey. "It is returnable to an office two hundred miles off and it is going to be carried the last four miles by a boy on a horse, and if I put it in the county's bag it will sit in the county clerk's basket for a week, and I have watched that happen to three things this year and I have read all three of them afterwards and every one of the three had been useful to the sender in the delay and to nobody else."

---

The boy came at about the half hour, and she gave him the sheet and a halfpenny out of the box and wrote the halfpenny in the ordinary way, and then she stood at the lane door with the door open after him for longer than a woman stands at a door.

"You are not going to close it," said Sarn Oyelaran.

"No."

"He has been gone a good minute and a half."

"I am aware of how long he has been gone," said Mara Vey, and shut the door, and stood against the inside of it with her back to it for a moment, which she had not done in four years and a half, and which Sarn Oyelaran looked at and then deliberately did not look at, and which is on the page because it happened.

Then she wrote the day-book entry, and it is the entry the volume ends on, and it reads:

---

*Ninth of Frostmonth, YR 312, a Monday, a market day, at the seventh hour. Returned to the Office of Provincial Registration, one sheet, the figure eleven, county Merefen, how compiled stated at length, the column headed* Keeper *left empty and no reason entered, because a form with a figure in it does not need a name in it and a form that needs a name in it is a different kind of form and this office did not get that kind.*

*The claim number is entered against the office and against no person, with the date and the hour, and there is one claim number, and it is the same claim number the office gave this sheet and it is a number about a piece of paper and not about anybody.*

*No opinion is entered. The not writing the opinion is entered, and it is the third time this year I have entered the not writing, and I have begun to think of it as a kind of entry in its own right, and there is a keeper in this county who would tell me that a not writing entered in the same book and the same hand as the writing is a second kind of entry and I would be right, and I am going to go on doing it, and if it is wrong then in nine years it will be wrong in my own hand and with the hour on it and that is the best I can do.*

*A figure is not a list. A list with the names taken off is a figure, and this office has said that in a room of forty people and in this book, and today this office has said it to an office of the Crown in writing, with a claim number against it, and I would like it recorded that I knew exactly what I was doing at the seventh hour of a Monday and that I did it because the alternative was a list.*

---

The knock was at the seventh hour and it was not the lane door, because the lane door had been shut for about twenty minutes, and it was on the stair.

Marda Oakes was forty-five and she was a member of the county council and she had come from the county town, and she came up two flights of a stair over a weigh-house in a plain good coat and asked, on the stair, whether the office was a business, and when Mara Vey said it was, she said, "Then I will not leave my card," and went up.

"I have read your writs of enquiry," she said, before she sat down. "The one of the ninth of Longlight and the one of the twenty-ninth. All of them, and nothing else that has been served on this council in a year, and not the finding, which is a different document and does not pretend to be the same one. I have read the finding twice and I want to say something about the finding and I am going to say it at the end, because I have been in a room before and I have watched people open with their best material and go home with nothing."

"Sit down. That chair is sound."

"I will sit on that one, thank you." She sat on the other. "I have wanted to come here for about six weeks and I have not come, and the reason I have not come is that I did not want to arrive with a resolution and I have not got one, and I am going to tell you what I have got, and then you are going to tell me to go away, and I will go."

"Go on."

"I want this office to be a corporation." She put her hands on her knees. "That is the whole of what I have come to say and I have said it in the first minute and I have not got a reason yet, and I have got three reasons and they are in the wrong order, and I am a member of a county council and I have spent eleven years watching what a county does to itself in a room with a door, and the thing I have learned in eleven years is that the county can only act against a thing it can be told about, and it can only be told about a thing it can sue, and it can only sue a thing that is a person."

"And this office is not a person."

"This office is a woman with a lease on a room and a press that is the property of this office and a door on a lane, and four pence a year, and the smallest paid item in this business twice over, and I know that, and I have read the lease." She stopped. "I am going to tell you what a corporation is for and then I am going to stop, and I am not going to finish the sentence, and I want you to understand that I am not finishing it on purpose."

"I should like to understand that."

"A county council can write to a keeper of records. A county council can send a woman to a room with a paper. A county council can say *will you produce this* and get *no* written in a book in about nine months, and then what the county council has is a woman who said no, and a woman who said no can be told she said no, and the county has no remedy at all, and it will find another thing to do." Marda Oakes looked at the long table and at the press and at the day-book. "**I have spent eleven years being that.**"

The fire in the small grate did not crackle because there was a very small fire in it.

"That is the half," she said. "I am not going to say the other half in a room with a keeper in it, and the reason is not manners. If I say the other half, then in four years, when there is a reason, you will remember that a member of the county council said it to you first, and you will think of me, and you will think well of me, and you will have been warned in advance by a friendly person, and that is the one way I must not do it. **You are not an office yet. When you are one, everybody who helped you will be a person who helped you.**"

Mara Vey sat very still.

"The finding," she said. "You said you wanted to say something about it at the end."

"The finding of the fourth of Greenmonth is the most carefully made document I have ever read and it has been read by exactly four people, one of them me, and three of the four of them work for you." Marda Oakes stood up. "It is a notice. It is a magnificent notice, it is beautifully made, it has a date on it and a signature at the foot of it, and the office it is aimed at has never read it and is not going to and will not be made to, because there is no mechanism in that office or in this kingdom that compels a person to read a document. **I have been on a council for eleven years. Notices do not stop anybody. They only stop people who read them, and there are four of those in this county and three of them are yours.**"

"That is fair."

"It is not fair," said Marda Oakes. "It is the most useful thing anybody has said to me in about two years and I have not come here to be fair with you. I have come here to tell you that a document is a thing that is read and that a county is a thing that sues, and that in this county at present a document wins, and that in about four years, in my own judgement, it will not, and that the thing that will beat it is a thing with a door on it and a number, and that the number is the whole of the difference, and I have said all of that and I have not said the sentence, and I am going now, and I would like to leave my card, and I know you said not to."

"I said you did not have to. That is not the same as you not leaving it."

Marda Oakes put a card on the table. It had a name on it and a county and a room in the county town and nothing else at all, no seal, no arms, and no office, and Mara Vey entered it in the day-book against a person, with the date and the hour, which is the only time in four years she has entered a card against a person.

At the door she said, over her shoulder: "There is a man in this county who reads forms for a living and reads them better than I do, and of everything that has been served on this council in a year, none of it has ever once asked you for anything, and I have been wondering for six weeks whether that is a very good sign about you or a very bad sign about him, and I have not worked it out, and I thought you might have thought about it."

Then the stair, and the door below, and the lane.

Mara Vey sat down and then stood up again, and then sat down.

"Sarn."

"Two hours," said Sarn Oyelaran.

"She did not ask me a single question about the file. Not one. In an hour she asked me about a corporation and told me that a notice does not stop anybody and left me a card, and she is the first person in about a year who has come into this room and not wanted a name out of it, and I have not been able to work out whether that is a kindness or a method."

"It is a method," said Sarn Oyelaran. "Everything anybody has done in this case for a year has been a method. The man with the rule is a method. The man with the churn is a method. That is not an insult; that is the trade. **A thing done without a method is a thing done by a person, and there have been about four of those in four years and one of them is you and two of them are dead.**"

"I am going to write down what I thought about her, and I am not going to write down the sentence she did not say."

"Enter that you did not write it."

"I am going to do something I have never done," said Mara Vey. "I am going to write a paragraph and stop in the middle of a sentence, and enter that I stopped, and enter that I stopped on purpose, and not write any more about it, ever, in this book, because a woman who finishes that sentence in her own day-book in about four years' time is a woman who has decided to want it, and I did not decide to want anything this afternoon, she did, and she did it in a room and then she went down a stair and left a card with her name and no office on it, and I am not going to be the woman who says a thing like that in writing."

---

At about the eighth hour she went and got the file for the Dain instrument, to make the claim number in the day-book agree with the number on the copy, and the day-book was where she always kept the unopened things, because the day-book is the only book in that office that nobody can serve.

It was on the seventeenth of Wolfmonth. A county letter, four days old when it came, dated at the place of issue the **thirteenth of Wolfmonth** — a Wednesday, in a hand she knew from the outside of a letter on the fifteenth, in a woman's writing and not a clerk's — entered against no person with the hour, and it had sat in the back of the day-book for twenty-two days, and she had never read it, and the reason she had never read it was that it had asked nothing of this office and named nobody in it, and in four years and a half she has never before had a paper that asked nothing of her arrive in her own day-book.

She read it now. It is three lines long.

*Received. The office notes that a count of persons is not a list and proposes a count of persons for the county as a whole.*

Then nothing. Three lines, a date, a signature.

Mara Vey read it twice, and then she got out the day's post and looked at the top of it, which was the copy of her own return with eleven in it and the *Keeper* column empty, and she put the two sheets side by side on the long table.

The ninth of Wolfmonth: a yard, on Weir Street, four men in a gateway, a carpenter's answer on the back of a week's wage sheet, a figure of **nil** and a sentence about a yard not being an office.

The ninth of Wolfmonth: this room, this office, **eleven**, a claim number, a *Keeper* column left empty, and a return that had gone to the province four hours ago.

The thirteenth of Wolfmonth, four days after the second: **received.**

She read it a third time and then she put it down and wrote in the day-book, at the eighth hour, in the ordinary way, with the date and the hour and nothing else on the line:

---

*Thirteenth of Wolfmonth, YR 312, a Wednesday. From the Office of Provincial Registration, three lines, signed, proposing a count of persons for the county as a whole. Entered on the seventeenth, against no person, and not read until this day, which is the ninth of Frostmonth, and which is twenty-two days, and the reason it was not read is entered above and is that it asked nothing of this office.*

*This office has no instrument that can touch a figure for a county, and I am entering that as a fact and not as an opinion, and the fact is that a count of persons who have had knowledge of a declared object is a different thing from a count of the persons in a county, and there is no form in this office that asks the second question, and there is no office in this county that could answer it, and the county's schedule of provincial offices has been searched and does not carry one.*

*And I am entering, because I have to, that this office never asked for a figure for a county. It did not occur to me. It occurred to them, on the thirteenth of Wolfmonth, four days after a carpenter wrote nil on the back of a wage sheet, and I have read the two documents and I am the only person in this county who has read both of them, and I have read both of them and I do not see anything in them.*

---

She read that over twice, and then she put the pen in the drawer, and shut the drawer, and did not go down to the yard, because it was not the yard's business, and she did not send for the man who writes the dates down in a book on a bench-board in a gateway in Weir Street, because nobody had asked her to, and because he was not the office for it, and because he would have told her what was in it.

---

**None of the next three paragraphs is known to her, and she is not going to be told it, and that is not a gate and not a mystery: a man who refused to tell her one thing in a room on the fifth of Wolfmonth is not a man who will be told the rest of it in a week, and a keeper who has found out a thing like that from a third party is a keeper holding two documents about one person instead of one.**

He would have told her inside a minute. He had a whole shape of it in a notebook in a coat — half of it; the office, the form, the column with a word at the top of it, and half of the other half — and he had written down, in his own hand, in a clay county four hundred miles off, that he had decided to leave the rest alone, and that he had decided it on purpose so that a clerk in Merefen could see it was a decision and not an oversight, and he had been back in this county thirty-four days.

He was twenty-five yards from this door at that moment. He was not going to be in this county in three weeks. There was a letter in a file in the guild hall over the wharf, made at a bench in this town about a thing a bench of bridgewrights in a county four hundred miles off had wanted done, and it had been made on the ninth of Embermonth, on a Thursday, at a market, and the only two people in this county who knew what it bound him to were the two who had made it, and one of them was not a member of any bench, and the other one did not vote.

He would not be here. And that was on a form somewhere in another county, and it was not anybody's business but his and Toban's, and it was going to be the fourth of Hearthmonth.

---

Back in the room over the weigh-house, at about the eighth hour of a Monday afternoon, the served schedule of the eighth of Fallowmonth was on the desk under a weight, with its eight ruled lines, and the eighth of them was the city's, and it was empty, and it had been empty for four years, and she had looked at it twice in a year and had wanted to fill it twice and had written down both times, and on the ninth of Frostmonth she put her hand flat on the weight over it, four inches above the line, and left it there for as long as filling it in would have taken, and then took it away, and shut the press.

Then she went downstairs and closed the lane door at about the ninth hour, and the room over the weigh-house went on, as it had gone on for four years and a half, doing two rooms' work in one, with a claim number against a figure and no name anywhere in the county that had been written down since the twenty-seventh of Fallowmonth.
