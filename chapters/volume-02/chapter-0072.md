# Chapter 0072

The fifteenth of Wolfmonth was a Friday, and Fridays in Merefen are not market days, and a room over a weigh-house at the north end of a town that has lost a market day is quieter on a Friday than it is on a Thursday, and the reason is not the day. It is that on a Thursday about four hundred people come through a town and on a Friday about nine hundred people do not, and a keeper of records with four hundred and ninety holdings on a long table can tell the difference at the second hour.

Mara Vey was at the second hour with Sarn Oyelaran and the day's work, and Sarn was reading out of the fourth press's shelf register, which she had a fair copy of and which was the only thing in the world she was reading for pleasure.

"—and it is a fair copy of a fair copy, and it is not produced in court, and it is held in this city, and I have written all four of those on the wrapper in my own hand as a copyist with no opinion, and I would like it minuted that I have said the word *opinion* eleven times in four years and I have never once had one."

"You have never once had one," said Mara Vey, "and I have read every one of the four and I do not believe a word of it, and I am not going to argue about it on a Friday."

"You never do."

"I never do. It is a professional disagreement and it will keep."

The lane door made the noise it made.

---

Rowan Petch came in the way a man comes in who has been walking for three days and has slept in a coach and has decided, somewhere about the fortieth mile, that he is going to do this.

He was twenty-six and thin in a new way, and the coat he had gone in on the eighth of Embermonth was the coat he came back in, and it had been rained on in four counties. He put a folded sheet on the table, squared it, and squared it the way a provincial clerk squares a thing, which is to the table and not to its own creases, and then he sat down without being asked, which he had not done in four years.

"I filed it on the twelfth," he said.

"Show me."

He did not show him. He pushed it two inches nearer, which is what a man does when he has decided to let somebody read a thing he is ashamed of.

---

*To the Office of Provincial Registration, in the matter of the county file held by the provincial court in the crossing of Alder Vale.*

*I am directed by this court to state the purpose for which the said file is held, and I am unable to comply, and I set out below the whole of what I am able to state.*

*1. There is a box. It is in this court and it is in a room and the room is locked and the key is held by the clerk of this court and by nobody else.*
*2. The box came into this court on a day I am able to state and I have stated it in the court's own book with the hour on it.*
*3. The hand upon the outside of the box is the hand of the man who put it there, and it is not the hand of the man who keeps the shelf, and those are two different men and I have met both.*
*4. Upon the top of the box is the shelf mark, and the shelf mark is in the press register, and the press register is the register of what a man said he did with a shelf.*

*The court cannot state the purpose for which the file is held, and the court does not decline to state it, and the difference is the whole of this return. If the purpose is required in order that this court may do something, then this court will do that thing when it is told what the thing is. If the office holds a purpose, then the office is the holder of it, and the office is the only body in this kingdom that can state its own purpose in its own hand under its own seal, and this court respectfully requests that it do so, and will then answer it.*

*Entered against the officer of this court, by whom it was returned, with the hour, on the twelfth of Wolfmonth.*

*Rowan Petch, clerk of the provincial court.*

---

Mara Vey read it twice and then read the last sentence again.

"Three," she said. "You have said *I have met both*."

"I have met both."

"That is the load-bearing line in the whole of it and you have put it in the second position where a man in a hurry will not read it."

"I put it in the second position on purpose and I have thought about it for two days on a road," said Rowan Petch, "and I will tell you why. If it is in the first position it looks like the point. If it is in the second position it looks like an address, and a man in a hurry reads an address, and a man in a hurry who is about to decide whether there is a purpose in that box will read a line that says a clerk has met two men, and he will put it down as a clerk being helpful. And in four years, when somebody wants to know how the file came to be in this court, there will be a return in a book in a filing room two hundred miles off that says the clerk of this court has met both of them, and nobody will remember writing it, and that is the only kind of sentence I am ever going to get to write."

Sarn Oyelaran put her pen down.

"May I say the thing," she said.

"You may always say the thing," said Mara Vey. "You have said it in this room eleven times and it has been right eleven times."

"You have written a return that says the court cannot state the purpose and does not decline to state it," said Sarn Oyelaran, "and that is the first thing anybody in this case has ever written that cannot be turned into a refusal by a man who does not want to look at the fourth line. If they read it as a refusal, the file is closed to them and they have to open the box. If they read it as a compliance, the file is answered and nobody ever has to open anything. **There is no reading of it that is comfortable and that is the whole of the trick, and I have not seen it done before and I have been a copyist for nine years.**"

"I did not think of that," said Rowan Petch.

"No," said Sarn Oyelaran, "and I would like it noted that I have said a thing in a room and a man has admitted he did not think of it, and that this is now twice this year and both times it was in this room, and this office is a very small room."

Mara Vey was not looking at either of them. She was looking at the return.

"You said it in a gateway on the seventh of Embermonth," she said, "and it went in the yard's book with the date and the hour, and I did not know that until Tovan Kest told me a fact about a bridge on the fifth of Wolfmonth, which he was entitled to tell me and which I have entered against no person. And now I have the sheet itself. And I want to say something about the fourth line and then I am going to stop."

"The press register," said Rowan Petch.

"The press register is the most honest book in that office and it is honest about the wrong thing, and I know that, and I know who taught me it and he is not in this room." She turned the sheet round so it was the right way up for him. "You have written *the press register is the register of what a man said he did with a shelf.* That is your sentence and it is a good one and it is yours and I want you to keep it, because I am going to need it in about eight months and I am not going to be able to remember that a clerk of the provincial court said it in my room on a Friday."

"It is not mine," said Rowan Petch. "It is the other one. Halven's."

"It is yours in your hand on your paper, and I do not care where you got it, and I am the keeper of this office and what is in this office is mine," said Mara Vey, "and that is a sentence you should not argue with."

He did not argue with it.

---

Then he took his hat off, which he had not done, and put it on his knee, which is a thing a frightened clerk does, and Mara Vey saw it and put her own pen down, which she had not intended to do.

"There is a second thing," he said, "and I have had it for two days and I have not said it in four years about anything, and I am going to say it and then I am going to be sick, and the order of those two is not the order they usually happen in."

"Say the second thing."

"The office has stopped asking about the box."

Mara Vey waited.

"On the twelfth, in the morning, before I filed, I was in the outer room with a clerk I have spoken to about nine times in two years, and he was reading out a column of a schedule against a list of counties, and he was doing it the way we all do it, out loud, half to himself, and he got to a county and he said, *this one, the file, has a person in it now* — and he did not stop, and he went on to the next county, and I stood there for about four seconds and then I said, *a person*, and he said, *a person having knowledge, on the fourth, in her own hand, and I would not put my name to that sheet in a court.*

"And then he said a name, and I have never heard the name before in my life, and I have written it down and I have brought it down here in my own hand and I have not written it into any book of this office, and I am going to write it on this table and then I am going to tear it up, and I would like you to look at it first."

She looked at it.

The name was Sabin Dain.

"Thirty-three," said Rowan Petch. "Eleven years in the office. Her title on her own paper is an aid to the register. She is not a member of anything and she has told me so herself, twice, in a corridor, in a way I would have thought was modesty and which is not modesty." He swallowed. "**And the office has entered her into a county file as the person having knowledge, in her own hand, on the fourth of Embermonth, and the file is the file in the matter of the crossing of Alder Vale, and the file is in your office's claim, on a claim number that is in this room.**"

Nobody said anything for about nine seconds. Downstairs a waggon went over the bridge and the weigh-house chain clinked.

"That is not a slow fuse," said Sarn Oyelaran.

"That is the exact opposite of a slow fuse," said Rowan Petch. "That is a lit one. A name in a county file is a person who can be produced. Not this year. Not by anybody who wants anything. But in four years, if this office ever wants to know whether a form went out of a county and who filled it, there is a name in a column, and the name is a woman of thirty-three who has done nothing wrong in her life except write down what she was asked to write down in her own hand, and she will be the answer to a question she never asked."

"Why did she write her own hand in it?" said Mara Vey.

"Because the form asked for the person having knowledge and there was no office of keeper in the county schedule and she is the person having knowledge, and she wrote the truth." He said it without any weight at all. "That is the whole of what she did. She wrote the truth in a column with a word at the top of it. And the column has cost her a thing she does not know she has lost yet."

"Do not tell me anything else about her," said Mara Vey. "Not her county, not her trade, not how she said it. If I hold one fact about that woman it is a fact, and I will enter the fact, and if I hold five I will start assembling them, and a keeper who assembles facts about a person is a person with an interest in a person. Enter the name in nothing. Say it once."

"I said it once."

"Then it is said once," said Mara Vey, "and I want it written down that you said it once and I have asked you not to say it again, and I have not asked you one question about her, and I have not asked you a single one, and I want that on the page as well, and the reason I want it on the page is that I have noticed over about two months that not asking is a thing I do, and I have started doing it for me and not for the office, and I do not intend to stop, and I do intend to be able to prove that I did it on purpose."

---

And then he said the thing he had come to say, which he had been not saying for two days on a road, and it came out in about four seconds and it was the sentence he had said in a gateway in Embermonth with four men in it and it had not improved on the road and it was never going to.

"I wanted to have been careful once," he said, "while somebody was watching. That is the whole of what I have wanted since I was twenty-two. I have been in this office four years and I have been careful every day of the four years and nobody has ever watched me do it, and being careful when nobody is watching turns out to be the cheapest thing a young man can do with his life, and I have just come back off a road having spent three days and my own money and a career to be careful in front of a man who was not watching either, and I have got a sheet of paper out of it, and the sheet of paper is a good piece of work, and I am going to be a man who filed a return that answered nothing and I am not going to be anything else as long as I live."

He put his hat back on.

"That is the whole of my want," he said. "It is not a want anybody would put in a book."

"It is a want a keeper of records would put in a book," said Mara Vey, "and I am not going to, because it is yours and not mine, and if I enter a man's want in my day-book then in nine years I have written a document about a person and I will not do it about you, because you are the only person in this case who has refused to hold a piece of information for me, and I am not going to be the reason you lose that."

---

The lane door made its noise again at about the fourth hour, and it was a boy of fourteen with a letter, and the letter had come by the county post and the county post had brought it from the north that morning, and it had been in the bag for three days.

Mara Vey took it and read the outside of it and then went the colour of the wall and then went back to the colour she usually is.

"It is dated," she said.

"It is dated," said Sarn Oyelaran.

"It came today and it is dated the twelfth, and the post from the north takes three days in a hard month and two and a half in a soft one, and the twelfth is a Friday, and it has been in that bag since Friday morning." She turned it over twice and did not open it, and both the other people in the room let her do that for about fifteen seconds. "I want to say the thing about that before I open it, and I want both of you to hear it, because I am going to think about it for a month and I want the words to be in this room now while the two of you are here."

"Go on," said Sarn Oyelaran.

"The gap between the day a thing is dated and the day a thing arrives is a fact, and it is the only fact in this business that nobody can hurry and nobody can argue with and nobody can do anything about," said Mara Vey. "It is three days. It was three days on the fourth of Fallowmonth and it is three days on the fifteenth of Wolfmonth and it will be three days in Greenmonth, and everything else in this case can be fought and every other thing in it can be refused. **I have been a keeper of records for four years and a half and the only thing I have never been able to do anything about is how long it takes a piece of paper to get here.**"

She opened it.

It was one sheet, printed, of the same series as everything else, and the address was to her office and not to her by name, and it was signed, and the signature was a woman's and was not a clerk's initial.

The request was for a figure.

Not for a name. Not for a sheet. Not for a person who could be produced and made to answer. A **figure of persons** in this county who had had knowledge of a declared object, to be returned on the form provided, on or before the ninth of Frostmonth.

And under the space for the figure there was the four words again. *How the figure was compiled.*

Mara Vey read it twice, and then she set it down on the long table with the printed side up, and put her hand flat on the blank space beside it, and did not say anything at all for about half a minute.

"Well," she said at last. "That is a very good instrument."

"That is the second one in a year," said Sarn Oyelaran.

"That is the second one in a year and the first one that has a day on it, and it is signed, and it is signed by a woman whose name I have had in my head for about ten minutes and who has just asked me for a number instead of asking me for a person." She took her hand off the paper. "And here is what I am not going to do, and I want it entered, so I am going to say it out loud and then I am going to write it in the book and I am going to write down the fact that I did not write down the rest of it."

---

She wrote the entry at about the seventh hour, in the day-book, on a page, and it read:

*Fifteenth of Wolfmonth, YR 312, a Friday, at the seventh hour. Received: one sheet of the series of the Office of Provincial Registration, signed, dated at the place of issue the twelfth of this month, asking this office to return a figure of the persons in this county who have had knowledge of a declared object, with the words* how the figure was compiled *under the figure.*

*Entered against the office and against no person. No claim number is entered against a person because no person has been named in it and none will be.*

*The instrument is lawful. It is dated, which the last one was not. It is signed, which nothing that has come into this room from that office has been. It asks for a number and not for a name, and a number is a fact about a county and a name is a fact about a person, and this office can return the first and has spent four years refusing the second.*

*And a figure is not a list. A list with the names taken off is a number, and this office has said that sentence in a room of forty people, and it is the whole of my trade, and I am entering it here again because it is about to be tested by somebody who is not me.*

*No opinion is entered. The not writing the opinion is entered, and I have learned in Fallowmonth that the not writing is the part of it that is a decision, and a decision that is not written down is a thing a person did once and can be asked about by a clerk in nine years, and I will not have it.*

*There is a figure in the front of this office that would answer this instrument. I am not entering what it is, and the reason I am not entering it is not confidentiality. The reason is that I do not yet know whether I have got it right, and a keeper who enters a want where she has a fact is a mouth and not a cell, and I have written that down twice this year and I am writing it down a third time and it is going to become a habit and habits are the only thing in this business that nobody can audit.*

She put the pen down and read it and did not improve it.

"Returnable by the ninth of Frostmonth," she said. "That is twenty-four days and I have twenty-four days to find out what a count of persons is, and I have a fairly strong idea that the answer is going to be a figure I already have, and I have a stronger idea that the moment I put that figure on that sheet it will be true for the rest of my life in a way that I will not be able to undo, and I would like somebody in this room to tell me that I am wrong about that."

"You are not wrong about that," said Sarn Oyelaran.

"I know. I asked anyway," said Mara Vey, "because a room with two people in it and a question in it is worth four with three, and I am going to bed."
