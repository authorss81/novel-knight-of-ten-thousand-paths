# Chapter 0060

The ninth of Embermonth was a Thursday and it was a market day, and the hour was the fourth, and the day was written at the head of a page in a book on a north bank four hundred miles off, in the roll of the Merefen bridge guild, in the day-book of a filing room over a weigh-house, and in two slates, one at each end, and there is no fifth place and the fifth place is the mistake everybody makes.

The line was made up at the third hour in the gateway of the yard on Weir Street, and the making of it took nine minutes, and every one of the nine was in the yard's book afterwards with a date and an hour on it, because Toban's rule is that nothing that belongs to a man goes in the yard's book and only a thing set.

The line was a thing set.

A rope, four fathoms, spliced at both ends, and the splice at this end was cut in a yard in Fallowmonth out of a cooper's bin and the splice at the far end was cut by a ferrywright on a river that is not in this county, and the two of them are not the same splice and were not meant to be. It is not the anchor. The anchor is forty yards of tarred hemp through the abutment on a splice out of the same bin, and that is a different thing and it is four feet off, and men have confused the two before and a man who confuses them is a man who cannot say which end of what he is holding. Two hands hold the line. A date on it. And a witness at each end who is not the person keeping the road, and if the witness at an end is not there on the day then the road does not open, and it does not fail.

That is the whole of the arrangement and it took four generations on one bank of a river and eleven months and a writ of enquiry on this one to arrive at, and it is four lines on a sheet of paper, and the sheet is the return, and here it is, because the return is the document on which the arrangement is written down and a thing that is not written down is a door.

*Return, first schedule. The ninth of Embermonth, YR 312, at the fourth hour, and the tide in it.*

*Kept at this end by: T. Kest, carpenter, of this town, who keeps the crossings of this guild and is not the man who cut the seat and does not want to be.*

*Kept at the other end by: S. Quen, ferrywright, of the north bank, whose consent is hers and not a bench's and cannot be argued with in a hall.*

*Witness at this end, who is not the person keeping the road: —*

*Witness at the other end, who is not the person keeping the road: —*

"There," said Tovan Kest, and put the flat of his hand on the last line. "That is the whole of it and it is one line and it is empty, and that line is the only reason I could tell you at eleven o'clock last night that today was going to be what it is going to be, and I did not tell you, and I am going to tell you now before the fourth hour, because I have thought about it since two o'clock this morning and I have decided that the not telling has run out."

He had the yard's book open on the bench-board and his hand was not on the last line any more but on the page before it.

"Read that one out," said Aren Kest.

"I have never read it out," said Tovan Kest. "That is why I am telling you I am not asking. I have had it in that book since the second of Fallowmonth with a rule over it and I have not read it out to a person in two months and I wrote it for nobody, and writing a thing for nobody is the same as not writing it, and it turns out I did not know that until this morning."

"Then it was not for nobody," said Aren Kest. "Read it out."

---

*Eighth of Greenmonth, YR 312, at about the eleventh hour at night, on a parapet at the near abutment, and he had a lamp of his own and he would not come closer than a man needs to, and he asked me for the bit I had written for myself, because he said he was a carpenter and he knew what a man writes when he is writing to himself, and I read it out, and I read it twice, and I gave it to him, and he signed it at the foot with the hour and put it in the yard's book on the ninth page of the week, and I went up Weir Street.*

*He read me the whole of it. Every day of it and the hour and the seat and the two discs and the maker and what he was going to do and what he would promise and what he would not. And he did not ask me one thing. Not one, in four hours, standing on a stone in the dark, to a man who is seven years younger and has been his brother since either of them can remember. And I have had two months to work out why and the best I have is that he was not asking me for anything. He was telling me. And there is a difference between a man who tells you a thing and a man who asks you for one, and I have not been able to say in words what the difference is, and I have read that page a great many times.*

*I have written it in this book with a rule over it, and the rule is mine and not the office's, and the reason is that I do not want a man to be able to come into this yard and read a thing about his brother out of my week-book without me standing here while he reads it.*

He read it out with the yard going on round him, and the man at the drawknife stopped drawing again, and the two men on the grindstone stopped, because a yard is quiet the way a room is quiet, and nobody said anything when he had finished.

"Right," said Tovan Kest. "That is the eighth of Greenmonth. And the thing I have not said since the eighth of Greenmonth is the reason I have not asked you where you go, and I am going to say it now, and it is not a virtue, and I have told you already that it is a trade, and I have come to the end of the trade this morning."

"Go on."

"You went out on the first of Fallowmonth at the fourth hour and you came back on the second and I have wanted to ask you every day since the first, and I have not asked, and the reason I have not is not that I do not want to know." He shut the book. "The reason is that the day I ask is the day you have to answer. And a man who has to answer is a man who is going to think about the answer on the far side of a thing instead of about the work, and I have watched you come back off that sill unable to stand and I have read that page every morning since the second of Fallowmonth and I have known what you were doing and I have known that it cost you three days of your own clock and a morning in a room over a cooper, and I have let you have it for a month, and I am not going to let you have it on a market day when there are four hundred people in this town who would rather I did not."

"That is a good reason," said Aren Kest. "And it is not true."

"No," said Tovan Kest. "It is the reason I have."

And then the reeve came round the corner of the yard with a sheet and a rule under her arm, because a reeve is at a market at the third hour on a Thursday and has been for nine years, and she came into the gateway and looked at the return on the bench-board and read the last line with her eyes, standing up, in about two seconds, the way she reads everything.

"Sign the third line," she said.

"Two lines," said Tovan Kest. "There is a second witness at the fourth hour. I have not got him yet."

"Then get him, and it is not me, and I am going to tell you why and I am going to say it once and I am not going to want it minuted." She put the rule down on the board. "I am the reeve of this town. A reeve is the town's witness. That is what the word means and I have been it for nine years and I have wanted to vote on this guild's own matter for nine years and I have not, and I will not stand on that line today, because a road this town does not own cannot have this town as its witness, and if it has, then in four years somebody will say the town stood there, and the town will be right and the road will be shut, and the road will have been shut by a signature and not by an argument, and an argument is a thing that can be had again on a different day by a different bench with a different temper in it."

Nobody in the gateway said anything, and the man at the drawknife said, "That is the best thing anybody in this town has said in a year," and the reeve said, "It is the first thing I have said in nine years that anybody in this town has written down," and Tovan Kest wrote it down.

---

So he went into the market and came back with him at about twenty minutes to the fourth hour.

He was forty-seven and unemployed and had not been paid for anything since the fourth of Greenmonth and had not been promoted to anything and was not going to be, and he had a coat that had been good in Greenmonth, and he stood in a gateway where he had been told on the fourth of Embermonth that he was one of eleven people in a county who could say no and be believed, and he said, before he said anything else:

"I am not going to say anything about the fee for this."

"Fourpence a week," said Tovan Kest. "The witness is fourpence a week and it is paid out of the yard's own box and not out of the guild's, and it is the first time I have paid anybody in this yard for turning up. And I have costed it, because I cost everything. This road is kept on the fourth and the ninth, which is two days a month, which is twenty-four days a year, and a man who stands on two days a month stands on about thirty-two weeks of them, and thirty-two weeks at fourpence is a hundred and twenty-eight pence, which is ten shillings and eightpence, and I have added that in pence twice and I am not going to add it a third time."

"Then I will take the fourpence a week and I will not draw it, and both of us will enter that." Bram Ottery took the stub of pencil and signed the third line of the return, and his name went on it in a watch sergeant's hand, which is a small neat upright hand that has signed nine hundred things and none of them is a document, because a watch sergeant signs a book and not a paper, and here is a paper and he has signed it, and the two are not the same trade and he does not intend to be told so.

"You know what the rule is," said Aren Kest. "If you are not here on the day, it does not open."

"I have been in this town nineteen years and I have watched four hundred people cross that bridge on that deck and I have never once been on it," said Bram Ottery. "That is what I am here for. I wrote a true thing at my own table at the second hour of the second of Greenmonth and I brought it in without being asked and it cost me my post and nine months and three weeks and a notice, and I have not been sorry for one hour of it and I have not been thanked for one hour of it either, and that is the whole of my life as far as this county is concerned." He put the pencil down. "So I will tell you the reason I am standing in this gateway and I will tell it once and it is a bad reason and it is the only one I have got. I would like to stand somewhere, once, while a true thing is being done. That is all. Not a road. Not a crossing. One morning, in a gateway, and there will be a thing on a piece of paper and it will be true, and I will have been part of holding it up."

"Aren," said Tovan Kest. "Write that down."

"I am not writing it down," said Aren Kest, "and neither are you, and it is the only sentence spoken in this yard in five months that does not go in a book, and that is exactly why I am not going to let it be the only one."

And he wrote it down, in the flat hand, in the back of the notebook, on the bench-board, in a gateway, with the market going on outside, and Tovan Kest entered in the yard's book that the witness was taken and the witness's fourpence a week and the hour, and entered nothing else, because a yard holds a thing set and not a thing believed, and a man's reason for standing in a gateway is a thing believed, and a yard is not an office.

---

The bell went at about a quarter to the fourth hour, rung by a boy of fifteen on the near abutment, alone, because the far end was not there and had not said so, and the third stroke was not struck, it was leaned on, and it was leaned on because a woman four hundred miles off had told them in a book that it was leaned on now, and that the leaning is shorter and takes less time, and they had stopped saying that it was cracked.

The sill was under the boy's feet the whole time and nobody looked at it. It is a dressed block and forty yards of tarred hemp through the abutment on a splice cut out of a cooper's bin, and the fourth course above it is open the whole depth of the joint the width of a hand, in register, and scarred, and there is no glass in this county and there will be no third seat, and the stone was cold.

The line went out at the fourth hour, and Tovan Kest held the near end of it standing in the gateway on Weir Street with his brother on the steps of the Old Toll crossing, and the yard went on behind them, because a yard goes on.

Nothing came.

That is the whole of what Merefen knows about it. The hemp did not slacken and it did not tighten and the tide came up the channel at about the fifth hour and went out again, and four hundred people went to a market and came back from a market, and about the second hour a boy who works on the west channel said in a doorway that the warden had been seen at the crossing at the fourth hour and had gone off north with a roll of paper in his coat, and the boy who said it had it from a carrier who had it from two boys, and by the third doorway the story had a man with a bar in it, and by the fourth it had two people sent, which is what a story does when it gets to be about a man who is sent to places.

At the sixth hour the reeve wrote to the toll-holder's clerk and the clerk wrote to the north bank, and on the eleventh a line came back in the fair hand of the woman who keeps the crossing book, and it said that the road had not opened at this end either, and that it had not failed, and that it would be kept for the fourth and the ninth as before, and no more.

---

And the things that were not done that day, in a town that has a book for everything:

The scour under the nose of the third pier at Sedge Reach, which is a washtub and has been a washtub since Mudmonth, and whose honest repair is eleven tons of stone and a fortnight with the road shut, was not read that day, and the bench's mark-day reading was not taken, and the reason both of those are one sentence is that the man who has read that scour twice in this year went out of this county at the fourth hour of the market morning and did not come back for twenty-six days. The bench has one man with a rule in it and the rule is in a leather tube and the number on the ring was a quarter of an inch low in Longlight, and a bench of nine that has never compared its own two numbers a year now has a third day to wait for.

The yard has four weeks and a day in it and it is spoken for, and the factor's stone is gone and a county town's wall is going, and there is a cart in the mud by the mill that has been ignored since Fallowmonth, and the man who has been the reason there is any work in it for five months was not in it.

And three shillings a week is in the county's hands, three Fridays of it, which is nine shillings, from a copying post that a man took on the sixteenth of Fallowmonth in exchange for a schedule of forty-one inspections that he refused, and he has not been to the survey office, and a man who is owed three Fridays is a man who has been somewhere.

---

He read the paper of the eighth again at about half past four, standing in the gateway with the day's sheet in his hand, and the fourth line of it was the one that the yard had read out loud, and he read it out a second time himself, and four men heard it, and it went into the yard's book because a thing that a man reads out in a gateway is a thing set.

The top of it had two names on it and a date. The first name is the hand of the Ninebridge and he has it and has never met her. The second is a person the county has chosen to stand at its end of a line, and her paper gave him that person's name, and he wrote it in the notebook in the order it was written and has not used it once, because a name a county hands a stranger is a person he has no use for and a person he has no use for is a person he will be in a room with, and he would rather find out which one it was from the county's own mouth in a market. The date on it was *the nineteenth day of the rolling, being the sixth day after the low water, the year counted from the first firing*, and he copied that into the notebook in the flat hand with the hour against it, in full, in the order it was written, and he wrote underneath it that he had copied it exactly and that he could not check one word of it, and that a man in a filing room in Merefen was going to read a copy of that line in about seven months and would not be able to tell from the line which side of it the year was on.

And the fourth line was this.

*We were asked a question by a stranger and we have written our answer down, and we do not know the answer, and the writing of it is the answer.*

Then he folded it and put it in the notebook, and he said out loud, to four men in a gateway on a Thursday, the thing he had said on a bridge in a market town four hundred miles off and had said to nobody else and had not been asked to say anywhere.

"She did not open it and she was right, and I am not the man who sends anybody, and it made no difference, which is how I know she was right. And they have written down the names of the two people who would hold a line, without being asked, and they have got the date wrong and it is their own date and not ours, and they have told us they do not know the answer, and I have been in this county eleven years and that is the first time anybody has ever told me they do not know something and put it in a book and sent it to me on purpose."

"So are you going?"

"There is no road," said Aren Kest. "There is a county, on the other side of a bridge, with a market on it, and a woman on it with a set of tile-cutters' tools who told me to get off her kerb, and there are two names at the top of a piece of paper and a date in their own calendar and no office in it anywhere, and I am going over that bridge on my own legs at the fifth hour like a man walking into a market town, and nobody is sending me, and I will be back when the work is done and not before, and I am telling you all four of you that, in a gateway, on a Thursday, on a market day, in daylight, and there are about nine hundred people in this town who will hear about it by the middle of next week, and I have said it in a gateway with four men in it because that is the only way a man in this county says a true thing."

He went over the Ninebridge at about the fifth hour on his own legs, behind a woman in a leather apron with a roll of tools at her hip, and he did not go fast, and a market that is a market does not get out of the way of a man and does not have to.

In the first street of Kilnholt, about an hour later, the man with the ledger and the satchel caught up with him, and he did not ask his name again, and he said:

"I have a mark to put against a person and I cannot get your name and I am not going to ask you a third time, and I will tell you why I asked twice and did not ask three times, because you have been here a day and you have been right about it, which nobody has ever been about anything. Once is a clerk. Twice is an officer. A clerk is asking a question and an officer is establishing a fact, and there is a difference and it is four hundred years old and I am the only man in this county who knows it is there."

"And what do you want?" said Aren Kest.

"I want a trade," said the man with the ledger, "because I have to put a trade against a hearth, and there is no room on this page for nothing, and if I leave it blank then this county's figure is short by one and I count this county and not myself, and I have never in nineteen years been short, and I would like to know what you are for."

"I am a sett-cutter," said Aren Kest. "I cut setts and I set them in a road, and I read where the load goes, and I am not good enough to be trusted with the second thing and I am the only man anybody has got for it at the moment, and it is a market day in a fen county and I have said it in a room in another country and I do not expect to be believed in either of them."

The man with the ledger wrote it down in a column headed *trade*, in a hand that had got worse over nineteen years, and then he came up half a step and asked the next question, which was the one he had to ask, because a trade belongs to a hearth and a hearth belongs to a county.

"And the county?"

"There is no county," said Aren Kest. "Or there is one and it is not on any map in this world, and I have looked, and I went to a filing room with a woman who has a press and spent a shilling on a map she did not need, and I would write it down if I could and I cannot, and I have written *north*."

He wrote *north* in a column headed *county*, and it was the first entry in that column in nineteen years that was not a place anybody in that county could walk to, and he wrote it without asking again, and then he put the ledger under his arm and looked at the next line on the page, which was empty, and which was headed *hand*, and he did not put the pencil on it.

"No," said Aren Kest.

"No," said the man with the ledger. "It is a column and it will have got nothing in it, and you have been here a day and you have already watched somebody do that, and I have not got a name for what the two of you are doing and I have got a full page." He went along the kerb with the black thumb going from tile to tile, counting, the way a man does who counts fires and does not count himself.
