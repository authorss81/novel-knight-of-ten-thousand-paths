# Chapter 0069

The nineteenth day of his twenty-three was the fortieth day of the rolling and it was the fourth day of the week he had asked for, and he spent it in a shed at the edge of a village with about eleven men in it, and his whole business was carrying boards.

That is the whole of what he did for about nine hours. Two men went round the hearths of that village with a list of names on a sheet of a copying book, and a third man asked, and the asking was the whole of the work, and it was the first piece of civic work in this county that nobody had had to invent a form for.

"Are you on this list?"

"I am."

"Have you been on it since the autumn?"

"Since the autumn."

"Have you been asked before this, by anybody, in any year, whether you are on a list?"

"No."

And then the second man wrote the answer in his own book, in his own hand, and shut the book, and they went on to the next house, and nobody made a copy and nobody put a name into anything.

It took about a minute and a half a house and it is the only work Aren Kest did in the County of Ash in twenty-three days, and it is the only work in this volume that nobody has ever written down, and the two men who did it in that village are trades — a cord-cutter and a man who drove ash-wood — and they did it in their own time and were not paid and had not been asked to do it by anybody with a stove in it. The hand of the Ninebridge had gone round the four villages she could reach in two days on foot and had told two men in each that a stranger was in the county and was going to carry boards.

"That is what you are for," she had said. "Not the tiles. The boards. A man who comes into a shed and asks nobody anything for nine hours is the only kind of man anybody in this county has ever been glad to see, and I have had nine years of men who came in and wanted to talk."

---

On the evening of that day a kiln-man came over the Ninebridge on a barrow with a thing under his arm, and the hand of the Ninebridge was on the fourth span, and she took the thing from the barrow and carried it to the middle of the bridge and stood it against the parapet on the dry side.

It was a fired tile, six inches by four, the size of any other tile in this county, and it was grey, and it had five lines on the face in a script that was not a clerk's and was not a die's, and it had been pressed with a stick into clay before the fire.

"The Kilnholt kiln," she said. "Nine lines cut for it and I have had it four hours and I have read it nine times. It came from a room, not from a person. That is what I am going to tell you first, because you will want to know who sent it and nobody sent it, and if that is not the first thing you hear you will spend a night on it."

The fire was in a fire-basket on the parapet of the third span and the market had gone and there was nobody on the bridge but the two of them and a dog on the far bank.

"Read it out," said Aren Kest.

"No."

"You have read it nine times."

"I have read it nine times and I have not read it out, and that is not because of you. I have not read it out because I have never in nine years read out a thing a room gave me, because in this county a room gives a person a thing and the person says it, and I have never been the person." She put her hand flat on the tile. "You are a stranger. A stranger in a room in this county is the only thing that has ever been given a thing and not been the one it was given to. Read it out and you will be the first man in nineteen years who has stood on this bridge and said a room's words, and in nine years there will be a clerk in a county that is not this one who has got a copy of what you said, and it will be your words in his file, and not one person in this county will have said them here."

"So you want me to be the man who says it."

"I want somebody to be the man who says it and I do not care which," said the hand of the Ninebridge, "and you are here, and you have been here nineteen days, and you have not asked me one question about the road. So read it out, and read it exactly, and if you want to stop reading it out halfway then stop, and I will not say anything about it, and I will put the tile back in the barrow myself."

He read it out.

---

*1. The road is not to be made to carry a person.*

*2. No name is to be read out on the far side of the line by anybody from the north; and no name is to be written in the line's book at the north end.*

*3. The north is not to send anybody who can be asked to come and look.*

*4. A person who is struck may answer; and if a person who is struck answers, the person is restored.*

*5. The road is kept by a hand at each end and a witness at each end; and the witness at Ash's end is chosen by Ash.*

Six inches by four, and five lines on it, and the second one had to be in three sizes of writing because it was forty-one words and the rest were fifteen, and the stick had been a broken piece of a saw, and the whole of it had been pressed by somebody's thumb in about a quarter of an hour, and it had been fired in a rolling that was a week old.

The tile came from a room. A tile cannot be copied out of: to take a mark off a fired tile you chip it, and a chipped tile is a tile with a hole in it and a hole in a fired tile is a permanent account of a man having wanted a copy. So a thing on a tile can be read by anybody who turns it over with a heel and cannot be taken out of by anybody at all. And a book can be copied, and a copy in a book is a thing that a court can have, and a thing that a court can have is a thing that can be produced.

So there was a tile, which could not be copied out of, and a book at the assizes with the same five lines in it, which anybody with a warrant could have a copy of and anybody with a chisel could take the original out of.

"They have understood that in four years," said Aren Kest, "and nobody in the north has understood it in eleven."

"What is it?"

"That a thing nobody can quietly take out of the book, and a thing everybody can be shown, in the same room, and the two of them agreeing, and the tile being the one that cannot be lifted." He was quiet for a moment. "There is a woman four hundred miles off who has got one room out of four and has said out loud that a lock-up on a wharf is a thing a creditor could sell, and she has written that she cannot order anybody to make another one. I have read her hand on a page. They have worked it out in four years with clay and she has got it in eleven months with paper and neither of them has got the other's half."

"Which of them is right?"

"They are the same and she would be astonished at the comparison and I am not going to be the man who makes it for her," he said, "and I have no business in it, and I have been in this county nineteen days and I am going to be in it four more and then I am going to be in a fen with a fever on me and a woman with a day-book in front of me who is not going to ask me anything. That is the whole of my arrangements and I can feel it getting longer every day and I have not written any of it down."

---

He worked the fourth term out with her on the bridge, in about forty minutes, standing at the parapet, and she got half of it and he got the other half and neither of them put the two halves together, and it took the whole of the next two days for him to work out what he had actually said.

"A person who is struck may answer, and if a person who is struck answers, the person is restored." He got the thumb out and put it on the fourth line. "That is a rule. That is a rule you could put on a door. And a rule needs a way to be carried out, and the way it is carried out in this county is the law, and the law says a person is on the roll if two other persons witness them and their tile agrees with the entry. So to put a struck person back you have to witness them, and to witness them you have to read the roll, because the roll is where the witnesses come out of — a witness is a name in the roll, and there is nowhere else in this county a person's name is kept that can be asked to say something."

"So you re-read the roll."

"You re-read the roll at the person. You cannot do it any other way. The book has to be in the room, because the witnesses' names are in the book, and the person's tile has to be on the table, because the tile has to agree with the entry, and the two witnesses have to be people whose names are in the book, and they have to be in the room. And that is it. That is the whole of the fourth term, and it is the best sentence anybody has said in this county since the roll was begun, and it is going to cost you two hundred and six days and it is going to cost the county the only clerk it has."

"You have said it as though it were a small thing."

"It is a small thing," said Aren Kest. "It is a small thing and it is the size of a room. That is what I did not see for about a day and a half. It is not a sentence. **It is a room, with a book in it, and two hundred people to go through it, one at a time, and the whole of it resting on one book in one room in one building.**"

"That is a room," she said. "A bench would do it. A shed would do it."

"A bench would do it and a bench would be wrong," he said, "and I could tell you why in a fortnight and I have not got a fortnight, and I have got four days and I am going to spend them on this."

He did spend them on it, but not out loud, and not on the bridge, and the two days were spent at the low end of the rack with a wet cloth in his hand, re-pressing tiles in four hundred yards of a county's only non-argument, and the notebook was on his knee.

---

The tile went back in the barrow on the twentieth day and the book stayed at the assizes, and he did not see the book, and he was not asked to sign anything, and nobody in that county asked him for a single thing in four hundred miles' worth of days. He carried boards, and he re-pressed tiles, and he was useful, and he was not a visitor, and at the end of the fourth day of it he went and sat on the low stool in the cutting shed at the north end of the Ninebridge with a board on his knee that he did not need to re-press, and he opened the notebook to the back pages, and he wrote the following, and it is the entry that matters, and it is in his own hand, and nobody in either country has ever read it.

---

*Term four. Nineteenth day of this. I have been two days on it and I am going to write down what I have got, in the words of the trade, and I am going to write it down now and not in four months, because the reason I am writing it now is that I have noticed that the reason I did not write it down the first time was so that I would not have read it, and that is the same trick twice, and a man who is using the same trick twice is not being careful, he is being frightened.*

*Set out the parts of it, because it is not a conversation. It is a working and it is a working at a scale of two hundred.*

*Anchor — the roll. One book, one room, one building, in a county of nine hundred and forty-one hearths. It is the only thing in this county that does not move and it is the thing the whole of the fourth term rests on.*

*Witness — the person answering, and two whose names are in the book. The rule in this trade is that the witness is not the man doing the working and is whoever would have refused. Here there is nobody who would have refused. I asked the keeper of the roll to her own face in a room with a physician in it whether she would refuse and she said no, in front of me, in one word, and she is right that she would not refuse, and the reason she would not refuse is that she is the anchor. **A witness that is also the anchor is not a witness. It is a second reading of the same hand.***

*Counterweight — the county. Nine hundred and forty-one hearths and the cost of a week of wedging and forty men losing a day. I have costed the tile part and she has costed the road part and neither of us has costed the part where the book is wrong about all of it at once.*

*Bound — and here is the whole of it, and here is the thing I am going to be sorry for — **the promise that these two hundred are the people.** Two hundred people to one office. The county's claim is that the roll is right. The bound is the roll. The roll is in one room. If the roll is right then one woman in Kilnholt is the only person in this county who can be wrong about two hundred human beings, and if she is wrong about two hundred human beings then there is nothing in the world that says she is not also wrong about the other two thousand two hundred and fifty, and a bound that goes all the way through a structure is not a bound. A bound has to be the narrowest thing you can make it. The width of the split, the length of the timber. This one is the whole of the county and it is not narrow and I have made it and I know the size of it.*

*A bound is a promise, and a promise about two hundred people to one office is a shape I have had refused before me by a man in a room in another county, in the last week of the fourth month of this year. I gave him a shape and he would not give it and I did not ask him twice and I have been sorry for it for nine weeks. The shape I gave him was a paper about a number of persons in a place that could not be asked a question. I have since found out that there is an office and a form and a column on the form with a word at the top of it, and I have half of the other half of that, and I am not going to write the rest of it down.*

*I do not know what it is. I have the shape of a thing and I have not got the thing, and I have decided on the fourth day of a week in a clay county, four hundred miles from any room I know anything about, to leave it, and I am going to write down that I decided to leave it, and if a clerk in Merefen ever reads this line he is to understand that it is a decision and not an oversight, and I made it in the knowledge that half of a thing is enough to build with.*

*Figure. A working at that scale wants a figure and I have not got one.*

*I do not know what a person weighs in a working. I have set a deck for forty tons on a hand's width of oak twice in my life and I could tell you to the pound what that is. I have no idea at all what a man is. I am going to say two tons each, which is a number I had off a barrow of setts in Greenmonth and for which I have no reason of any kind, and two hundred of them is four hundred tons, and four hundred tons into a bed of river gravel at Ninebridge is a thing I can point at and say *there, that is what this is*, and it is on the right side of four hundred and it is the correct order of size for a thing I do not understand.*

*A document's load is not in tons. I know that. I have written it in tons anyway.*

*That is the sentence. That is the one to read in four months' time, and I am writing it now so that the man who reads it cannot say he did not have it in front of him. In four months, or thereabouts, which is the same thing at this rate and I have not done the sum because the sum is in two calendars and I have not got both of them, and the one I have got does not have a week in it.*

*I am going to build it anyway. That is written down as well, because I have written the mistake down before and read it and gone on, and it is the second time, and the thing I have noticed today, and the only new thing in this book today, is that **it is the same mistake both times and noticing it has never once stopped me, and a man who has noticed a thing twice is not being careful, he is being consistent.** That is not a trade sentence. I do not know what it is. I am putting it in this book because it is in here and the book is on my knee.*

*And the honest end of it, which I am writing because I have been in this county twenty days and I owe this book one true thing about myself: on the first day she asked me a question and I gave her the correct answer and she told me to get off her kerb, and it cost her nothing to be right and it cost me the road. I have not gone back to check. A man who goes back to check is a man asking to be let in. If the bound is what I think it is, then the nine months I am going to spend in this county is nine months the road is shut, and I am going to find that out in a month, in a market, from somebody who has it from a boy who has it from a carrier, which is how everything I have ever found out has come to me.*

*Closed the book. There are about four hundred soft tiles in this county between here and Kilnholt and one pair of hands and mine, and I am going to do the bottom of the rack next, and it is the best work I have had in five months, and nobody in this county is going to hear a word about any of the above from me, ever, in a room, with a clerk in it, ever again.*

---

On the twenty-second day the sittings of the assizes sat, and the list of two hundred and six names was a year old and had been a year old for the whole of the week, and the law permitted it to be burned in the market square in front of witnesses, and it was not burned, and the reason it was not burned is that the clerk of the roll was asked in the magistrate's own room, in the presence of no stranger, and said no, and gave a reason, and the magistrate accepted it in about eleven seconds.

Her reason was not the fever and it was not the physician's four pages. It was that two hundred and six fired tiles broken up and wedged back is a day and a half of a wedging shed, and that in about eleven years somebody who is not her is going to have to fire a roll for a person who has been struck and put back, and there is going to be a mark on that roll, and she would like the county to have spent the day and a half.

That is the whole of the first refusal in this county that worked, and the reason it worked is that it was not a moral sentence, and it was made by a woman with a trade, about clay.

---

He was on the bridge on the twenty-third day, in the morning, with the tile, and there was a second tile in the barrow.

The second tile was small, four inches by three, and it had no lines on it, and it had a press on the face of it, and the press was a hand laid flat on a book, and it had the same kiln's mark on the back of it as the tile of the terms.

"That is not a bell," said the hand of the Ninebridge. "That is a tile off a kiln in this county and it has been on the coping of my second span since before I could walk and I have asked two men in this county what it is and one of them said his grandfather put it there and the other one said he did not know, and the man whose grandfather put it there has been dead since the eighth of the last rolling. I have had it in a barrow since the fifth hour this morning and I have not put it back and I am not going to, and I am not going to tell a magistrate either, because he would take it into a room and a room is where a thing like that ends up."

"Then why have you got it out?"

"Because a man came out of a road twenty-three days ago and stood on my kerb on the first morning and looked at it from two hundred yards in bad light and did not say one word about it," she said, "and I have had twenty-three days to work out that a man who looks at a thing and says nothing about it is a man who knows what it is, and I have not asked him, and I am not going to ask him, and that is not a courtesy either, it is that I have a barrow and four hundred yards of kerb and a county to feed and I have not got time to be the woman who pulls a stranger's arm about a piece of clay in a market town."

He did not say it. He had it in a notebook in a coat, on a piece of paper, written in a hand that is not a clerk's and in a calendar he cannot check, and he had had it for twenty-three days and had never once seen the woman it belonged to, and if he used it now then every hour he had spent on her bridge would be worth nothing, and the refusal she gave him on the first morning would turn out to have been a thing he had been holding the answer to since the morning before it, and there is no worse way to lose a refusal than to arrive at it already holding the key.

So he looked at the small tile in the barrow, and the mark on it, and the mark he had seen four times in eleven years in three countries, and he said:

"That is a good tile. That is a well-pressed one. The face is true and it has not been re-pressed since it was made and whoever cut that die knew what he was about."

"That is all you are going to say about it."

"That is everything I know about it," said Aren Kest.

And then, in the middle of a market town in a county of nine hundred and forty-one hearths, on the twenty-third day of twenty-three, four hundred miles from a fen, a man took a fired clay tile off a barrow, turned it over once, and put it back face up, and did not touch the rope that was on it, because the rope was not part of it.

"Take it back on the coping," he said. "It is not doing any harm where it is and there is a woman in this county who has known about it for forty-one years and has never once asked anybody to take it down, and she is right, and I would like it on the record that I said so in a room with nobody in it."

He went down the clay steps on the side he had come up and put his hand on the coping on the way down, and the stone was cold, and there was no warmth in it whatever, and that is a fact he wrote down about a hand and a habit four months later, and did not know was a fact yet.

Behind him on the coping of the second span there was a rope on a tile and no yoke, and the rope was about a year old, and the tile was older than anybody alive who had ever touched it, and in a county that had never once refused anything, and had therefore never once rung anything, it hung exactly where a thing hangs that somebody put up for no reason at all.
