# Chapter 0079

By the eleventh week the ninety cords had not been cut, and nobody in the County of Ash was surprised, and two of the four kilns stood idle, and it turned out that a county with three kilns and a kiln trade is a different county from a county with four kilns and a kiln trade in about nine weeks.

Nobody had argued against the price. That is what he kept coming back to for about half of the eleventh week. Two hundred people in a drying floor, a column on a board, a figure that added up in pence, and not one voice in ninety-one feet saying *you are wrong*. The man at the cutting bench had said a price and sat down. The woman had said what the price was for and had shaken off a hand. The clerk of the roll had refused to read a book out loud in a room for the second time in nineteen years and had been right twice.

And the ash-woods had not been cut, because there is no office in this county that can put forty men in a wood for a day and pay them for it, and the man who could have put them there was four days away at the assizes with a boundary, with a paper on a table in Kilnholt that said *I am not giving this room an instruction*, and the paper was read out in a drying floor and nobody argued with it, and the wood stayed uncut for four more weeks, and by the eleventh week the two kilns at the two villages had no cord and could not have a cord, and the question was not whether they stopped.

The question was how a kiln stops.

A rolling kiln that is charged is nine days of clay, and clay that is charged and not fired does not wait. It has to go back into the wedging shed, and the wedging shed is a room with a floor and a bank and about nine feet of headroom, and it is the scarcest room in this county, and the reason it is the scarcest room is that the clay in it is the only thing this county has that is not in a building anybody can take.

It is also the room the clerk of the roll kept a list out of, in a corridor, nine years ago, for a reason she gave in a breath, and then again in a room with a stranger and a physician in it on the sixteenth day of the first rolling, and the reason was a day and a half of a wedging shed.

Nobody said it. She said it herself, in the middle of the eleventh week, standing on the bank of the wedging shed with a hod of clay in each hand because there was nowhere in that room to put a hod down, to about twenty men who had come in out of the wet and were standing in the doorway because the floor would not take twenty pairs of boots.

"Right," said Pernel Quist. "I told a magistrate in a room with a stove in it that two hundred and six fired tiles could not be broken and the clay put back in the same rolling, and the reason I gave him was a day and a half of this shed, and he wrote it down, and he was right to. I want that said in this room by me and not found out in about four years."

She put one hod down on the bank and kept hold of the other, because with nine feet of headroom and a floor that will not take twenty pairs of boots, a woman who has come into a room to say something does not put down both.

"There are two kilns coming off inside the month and about nine days of charged clay coming back into this room, and it is the floor of this room that is the scarce part and not the headroom, and I would like somebody to write down that I know what I have just done. A reason that was correct on the sixteenth day is a reason and not a law, and I have found that out at four months' work, and I am not resigning, and if anybody in this doorway wants to tell the magistrate I said so, they may tell him at their own cost and not out of my hand."

The man at the cutting bench, who had come in out of the wet behind the rest of them, said, "You are not resigning over nine days of clay."

"I am not resigning because a rollkeeper who resigns is a woman who has said the county's records are not worth a room," she said, "and this county has had four of those in nineteen years and the county did not stop, and it got a new woman each time and every one of them was all right and the county went on. **This county's trouble is never that the office is wrong. It is that the office is right, and there is no way to be relieved of being right.**"

Nobody in the doorway said anything to that, and the ash-driver took his cap off, which he did not do for her, and put it back on, and went out into the rain without one word of it, and she watched him go and did not ask him why either.

---

Two rolling kilns were stopped in the twelfth week. The one at the north village had a rolling half-charged and fired it out, because a half-charged rolling fired out is nine days of wages for a kiln that will not be paid for nine days, and the one at the east village had not been charged and could be left, and was.

The men at the two kilns had no trade in this county. That is the thing a fen man says about a clay county and it is wrong in both directions: a man who has cut clay for nineteen years can cut clay, and there is no other clay in this county, and so a man whose kiln has shut can stand at the front of a working kiln and be told, by nineteen years of everybody's body language, that he is not to stand there.

The last man out of the north village was a boy of nineteen who had been on the charging floor for two years and a bit, and he came down the road to the Ninebridge in the twelfth week with his apron over his arm because he could not think what else to do with it, and he stood at the low end of the rack for the better part of a day, and nobody sent him away and nobody put him on anything, and on the fourth day he asked a man at a board whether there was anything that wanted carrying, and the man at the board looked at him for a while and said there was not, not today, and the boy said thank you and stood back at the end of the rack and carried two boards anyway because the boards wanted carrying and that was not the same thing.

About forty men were out. That is the number the ash-driver wrote down in the fourth week and it is the number the room accepted, and the ash-driver would not go past forty, and when the man at the loading end said it might be fifty he said, "It is about forty, and I will write about forty, and if it is fifty at the second low water then I will write fifty at the second low water, and I am not going to write a number in a week I have not counted."

In the fifteenth week the two kilns that were still burning went to half, because half of a kiln is a kiln with a hole in it, and the two of them took twenty-seven of the forty between them.

The other thirteen did not go anywhere.

That was the fifteenth week, and it was not a thing anybody argued about, and it is the only figure in this whole business that everybody in this county has ever agreed on without an argument, and the reason it is agreed is that it is the ugliest one.

**A struck man, an employed man, and a man whose kiln has shut are three different people and the room knows it and does not say so.** A struck man is on a list and can be put back and knows the law and has a tile in a box. An employed man has a hearth, a fire, a wage drawn at the end of a week, and two witnesses whose names are in a book, and is the only one of the three of them who can be asked a question in a room. A man whose kiln has shut is none of those things and is not on anything at all, and there is no column in the County of Ash for him, and there has never been, and a man who is not on a column cannot be put on one, and a man who cannot be put on one cannot be given a vote, and a man who has not got a vote is a man who is not a witness, and a man who is not a witness cannot be restored.

The thirteen went out of the county before the seventeenth week. Four of them went to the county town and got work of a kind, and two of them went to a coast, and one of them went to his sister's house and stayed there, and six of them nobody in this county knows what happened to, and the number is written down in a book at the assizes in a keeper's hand with the date and the hour on it, and the hand that wrote it did not write one word of an opinion, and the six are six lines with nothing against them and no reason on them.

It was the man at the loading end who asked her for them, in the seventeenth week, in the long room at the assizes, and he asked it in a voice that had no authority in it at all and did not pretend to have any.

"I want the six in a column," he said. "I want them in a column and I want the column headed and I want the date on it, and I do not care what is under them."

"You want the six in a column with nothing under them," said Pernel Quist.

"Yes."

"That is a column with nothing under it, and I have no column for that, and it is not a thing this office has ever had to hold, because for nineteen years a name came off a roll and went onto a list, and the list was the column, and the column had a shape and the shape was the law. **What you are asking me for is a man who is not on the roll and not on the list, and this county has never had a shape for a man who is not on the roll and not on the list, and I am not going to invent one in the seventeenth week of a year when the county is short of clay.**" She wrote the six names on a page of her own and wrote the day and the hour against the head of the page and stopped there, and turned the page round so the room could read the six and could see that under the six there was nothing, and not one reason, and no line for a line. "**That is what it is. Six names, a day, an hour, and nothing under them, and a clerk in nine years is going to find that page and count the six and write *six unaccounted* at the top of it, and there will be nothing under that word either, and that is not my doing and it is not his. It is that nobody in this county ever built a place to put a man who has stopped being anything.**"

Ostry Vaile's line is the second one on the page headed with a number that nobody had put at the top of a page before, and it is the first entry in nineteen years in that office against a man by name that is not a removal, and it is four words long, and it was entered in the fourth week while he was still able to ask.

---

It was in the sixteenth week that the letter came.

It was brought to the Ninebridge by a person who carried papers and did not stop in this county, and he put it into the hand of the Ninebridge and would not stay, and it was four sheets, and it was in an envelope with a seal on it that had a device on it which is a hand laid flat on a book, and the hand of the Ninebridge looked at the seal for the length of a slow count and then cut it off with a knife and dropped the seal into the pit, and did not say why, and did not have to.

It was from the counting house of the tolls of Bellweather-Living, a city that is not on any map in this kingdom and that does not admit to the Crown that it exists, and it was from a man who is a toll-house steward there and has a grandson of nine whose name is on the front of a four-hundred-leaf book, and it was dated at the top in a form this county has no word for, being a month and a year counted from something that is not the first firing, and the hand of the Ninebridge read that date out loud once in a shed and then said, "We cannot check one word of that," and nobody asked her to check it, because she is the hand of the Ninebridge and a hand does not get asked.

The letter said, in four pages, the whole of what the counting house was and what it kept and why it kept it and who had kept it, and it said — and this is the sentence the county read out loud twice in a shed —

*A creditor's press is the worst place in the world to keep a roll of persons and the only place in the world where nobody can quietly take it out of the book. I have been arguing that for eleven years in a hall and I am now the man who is proposing it to my own city, and I would like the argument minuted and the minute put where a Crown's man will find it in about nine years.*

And it offered: a press, and a shelf, and a farthing of salt as the price, and four sheets of conditions.

And it named a price for the shelf, and the price was not money.

**The price is the eleven sheets.**

A chain of eleven sheets had lain in that counting house for eleven years for a creditor who did not want them, in the press on the shelf that is now empty, and the shelf is where a roll of persons would go, and a shelf cannot be given away for nothing because a shelf is the only thing in a counting house that cannot be argued with, and so the counting house is asking for the eleven sheets in exchange, and the eleven sheets are in this county's equivalent of nowhere at all, and this county does not know what they are, and no one in this county can go and get them.

The ash-driver worked it out standing up, in a shed, with the letter in his hand, and he had got to the end of the signature before anybody else had finished reading the first page of it.

"There is a road to that city," he said. "There is a road to this county's north and a road from the north and the roads do not join and the county's terms say a name is not to be written in the line's book at this end, and a man at this end cannot go and get eleven sheets off a shelf in a city that is not on any map. **We have been offered a shelf and the price is a four-hundred-mile errand we are not allowed to make, and a county that cannot pay for a shelf has got a fourth hand that is promised and not held, and a promised hand is not a hand.**"

---

And so, in the seventeenth week, the county wrote to the only office any of them had ever heard of that keeps press registers, and it wrote a letter with no name in it, because the second of the five terms is forty-one words long and says that no name is to be read out on the far side of the line by anybody from the north and no name is to be written in the line's book at the north end, and the county is not going to be the first body in nineteen years to break a term it wrote itself.

The question they asked was fifteen words long and it is on the page because a man copied it out of the letter and a man who copies a whole thing is a man who cannot help it.

*Will a press hold what is put in it, and refuse to give it up?*

The answer came back in eleven days, and it came to the Ninebridge and not to the assizes, because the assizes is an office and an office is a thing a Crown's man can ask a question of, and the Ninebridge is four hundred yards of tiled kerb and a shed with a door on the side of it.

It is one page, and at the foot of it in a small flat unhurried hand that had been copying for thirty years:

*Yes. That is the whole of what a press does. It holds what is entered on the shelf and it refuses nothing and it gives nothing up, and a court cannot take a thing out of a press that belongs to somebody; a court can take it out of one that belongs to an office, and the difference between those two is whether there is a person at the end of it, and the person is what a register is for. A register answers what was entered on the shelf and nothing else. If you want a book that can be asked what a thing in it is for, then you want a court, and a court will give it to whichever of you asks loudest, and that is not a defect in the court. That is the trade.*

*Ivet Salter, of the third press, thirty years.*

And above that, in a younger and quicker hand, and covering the whole of the page from end to end, including four ruled cells at the foot that had nothing in them, was a fair copy of a leaf of the fourth press's own shelf register. **The letter had not asked for the register. The letter had asked one question about whether a press will give a thing up, and the office in its own office had decided that the answer to that question was the register, and had sent a copy of it, unasked, in the same bag.** The copy was made by a clerk of twenty-eight called Sennet Roil who did not ask to be sent it and who has a grandmother's habit of copying a whole leaf, and a whole leaf means the ruled cells at the bottom as well, and a copyist who leaves the blanks off is a copyist who has made an argument, and her grandmother was a copyist for forty years and was struck off a parish roll once in her grandmother's working life and never once said so.

He read the copy on a board at the low end of the rack with a wet cloth in his hand, because there was always a wet cloth in his hand in this county, and he got to the fourth ruled cell at the foot of the leaf, and it was empty, and it was empty in the way a cell is ruled and left, and there was a line above it with a number on it and a date and a county column, and the county cell on that line was empty too.

It is the fourth ruled cell at the foot of a shelf register in a press two hundred miles off, in a town this county has never had a reason to send anybody to, and it has nothing whatever to do with a rolling of eleven sheets.

He stood at the rack with a wet cloth in his hand until the cloth went cold, and did not say one word about it, and then he went to the end of the rack and sat down on the low stool and took out the notebook and wrote, in the flat hand, in the back, one line.

*Seventeenth week. A cell, ruled and empty, in a copy of a shelf register in a press two hundred miles off, in a line with a number and a date on it. I do not know what it is. I have written down that I saw it and that I did not say anything, because a man who says a thing about a cell in a shed full of people is a man who has got a shape and no fact, and I have got a shape and no fact, and last time I acted on a shape with no fact under it I put a bell in a file four hundred miles off and I have not finished paying for that.*

*And the woman of sixty-one has written that a register answers what was entered on the shelf and nothing else, and that is her office and not one thing more of it, and she says it as though it were a small thing.*

He shut the book and went back to the rack, and Bel, four years old, at the fourth row on a stool, said "again" without looking up, and he did it again.

The letter from the counting house went back into a drawer in the hand of the Ninebridge, with the four conditions, and the eleven sheets, and a term. And the answer from the third press of a registry in a province went into the same drawer, on top of it, because it was eleven days old and it was shorter.

Two sheets of paper, in a drawer, in a county that has never refused anything, and the drawer is not locked, because in this county a drawer with a lock in it is a thing somebody has to have a key to, and a key is a name, and a name is the thing this county has decided it will not do.
