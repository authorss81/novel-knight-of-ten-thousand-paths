# Chapter 0091

The second of Greenmonth was a Tuesday and it was not a market day, and Aren Kest came up out of a clay county at the first light with a fever of three days on him and eighty-eight days of absence in a book in a room over a weigh-house, and the fever was the second of the three and it would break on the fifth at about the sixth hour or it would not.

The lane door made a noise she knew. He came in at about the sixth hour and he had a bag on his left shoulder and grey clay in the seam of it, and he got about eight feet into a room with a long table in it and then took hold of the end of the table, which is what a man does when the floor has become a thing he has opinions about.

"Sit down," said Mara Vey.

He sat down. He put the bag on the floor beside the chair, and she looked at the seam of it for about a second and a half and did not ask about it then or afterwards, and she had a list in her own fair hand face up on the long table with the hour against it in the day-book, and the list had been in that room since the first light on the first of Greenmonth, and it was four hundred words long and itemised and there was one line in it she had read four times.

He gave her the numbers before she asked, because he always does.

"Nine months and six days," he said. "In there. The county keeps a rolling and weeks and months and it has two low waters a year and it counts the year from the first firing, and there is no month in it that a clerk in this country could write down, and I did not convert anything, and I want that entered, because I have been out four times in eleven years and this is the first time I have come back with a figure I cannot put next to a date in this room."

"Eighty-eight days here," said Mara Vey, and wrote it. "From the fourth hour of the fourth of Hearthmonth to this morning. I had a figure of it in the day-book on the seventh from a carrier's note and I entered it and I did not know what it was a figure of."

"It was mine. The carrier's is right by about a day and I have not corrected him."

"Entered as his and not corrected." She wrote it down. "Go on."

"Twenty-one days," he said. "That is the figure printed at the foot of the terms, in an office's hand, in a smaller type than the rest, as a standard of measurement. It is not a term and it is not about any of the five. Eighty-eight against twenty-one is sixty-seven days."

**Sixty-seven days.** She wrote it and read it back, and he watched her do it.

"That is the only number in this case I am frightened of," he said. "Not the eighty-eight. The sixty-seven. Because the warning has not changed. It was three weeks when I went out on the ninth of Embermonth and it was three weeks on a form in a drawer in a shed four hundred miles off, and the office cannot say whose figure it is and neither can I, and it went five days wrong on the first time, twenty-six days out against twenty-one, and it has gone sixty-seven days wrong on the second, eighty-eight out against the same twenty-one, and the ratio is not a constant, it is about three-quarters of the absence, and it gets worse in proportion to what I do over there. I wrote that down in Greenmonth in a cost sheet before any of it and I would like to be allowed the one true thing I predicted."

"You said that in a room to six men in a year you were not in this county for—" she began, and then did not finish the sentence, because he was not going to be managed into a smaller thing than the thing he was saying. "You may have it. And I am entering it as a prediction, which is worth about a third of what it is worth afterwards, and I am telling you that so that you do not get proud of it in front of me."

Then he put his hand flat on the table, the way a man puts it on a thing to prove the table is there.

"And four pages," he said. "In my own hand, in a notebook, done in a shed in the last week, and they are about the record and not about the road, and I will read them to you and you will tell me they are wrong, and I want to be corrected in a room by somebody who reads for a living."

She read them. It took about twenty minutes. They were not good pages. They were the best four pages he has ever written and they were in the wrong order and two of them had a figure in them that she made him go and get the book for, and one of them had a sentence in it that she read twice and then read him back, and he agreed with her correction and did not argue, and that was the first thing that had happened in this room in four years that she had not had to ask twice for.

---

**And that was the whole of what he said about it, and it was four-fifths of the truth, and there was nothing in it that was a lie.**

The fever was the second of three. He was in and out of the chair between the seventh hour and the ninth. She had the physician's line in the day-book from the last time and did not send for anybody, and he told her not to, and she told him that was not his to decide, and he said it was the second time this year he had been wrong about a body and he was going to be wrong about it again.

At about the tenth hour she said: "The half tile."

"It went north on the fourth of the ninth month, in the bag of the woman who keeps the road at that end, on a day that is the fourth of that month and is not a day in this calendar," he said. "It weighs a shade over ten ounces and there is not one thing in it that anybody can take out of it. **I have got no way of finding out whether it is in this building and I am not going to ask, because a man who asks whether an object arrived has told the other end that the object is worth arriving.**"

"That is a good reason and it is not the reason."

"It is the reason I am giving you."

He put his hand into the bag then, without hurrying, the way a man reaches for something he has decided about, and he put a thing on the long table between them.

It was grey fired clay, six inches by four and about two fingers thick, broken across the middle, and the half that was on the table had nothing on it at all. Not a device. Not a hand. Nothing.

"He made it in a shed at the back of a kiln in about four minutes on the second day of a month I cannot name," he said. "There are about nine hundred of them in two hoppers and they are the only thing in that county nobody in it is entitled to want, and a whole tile is the account of a person and a half a tile is the account of nothing, and I have made this one myself in a shed with a cold saw and I have not told the woman who fired that kiln that I did it."

"Why not."

"Because she was asked four questions in nine months and answered all four and one of them was a question about a date, and because I made a thing in a shed behind her kiln and put a saw mark on it and she would have had to look at it." He pushed it a half inch towards her. "Weigh it. It is a shade over ten ounces and it is the account of nobody and you can put it in the press and leave it there for nine years."

She weighed it on the salt merchant's own stone, which is in the room and is not a record, and it came to a shade over ten ounces.

"I already have a press for it," she said, "with nothing in it, and I did not know until this afternoon that it was for anything."

"Yes, you did," said Aren Kest. "You have known since the eighth of Embermonth and you wrote down that you knew, in a day-book, in about eleven words, and I read the same sentence in a shed in a county that is not on any map in this kingdom about four months later, written out by a man with a ledger who has been counting fires for nineteen years and had never heard of you."

---

**Tovan Kest came at about the eleventh hour and did not knock, because a yard does not knock.**

He stood in the doorway of a room with a press in it, and then came all the way in and shut the door behind him, which he had done once in four years and had not done on the last time either.

He looked at his brother for long enough to have said something, and did not.

"You are grey."

"I am."

"You are in that chair and I am going to stand here, and if you try to get up off it while I am standing here I shall take you out of it, and I am not going to touch you and I am not going to help you, and both of those are on purpose and you can ask me about either of them and I will not answer."

"Thank you."

"There is a thing in the yard," said Tovan Kest, "and it is a culvert at Four Elms, four days, two of you, mucky price, mud to the axle, and the man who owns it has paid twice on time and I will say that much for him. I took it on the third of a month you were not in this county for and I have had it in that book since with the hour on it and your name off it, and I did not put it there on purpose. It is a thing set, and it is the last thing I have written in that book that was a job taken and not a job finished."

"That is the fourth day of four," said Aren Kest. "You are keeping the four days on the page."

"I am keeping every day of it on the page," said Tovan Kest, "and I have written down the two days before that and the two days after, and there are four entries and they are in a hand you would know, and they are on the page because the page is the only place in this county where a man can be wrong out loud and be found out about it. I have not read them to anybody."

He put his hand flat on the table, on the fair copy of four hundred words in a woman's hand, and took it off again.

"And there is a thing that is not a job," he said.

He did not say it then.

He stood there looking at the bag on the floor until the room had been quiet long enough to be a decision, and his face did a thing that his brother could not read and that he did not try to read, and Aren Kest understood in the second of the three things that were happening in that room that **his brother had come here to say something about an order and was not going to say it.**

There were six or seven things in an ordinary morning. He could do every one of them. He had done them about nine thousand times, on a Tuesday, in a room over a cooper, in a yard on Weir Street, in an order he had never once had to think about, and he had been coming up those steps since he was nine years old.

He put his hand on the end of the table to get up, and he put his left hand on the table and then his right, and then he turned round about half a way and picked up the bag, and the room he was in was not the yard, and he knew that, and he did it anyway, and it took him five seconds longer than it would have taken him in a yard in a Tuesday.

**Somebody in the room had put them in the wrong order. That is the whole of what was wrong, and he could not have said what the order was if a man had come into that room at the fourth hour on the second of Greenmonth and asked him for it, and he would have said the same thing in the yard, and he would have said it in a gateway with a rope in his hands.**

Tovan Kest watched all five of those seconds.

He did not say a word about any of them.

He said: "I will go and do my own day's work."

"Tell me the thing when you want to tell me."

"I will not," said Tovan Kest. "I am telling you now that I will not, so that on the day I say nothing about it, you will know I decided in a room and not in a yard, and that is a different thing and it is the only difference there is." He went out and shut the door, which he had now done twice in four years.

---

**She gave him the county at about the first hour, in ninety minutes, in the flat voice she uses for a day-book, and he did not interrupt her once.**

The count had gone from four descriptions to eleven, and the eleven had gone out of this room on the ninth of Frostmonth with the *Keeper* column empty and no reason entered, and the office had answered it on the thirteenth of Wolfmonth with three lines proposing a count of persons for the county as a whole, and the three lines had sat unread in the back of that day-book for twenty-two days because they had asked nothing of this office and named nobody in it, and she was the only person in this county who had read both documents and she could not see what was in either of them.

A bench of nine had asked the guild for money for the second time on the fourth of Frostmonth and had been given two marks and a subscription book, and a carpenter had written *nil* on the back of a wage sheet and a reeve had used a casting vote twice in a meeting, and a clerk in a provincial town had walked out of a question nobody should have asked and had come back with four facts and a request, and a woman with a card had come up two flights of a stair and not left it, and the county court had let a count expire by discovering in nine minutes that a returnable paper with no party in it expires.

He listened to all of it. And when she had finished he asked her one question, and it was not about any of that.

"The Sedge Reach," he said. "The mark-day reading."

"Nobody has taken it since you went."

"Nobody has taken it in eighty-eight days. How many readings has the ring?"

"Two in this year that anybody has written down, and they are a quarter of an inch apart, and the guild's man with the levels has refused the guild's roll and he is right to, and the bench has had a day named twice and had it twice, and the next one is the twenty-fourth, and that is twenty-two days off, and it is going to be taken by the guild's man alone."

"Then it will be a number," said Aren Kest. "And there is nobody in this county who can check a number."

"No," said Mara Vey. "And I have entered that, against nobody, with the hour on it, and I am not going to enter anything else about it, because I have entered three things in a day-book this year that I did not have to and one of them was about a person and I am not doing it twice."

---

**The last thing of the second of Greenmonth was in a notebook, in a flat hand, at about the fourth hour of the afternoon, and the notebook was in a coat.**

*Second of Greenmonth, the second day of the fever, and the fourth hour of the afternoon. Out on the fourth of Hearthmonth at the fourth hour, in on this morning at the first light. Eighty-eight days here. Nine months and six days there. Sixty-seven days of error against a printed three weeks, and the printed three weeks is the same three weeks it was the first time and the office still cannot say whose figure it is.*

*I have given her every number. I have given her four pages and she has corrected two of them and I have let her. In eleven years I have not been in a room with a woman who reads for a living and let her correct a page, and I have been out of this room for eighty-eight days and there is not one figure in this book I have to hide and there is one thing in this book I have to hide and it is not a figure.*

*It is this, and I am writing it down because I have decided to and not because I have been asked to, and because a thing written in this book stays in this book, and because the book is the only place left where it can be said.*

*There are six or seven things in a Tuesday morning in the room over the cooper and in the yard on Weir Street. I can do all of them. I have done all of them about nine thousand times and I could do them now. I cannot do them in the order. I do not know what the order was. I came up at the first light this morning and I have been in this room twice since and I have not been in a yard yet.*

*And this: I stand like a man on a slip. Weight a little back, hands loose, and not in my pockets, and I did it coming up the lane door at the sixth hour this morning and I did not notice and Mara Vey has watched me do it off a window for four years and has never said one word about it and wrote down in a day-book in Fallowmonth that she was not going to ask me again. I cannot tell you which end of the string gave me that and I have been asked for it in print, in an office's hand, on the back of a form, as the second of five terms, and the form says that I will not be able to, and the form is right.*

*So there are two of them and they are both mine and neither of them is a road, and they are in this book, and they are not going in a form, and this is the third time I have decided what a keeper of records may not be told. The first was a letter and it cost this town a bench-clerk's afternoon and her two nights' sleep and me a year. The second was a mark on a coping and it cost a man of sixty-four the answer to a question he was entitled to ask me, and me a lie I am going to have to keep for nine years, and the lie is the only protection the answer had. This one has not cost anybody anything yet.*

*That is the whole of it. I have told her the numbers, because a man who reports his own costs is the only kind of man there is and I am not going to stop being that one over a morning. And I have not told her the morning. If she asked me this afternoon I would tell her, and she is not going to ask me this afternoon, and both of those are true and one of them is a decision and the other one is a promise, and I know which is which, and I am not going to be the man who works out which one she has made.*

*Three days. Second, third, fourth, and it breaks on the fifth at about the sixth hour. I have been right about that twice.*
