# Chapter 0074

The fourth of Frostmonth was a Wednesday and a market day and there was a hard frost on it, and the reason a bench of nine was out on a bridge at the fourth hour in the first hard frost of a winter instead of in the summer is the only good reason there is for cutting stone in a fen, and it is that a cold chisel is deaf.

A chisel that is warm rings. A man cutting a course in an abutment can hear, off the head of his own tool, whether he has found a natural bed or whether he has made one, and a man who cannot hear is a man cutting on the joint and getting away with it. In a frost the iron is dead. It comes off the stone like a rasp instead of a note, and the man has to go down to the bed by hand, with his thumb, in the cold, every four inches, and it takes twice as long and it is the only week in the year when the fourth course of the Old Toll crossing can honestly be re-set by nine men who are not a bridgewright's crew.

So they were on it at about a quarter to four, in daylight, with a brazier on the wharf and a boy at the string line, and the frost had the wharf white to the third plank and the river low enough to walk out to the second pier and stand there and look at a thing nine men had been unable to look at since the sixth of Mudmonth.

Tovan Kest went out to the deck with the level case under his arm, because the rule in that guild is that the man who reads the numbers carries them, and it does not matter whether he is a member, and he had not been a member since he was twenty-one years old.

He had been in the room eleven times in six months. He had said the true thing in public four times and had not voted once.

---

The hall over the wharf held the specification on the table with a rule across it to keep the leaves flat, and the specification is a thing a guild does have: **two leaves, the first the work, the second the standing conditions of this guild, of which there are eight.** The first leaf was the work. The second leaf was the conditions, and the eighth of them was the seat.

The guild's man with the levels put his thumb on the eighth line and left it there while he read the first seven, and read them at a speed that showed he had read them before, and then he shut the leaves and squared them and said:

"That is the specification. I have not read the eighth."

"You have not read the eighth," said the bench-clerk.

"I have not read the eighth," said the guild's man with the levels, "because the eighth line is a seat, and there is no seat."

Nobody in the hall said anything for a count of four, and the reason nobody said anything is that there is no seat in this county, there has not been a seat in it since a thing came out of an abutment in a wagoner's cart in Greenmonth, and the specification had not been reprinted, and the eighth line was the guild's own words and the guild's own hand: *that a seat so cut will be used and will fail, and that the number of uses is not stated here, because it is not a thing this bench may know.*

"You cannot read your own specification," said the man at the fish weir.

"I can read every line of it," said the guild's man with the levels. "I have been asking that this leaf be reprinted since the sixth of Mudmonth and the clerk will not reprint it and the clerk is right, because a leaf that is reprinted is a leaf that has had a line taken off it, and in nine years somebody will want to know what the line said. So it stands, and it says what it says, and there is no seat, and the number of uses of a seat that is not there is not a thing this bench may know either, and I will not read it out and I will not pretend I have forgotten it, and I will enter that I did not read it and why, and I would like the bench to enter it with my reason and not with theirs."

"Enter it," said the reeve.

The bench-clerk entered it. Tovan Kest read the line over the clerk's shoulder as it went in and did not say anything, because the man had entered the thing correctly and had entered the reason in the man's own words, and a yard is not an office but neither is a bench, and a man who improves another man's reason in a minute is a man who is about to be asked to do it every week.

---

Then the fifth line of the first leaf, and the forty minutes.

The fifth line was the course at the fourth above the dressed block: *that the course be dressed and re-set upon a bed of lime and oak-dust, and the joint be filled, and the opening at the joint be closed.*

"Close it," said the guild's man with the levels.

"No," said the man at the fish weir.

He was sixty-one and he kept the eel-weir and the slide above the mill, and the weir was eleven hundred yards down the river and had been in his family for four generations, and he was not a bridgewright and had never claimed to be and was the only man in that hall who had been in the water under that abutment in the last eleven years, and he had been in it twice, in a hard spring, with a lamp, and he had felt it.

"I have been under that abutment," he said, "and I will tell the bench what is under it. There is a weep. It is about a hand wide and it goes down through the second course into the bed and it is how that river breathes under a stone that has been in the same place since before anybody in this room's trade was a trade. Dress that course and fill that joint and you have shut the weep of a bearing course in a fen bridge, and a fen bridge with a shut weep is a bridge that is carrying the water in the stone and not under it, and you will find it out in about nine years when there is a soft spot in a place nobody put one."

"You will find it out at the next low water," said the guild's man with the levels, "because a weep is a hole and a hole filled with lime is a plug, and a plug in a hole under a bearing course is a thing a man can see, and I have a rule and the rule is in the leather tube and the rule is in the hall, and I will read you the level of the joint on the second span and on the third and you may take them down yourself and see."

"I have never taken a level in my life."

"You will not need to take it, you will only need to hold the rule while I read it, and a man who keeps an eel-weir can hold a rule," said the guild's man with the levels, "and that is not a courtesy, it is the custom of this guild, and it has been the custom longer than the guild has had a specification."

And the man at the fish weir held the rule for about two hours and swore at it twice and said at the end, in a completely different voice:

"Well. That is not a weep."

"It is not a weep. It is a bed of shells in the second course and the shells have gone out where the water came in and about eleven years ago, and it is a hole, and it is going to be a hole in about a hundred years, and a hole in a second course under a fourth is a thing you fill with lime and a lead strip and oak-dust and not with a scar."

---

But he did not let go of it, and the forty minutes were not about the shells, and Tovan Kest, standing at the end of the long table with the level case in his hand, wrote down the time in the margin of his own copy of the leaves because he counts things and could not help it, and the forty minutes were this.

"Do not touch the course above it at all," said the man at the fish weir. "That is what I am asking. Not to fill the weep, I have lost that argument. **Leave the course above the dressed block open the width of a hand, in register, and let it be open, and let anybody come and look at it.**"

"Why," said the bench-clerk, and he asked it in the tone of a man who has to write the answer down.

"Because it happened," said the man at the fish weir, "and because a dressed stone in a splice is a lie about the fact that the stone moved, and this county has a great many dressed stones in splices and every one of them is a thing that happened to somebody, and I have been on this river sixty-one years and I have a boy who goes under there with a pole and I would like there to be one place in this town where a man can stand on a bridge and look at a mark and know that the mark is where the thing was and not where somebody decided to say it was."

Silence.

"Now," said the man at the fish weir, getting up off the bench end, "I am aware that I have said a thing that is not about fish, and I said at the start of it that it was not about fish, and the man from the foundry can put it in the minute in the words I used and not in better ones, and I want the foundry's man to vote against this and I want him to vote against it out loud so that it is in the book that somebody disagreed."

"It is a lie about the stone," said the guild's man with the levels. "That is my whole objection and I will not soften it. You are asking this bench to leave a gap in a bearing course because the gap makes you feel better, and I have no argument on earth against how you feel, and I have a rule in a leather tube against the gap."

"Enter both," said the reeve.

---

And then the asking.

The reeve put it flat on the table with a sheet and a rule, and she had had nine years of doing that and she did it faster than the bench-clerk could get the sheet square, and there were nine in the room and Tovan Kest standing, and the guild's man with the levels was there because he had put up the specification and he is not a member and does not vote, and the bench cannot sit without a clerk and the clerk does not vote either.

"Second asking," she said, "and I am going to say it as the second, and the clerk is to enter the number."

The specification, worked out by the guild's man with the levels and the bench's own day rate, came to **four marks three shillings and fivepence.** The bench's chest, on the table, open, had **two marks** in it. The bench's whole year, voted in Longlight, was **four marks and six.**

"Four marks three shillings and fivepence, out of two marks, in Frostmonth, for a crossing this bench does not own and has never owned and cannot sell." She put her hand flat on the sheet. "That is the asking. I am putting it. A bench that is generous decides. A bench that is asked is being told what it is for."

Nobody said anything.

"And I will say the other half of it now, because I am the one putting it and I have thought about it for a fortnight and I would like it in the minute in my own words and not improved. **I want it done. That is the whole of my interest in it.** I want the fourth course set and the weep filled and the joint shut, and I want it done before the thaw, and I do not care one way or the other who pays, and I know what that makes me. I am the reeve of this town and I have wanted to vote on this guild's own matter for nine years and I have not, and I am not going to start now, and I am asking for four marks three shillings and fivepence out of a chest with two marks in it and I am not asking for a rate, because a rate is the same four marks three shillings and fivepence spread over a thousand throats instead of a subscription in a market, and a subscription is a thing a man can refuse in person and a rate is a thing a man finds out about in a quarter, and I know the difference and I am not pretending I have not chosen it."

The foundry's man said, "Will the subscription fill it?"

"I do not know," said the reeve. "I have a figure and no arithmetic. I have two marks of subscriptions promised in the last two weeks, in notes, from two men, and I have entered both and I have entered who gave them, and a note is not money and I have not entered it as money."

"Then that is four marks and there is three shillings and fivepence still to find."

"Yes."

"Three shillings and fivepence, then, in a hard winter, over a fortnight of markets. On a crossing the guild does not own, for a course that is open the width of a hand." The foundry's man laughed, once, and it was not an unkind noise. "That is a market's worth of a bad week and it is going to be got from about two hundred people, which is half a farthing a head, and if we ask a hundred people for a farthing we will get it from eleven."

"Then we ask eleven," said the reeve, "and if we get it from eleven we enter that we got it from eleven and we enter the whole of it in the market book, and in nine years a man can go and look at it and see whether this town was asked or whether it was told."

---

And that was when Tovan Kest said it, for the fourth time in six months, and he did not vote, and he said it standing up with the level case under his arm because he had said the other three sitting down and a man who has said a thing three times sitting down deserves to say the fourth one standing.

"Two things," he said, "and both of them are for the minute and neither of them is mine to move."

He put the case down on the table, which he had never done in that hall.

"One. The man at the fish weir is right about the mark and wrong about the course, and the man with the levels is right about the course and has no opinion about the mark, and the finding of this bench ought to be that the mark stays and the course is cut, and that is a better finding than either of them has asked for, and it will take eleven days and not two hours, because the course above the dressed block comes out and goes back dressed and filled and the opening below it is dressed and filled and the opening stays where it is, on the joint, in register, so that a man can stand on the deck and look down and see the mark. **I have been told for about a year by two men in two counties that a scar is a place a thing came through and can come through again, and it turns out the third one of us is a man who keeps eels.**"

"And the second thing," said the bench-clerk, with his pen up.

"The second thing is the whole of my objection and I have had it since the second of Fallowmonth and I have never said it in a room with a bench in it, and the reeve has just said the first half of it and got it right, so I am only saying the half she did not say, and she has not voted and will not and neither will I."

He turned round and looked at the nine of them.

"A bench that is generous decides. **A bench that is asked is being told what it is for.** And a bench that is asked three times and says yes three times is not a bench, it is a subscription, and I have watched a subscription happen. Once. In Longlight. Not here — up north, four hundred miles, in a city whose name I am not going to say in this hall because this hall keeps a roll and the roll is the town's. And it was not a Crown's business and it was not a marshal's and nobody sent anybody. It was a bench of nine that was asked for something in the first month of a winter, and said yes, and was asked for something else in the third month, and said yes, and was asked for a third thing in the eighth month, and said yes, and by the ninth month it was not deciding anything. It was voting on a list that had been made somewhere else, and every vote was unanimous, and every vote was recorded as unanimous, and **a book in that city has four years of unanimous minutes in it and there is not one disagreement in the whole of it, and you can have a thing like that by saying yes three times and meaning it kindly every time.**"

The stove ticked.

"That has not happened here," said Tovan Kest. "I want it in the minute that it has not happened here. This is an asking and we are answering it and we may well say yes, and I would like it written down, today, in a room with a reeve in it, that a man stood here and said the thing that is going to be necessary one day, and the day has not come, and he was not talking about anything that is on the table this morning. **I do not know when it will come and I am not going to guess, and I have said it because I have got about nine weeks' notice in me and I would rather spend the notice now than later.**"

"I will not vote," he said, "because I am not a member and have not been since I was twenty-one, and because there is work in this yard that I have not counted this month and a yard in a hard month is not a thing a man can count from a hall, and because this bench is about to be asked for money by nine men in a market and I do not want a yard in the argument."

Nobody said anything else about it, and the foundry's man moved that the sense of the meeting be taken, and it was taken, and the man at the fish weir said *against* out loud, as he had asked to.

The bench carried the work, on the bench's hands, with the man at the fish weir against and the reason he gave minuted in his own words, and the reeve did not vote and said why and it was minuted, and Tovan Kest said the true thing and did not vote and it was minuted whole.

And then the bench-clerk read the minute out, because a bench minute is read out before it is signed, and it is the shortest thing in the chapter and the only one anybody in that hall or in that town will read in nine years.

---

*Fourth of Frostmonth, a Wednesday, a market day, the fourth hour. Bench of the Merefen bridge guild, the hall over the wharf. Nine in the room. The specification of two leaves was put up and the first leaf and the first seven of the second were read and the eighth was not read, and the reason given and entered, that the eighth line is a seat and there is no seat. Specification amended at the fifth line of the first leaf: the course is to be dressed and re-set, the bed laid in lime and oak-dust, the hole in the second course filled and leaded, and the opening at the joint above the dressed block is to remain open, in register, and to be so left. Carried. The man at the fish weir voted against and gave his reason, which is entered. The reeve did not vote, and her reason is entered.*

*Asking, second in this matter: that the materials for the visit be found, being four marks three shillings and fivepence, against two marks in the chest. Carried on the bench's hands, the reeve not voting. To be found by subscription at the market, at the figure stated, and not by rate, and the whole of the subscription to be entered in the market book with the names of the subscribers and the figures, so that in nine years a man may go and see whether this town was asked or was told.*

*T. Kest, not a member, said the true thing and did not vote, and the true thing is entered whole.*

*The sum is short by three shillings and fivepence.*
