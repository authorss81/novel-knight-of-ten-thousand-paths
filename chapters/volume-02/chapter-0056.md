# Chapter 0056

Two things came off the county's file on the sixth of Embermonth and they came off within an hour of each other, and the keeper of the Bellweather Claims Archive was told about both of them before the post had finished delivering.

The first was the endorsement. Four lines, numbered 4 of the season, printed on the back of a sheet of a form series, discharging the writ of enquiry in the matter of the records of the reeve of Merefen; and the reeve's own clerk brought it up the stair himself at about the ninth hour and stood in the filing room and read it out, because a thing that discharges a writ ought to be read out in a room, and then he said, with a clerk's economy:

"Eleven days. I have been holding a bar on the reeve's records since the ninth of Longlight and a bar on this office since the same morning, and I have been very glad of both, and I am not going to pretend to you this morning that I am sorry about either of them going off, because I have spent eleven days watching two offices in this county be unable to do a thing they were going to have to do."

"You carried it," said Mara Vey. "I know what that cost and it is in your own hand in the yard's book and in mine."

"Yes," said the clerk. "And now the second thing."

The second thing was the third writ, which had gone down the county post on the ninth of Fallowmonth in a tube with nobody in Merefen knowing what was on it, and it was answered on the sixth of Embermonth, in the spaces, in a fair hand, by the county court, on the ground that the office named in it no longer held a process against anybody because the endorsing of that morning had discharged the only process that office had in this county, and there is a difference between a process that has been discharged and a process that has never existed and this county is going to be relying on that difference for about a year.

So the bar came off the press at the ninth hour of the sixth of Embermonth, and it came off in the same hour that a one-sided form came up the stair from the Office of Provincial Registration, dated the third, returnable the eighth, with two ruled boxes on it and one word printed over each.

*Does the office named below hold any record relating to the declared object, in this or any other county?* **Yes / No.**

*If yes, state the county.*

---

Mara Vey read it twice and put it down and looked at the wall for about as long as it takes to count nine, and then she said, out loud, to nobody:

"That is the cleverest thing anybody has sent me in four years and a half, and it arrived by the same post as the thing that took the bar off, which I am going to think about for a year."

Sarn Oyelaran had it over her shoulder. "It is one word and one county."

"It is one word and one county," said Mara Vey, "and if I write *no*, then in four years there is a document in a provincial file in which this office says it holds no record relating to a declared object in any other county, and that document is false, and I will have made it, and it will be in a file, and I will have no chain on it and no way of proving what it means. And if I write *yes*, then I put a name on a Crown's form, and the name is a county, and counties are not people, and nobody can be served on a county, and nothing happens to me."

"Nothing happens to anybody," said Sarn Oyelaran. "That is what I am saying. Nothing happens to anybody at all."

"Yes," said Mara Vey. "And I am going to write *yes* anyway, and I am going to write the county, and here is the reason and I am going to read it back to you and you are going to tell me if it is a lawyer's reason or a keeper's."

She wrote it on the form and read it out.

"That this office holds one record relating to a declared object in a county called Ash, received on the first of Fallowmonth, YR 312, from a road that has not been described to this office by any person, in four lines and in a hand that is not a clerk's hand; that the said record is entered in this office's day-book against no person with the hour on it; that this office has jurisdiction in no county outside this one and asserts none; that this office is a keeper of records and not an office of the Crown and has no power to produce, withhold, or answer for anything in the said county; that the keeper of this office can be compelled to answer for this form and can be sued for this form and cannot be sued for the record, because the record is not the keeper's and is not in the keeper's custody and has never been copied; and that the reason this office is answering a form at all is that the alternative is a lie in a document, and this office has not put a lie in a document in four years and a half and is not going to start in the eighth month of a year in which a book has gone out of this county under a receipt for a box."

Sarn Oyelaran read it back and said, "That is a keeper's reason."

"Enter it with the hour," said Mara Vey, "and enter that I said *any other county* aloud in a room, twice, and that the second keeper heard it both times."

---

The other thing that morning was the second answer, and it was a problem, and the office spent four hours on it and did not solve it, and the not solving is the useful part.

The claim of the seventh of Fallowmonth is held in four places, and the four places work, and they work because the thing in them arrived on a road with a name on a day with a date on it and four books in two countries.

*That is Mara's rule and she states it in about eleven seconds when anybody asks:* four different kinds of holding, because one kind of holding is one kind of failure.

A custody, a lending, a fact, and a transaction.

The second answer could go in none of them.

It could not go in the toll-holder's chest, because a lending is entered on a roll with a day and an hour and an office that may produce it, and this thing had come on a road that no roll in this county has an entry for, and if it went into a roll it would acquire a provenance in a day-book that it does not have, and a thing with a false provenance is worse than a thing with none.

It could not go into the yard. The yard's rule is that a yard holds the *fact* of a paper and cannot produce the paper, and the fact of this paper is that a paper exists, and a fact that a paper exists is worth nothing to anybody, because the existence of a paper is not contested by anybody in this case; the contents are.

And it could not go to the salt merchant's brother in Harvestmonth, because that is a transaction at a farthing of salt and a transaction is a thing a man can be held to in a court, and a man in a lock-up on a canal wharf who has never been asked what the paper is cannot be asked about a county called Ash by a magistrate of the eastern counties in nine years.

"So it goes in the day-book," said Mara Vey, "and in the press, and those are two answers and the second one is bad."

The press came off its bar at the ninth hour. A press is a custody. A custody is the strongest holding there is, and it is the one a court can order produced, and she had spent four years and a half building a press on the principle that the strongest holding is the best holding, and the principle is right, and it has one failure, which is that a custody is a thing you can be *made* to produce, and she has just signed a form admitting that a record in the day-book cannot be sued for because it is not in anybody's custody.

"You have just written that the record cannot be sued for," said Sarn Oyelaran, "and you have just put it in a place it can be sued for."

"I know exactly what I have done," said Mara Vey, "and I did it in that order on purpose, and I would do it again, and the reason is that the form went out at the eleventh hour and the paper went in at the fourth, and if I had waited until I had built a fourth holding I should have had to lie in the gap, and a keeper who leaves a gap on purpose is a keeper who has stopped being a keeper and started being a person managing a situation. The paper goes in the press. If anybody ever wants it out of the press, they can have it out of the press, and on the day they do I shall be able to say that I put it there and I put the hour on it and I put a copy of this form underneath it with the same hour on it."

---

Then the lease, which had been sitting on the wall of that room like weather since Longmonth.

The salt merchant sent word up the stair at about the second hour in the afternoon, by the salt merchant's wife, who is sixty-eight and has wanted to look at that room for five months and has never once been asked to and was asked that afternoon because a woman with a press had finally run out of ways to not ask her.

"The second floor is not yours after Rainmonth," she said, from the doorway, in the voice of a woman delivering a fact about a lease. "I told you in Longlight that it was the second floor of a man who weighs salt and there is one other second floor on this street and it is let, and you wrote it down, and I have come to say that the man who lets the other one has been asking me what I am going to do about my own."

"How long have you known?"

"Since Wolfmonth of last year," said the salt merchant's wife. "That is when he asked me the first time and I did not tell him and he has asked eleven times since, and I have not told him, and I would like it entered that I did not tell him eleven times and that I am sixty-eight and that I did it because I am not a fool and I am not sentimental either and the second floor has been let to a woman with a press for four years and I have never once regretted it and I have never once said so to anybody until this afternoon."

Mara Vey wrote nothing on that and made a note to write nothing on that, because a leaseholder's reason for not telling his landlord is not a record and is the most private sentence she has been given in four years and she is going to carry it and not use it.

"Thank you," she said. "What do you want?"

"Two rooms and a door," said the salt merchant's wife. "Anywhere with a door on a lane, because a stair is how the Crown's man comes up on you and a door is how he has to knock. And I will not come and look at it. That is your work and not mine and I have been in your room twice this year and I am not going to be a third."

So Mara Vey went and looked at rooms, and the yard on Weir Street is where she started, because Weir Street is four doors and a yard and a foundry and the coopers, and there is a man in it who has priced every foot of that street in the last eleven years and never once charged anybody for saying so.

---

Tovan Kest was planing a bench-board when she came into the gateway and she had a list on a sheet of paper, which he read upside down, and did not comment on, and then she said, "Four rooms in this county with a door onto a lane, and I have found three, and I want a man who has been in all of them to tell me before I sign anything, and I am not asking you for a favour. I am asking you for a price."

"Over the chandler's at the north end," said Tovan Kest, "there is a loft with a door on the lane and it has been empty since the man died, and the man's widow wants four pence a year and the water is up in a wet year, and the door is a good door and the hinges are on the near side so a man comes in and not out, and that is worth three pence of the four." He put the plane down. "And I will tell you the two things you have not asked me about, and then you can go and sign the lease and I will not come and look at it, because that is your work and not mine."

"Go on."

"First. That loft has got one room and not four, and one room is what you can keep a press in and a day-book in and a person in, and you have got a second keeper and you have got a boy of fourteen coming up that stair, and a man from an office is going to come and look at your shelves, and you are going to find out in about eleven weeks that one room is two rooms' work and no more." He picked the plane up again. "Second. My yard has got four weeks in it and it is spoken for, and the factor's stone is gone, and I told you on Friday in a gateway with four men in it and I am telling you again in a gateway with one man in it and it is the same number. I am telling you twice because the first time I said it to four men and a carrier and you were not one of them and I would rather you had it from me than from the Market Square board."

"Thank you," said Mara Vey.

"And the third thing," said Tovan Kest, "and I am saying it because I have been not saying it for five days and it has come to the front of my mouth twice this week. I have not asked you where he went. I have wanted to ask you every day since the ninth and I have not asked, and I want you to know that the not asking has been a *trade* and not a virtue, because the last time he came to this town and told a thing and did not ask, it cost me a day and a half and four men and a bench, and I decided in Longlight that I would never again be the man who was not told, and so I have not asked, and there is a line in my book on the second of Fallowmonth with a rule over it, and you are welcome to come and read it, and you are not going to like it."

Mara Vey did not read it that afternoon. She went back up the stair and paid the widow of the north end four pence for a year, one room, a door on the lane, and got a receipt for four pence in a tradesman's hand, and entered the transaction in the day-book against no person with the hour on it, and the entry is the smallest paid item in the business and it is the second time that has been true of a line in that book, and the first time was a farthing of salt, and the two are four times apart in price and about four hundred miles apart in kind, and she wrote under both of them one line and no more:

*That the sum of four pence is entered here because the room has a door on a lane and the door is on the hinges for a man coming in, and that this office has now been moved twice by a lease and has paid twice for the privilege of having a door, and that a door on a lane is the only thing this business has ever bought that a marshal of a Crown cannot serve a writ on, and that it cost four pence, and that I would like somebody in about eleven years to compare that figure with the four marks and six a year this county pays a bench of nine to keep a bridge.*
