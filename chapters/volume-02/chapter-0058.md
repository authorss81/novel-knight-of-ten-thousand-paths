# Chapter 0058

The keeper of the Bellweather Claims Archive had been in the loft over the chandler's at the north end for two working days, and on the second of them she found out that a room with a press in it and a day-book in it and a person in it is not a room, it is a corridor with three heavy things in it, and she wrote that down on the second of her first four days in the room and not on the first, because on the first she had been carrying a matlock and a stool and a boy with a book.

The door is on the hinges for a man coming in, and the hinges are on the near side, and it cost four pence a year, and it is the second time this office has been moved by a lease and the fourth time it has paid for the privilege of a door.

What put her at the long table at the eighth hour of the eighth of Embermonth was a form.

The form had gone down the county post at the eleventh hour of the sixth, on a sheet with one word and one county in it, and the county was Ash, and she had written it herself and read it out to a second keeper and had said, in that room, *anything else is a lie in a document.* And a keeper who has put a county into a Crown's form is not permitted the rest of that day to go on thinking about anything except that county, and this office had been not thinking about it for six weeks, and the only thing it held that could be asked a question about a county was a page with nine lines on it and no name in any of them.

The bar had come off the press at the ninth hour of the sixth. It had been on that press since the ninth of Longlight, and it had been on it for a bar that was discharged by a registrar's endorsement printed on the back of a form series, and a thing that has been under a bar since the ninth of Longlight and came off it in an hour because of a four-line endorsement is the most instructive object in this county, and she wrote that down on the seventh and had thought about it every hour since.

"Take out the index page," she said. "Not the line. The index."

"Are you sure?" said Sarn Oyelaran. "The conditions are on the wrapper and they are on the wrapper of both of them and they are binding on this office and I did not write them and I cannot waive them."

"I am going to read it and not copy it, and I am going to do it in this room with you in it, and if anybody in this county ever wants to say that a keeper of records read a document that was not produced, they may come up the stair and watch me not write, and I shall be glad of the company."

---

It was one leaf, and it was a fair copy of a fair copy, and it was the front leaf of a register of four hundred leaves kept by the toll houses of a city that does not admit to the Crown that it exists.

The head of it carried a press-mark: **press four, die eleven**, and under it, in a smaller hand, and in the first impression only, *first impression of the season.*

Below the press-mark was the heading, and the heading was *Index of the register of outgoing writs and tenders, with dispositions.*

Four columns. *County. Date of tender. Returned. Answered.*

It took about a minute to read the first eight, and after the minute the keeper of the Bellweather Claims Archive said, out loud, to a room over a chandler's with a door on a lane in it:

"Eight writs of severance, all of them against Alder Vale, all of them inside about a week in the year the nineteenth of Mudmonth was, and the *returned* column is filled in eight times out of eight, and the *answered* column is empty in all eight."

"And the first of the returns is the twentieth," said Sarn Oyelaran, "which is six days, which is a bench on a bad day and not a river."

"Six days is a bench," said Mara Vey. "So this county was asked eight times in four days by an office with a schedule, and it said no eight times, and every one of the eight is on a bench minute or a book order with a man's name at the foot of it, and there is not one name in this leaf anywhere, and the office that made the eight could not tell you today who said no."

The ninth line was out of order, and being out of order was the whole of it.

It sat in the middle of the Alder Vale run. The *county* cell was empty. The date of tender was in the three hundred and fifth year, which is four years on. The *returned* cell was empty, and being empty was not an omission, because a cell that is empty because there was nothing to return looks different from a cell that is empty because it was never filled in, and Sarn Oyelaran put her thumbnail on the two and said that they were the same ink and the same nib-width and the same angle of one hand, and that the clerk had ruled the cell and left it, which is a thing a clerk does when the answer to the question is that there was no answer to the question, or when there is no column for the answer.

And the *answered* cell had a date in it, and it was eleven days after the tender.

"Eleven days," said Mara Vey.

"Eleven days after the tender," said Sarn Oyelaran, "and I am going to say the thing I have been not saying since the seventh hour, which is that eleven days is a clerk's answer and not a river's, and I can give you both numbers. Six days to say no, and six days is a bench. Eleven days to say something, and eleven days is a man writing to a superior and waiting to be told he may."

"And then the superior said he may," said Mara Vey.

"Or the superior said nothing for eleven days and the man did what he was going to do anyway," said Sarn Oyelaran, "and that is the same document with a worse story in it, and I would rather not have the worse story."

---

So the writ at Alder Vale was refused. The writ at a county this leaf does not name was complied with, and a compliance is not a thing that happens to a document. It is a thing that happens to a person who tenders one, and it leaves behind a piece of paper, and the paper is in a book in a city four hundred miles off, and the book is four hundred leaves long and its front leaf is the thing in this room.

Mara Vey read the ninth line four times and then did not read it a fifth time, and she wrote one fact, in the fair hand, and it is the only thing this office has about it.

*Entered on the office's own account, against no person, at the tenth hour of the eighth of Embermonth, YR 312, that a document held in this office, being a fair copy of a fair copy of one leaf of an index of a register of outgoing writs, of which eight lines are of Alder Vale and the ninth is not, and of which the ninth carries a county cell with nothing in it, a tender in the three hundred and fifth year, no entry in the column headed Returned, and an entry in the column headed Answered of a date eleven days after the tender; that the said ninth line carries a press-mark of press four, die eleven and the words second impression of the season, where the press-mark at the head of the said leaf carries the same press and the same die and the words first impression of the season; and that this office has entered the fact and has entered no opinion, and the three refusals are set out immediately beneath and are part of this entry.*

Then she wrote the three refusals, and she wrote them out in full, and she wrote them out because a refusal that is not written down is a thing a person did once.

*First. That the said ninth line is of the county called Ash. It is not said to be. The county cell is empty and this office will not fill an empty cell in a copy of a copy in a book held in another country, and the reason it will not is that a county cannot be served, cannot be refused, and cannot be asked a question, and if this office writes a name into a cell that the register has left empty then this office becomes the source of a county, and a source is the only thing in this business that cannot be corrected by anybody who finds out afterwards that it was wrong.*

*Second. That the said ninth line was made in the same hand as the first eight. It may have been. A hand in a register of a creditor's office does not leave that office. A hand cannot be produced. A hand is not evidence, it is a resemblance, and a resemblance is what a man finds when he wants one, and this office has four years and a half of evidence that a man who wants a resemblance will go a long way to get one and will not put the fare in.*

*Third. That the paper of the first of Fallowmonth, received at this office from a road this office did not ask for, is the answer entered against the said ninth line. It may be. A date can be looked for. A press-mark cannot be argued with. A hand cannot be produced. And a county that is not on a map cannot be asked a question. The paper of the first of Fallowmonth is a question, and it was put to this office by somebody, and it was answered by a keeper who wrote down in a fair hand that she could not answer it, and there is no way on this earth of getting from a four-line letter in a hand that is not a clerk's to a ninth line in a column in a book four hundred miles off, and the reason there is no way is that making the join would be a copy, and this office has been forbidden to make a copy since the second of Fallowmonth by four sentences on an oiled wrapper written by somebody else's clerk, and those sentences bind this office and this office did not write them and cannot waive them.*

"Read the last sentence back to me," said Sarn Oyelaran.

Mara Vey read it back.

"That is the best sentence you have written in this office," said Sarn Oyelaran, "and it is about this office, and I am going to be difficult with you for the rest of the year and you are going to have to be difficult with me, and here is the first of it. You have refused more things in this building in four years and a half than anybody I have worked beside in my life and you have never once written down a thing you wanted. I am not asking you to enter a want. I am asking you to notice that the wanting is now the only part of this business that is not in a book, and that in about four years a man is going to be able to say exactly what you knew on the eighth of Embermonth, YR 312, and how you came to know it, and it will be because of what is on a leaf and not because of what is in you."

---

Then she put the other document on the table beside it, and the second document is the reason the first one is a leaf and not a book.

It is four lines long and it is a fair copy of a fair copy of a line out of the body of the same register, and it is the line for the nineteenth of Mudmonth, YR 301, and it is the line that has been in this office since the second of Fallowmonth under four conditions on an oiled wrapper, and it is the document this case was made of.

And it has a name in it.

It has a name in it because the register has a column headed *tendered by*, and a creditor's book has to have a column headed *tendered by*, because a creditor who does not know which officer of a Crown took a toll off him is a creditor who cannot complain, and the column for the nineteenth of Mudmonth, YR 301, says *Edric Hunn, marshal.*

And the index leaf in front of her says, in four columns, and in nine lines, and in no letter anywhere, who was answered.

The same book. Four hundred leaves. The name is in the book and the fact is in the book and they are eleven pages apart in a volume in a counting house in a city that does not admit to the Crown that it exists, and the conditions on the wrapper say that no copy of anything may be made, and that a fair copy of this office's may be kept in this office's press unsigned and not produced in court and held in this city, and that the fact leaves and the line does not, and there is no motive; and the register itself has not been asked for by anybody and will not be, and the reason it has not been asked for is that a creditor's register is a private book of a private trade, and a Crown's court has no jurisdiction over a private book in another country, and every man who has ever wanted one has been told so by a clerk in about eleven seconds.

"They are in the same hand," said Mara Vey.

"No," said Sarn Oyelaran. "Look at them properly and then do not say anything for a minute and then look at them again."

They looked at them for about a minute.

"They are in two hands," said Mara Vey. "The line is in a hand that is copying beautifully and copying fast, and the index is in a hand that is copying correctly and copying slowly, and the two are not the same man, and there is a third hand somewhere in that book because the withdrawal of that writ on the twenty-second is not in either of these two documents and it is in a third."

"Yes," said Sarn Oyelaran. "So the two documents that this office holds about the same book are in two different hands, and not one of the two hands is the hand of a man who signed anything, and the thing that ties them together is a press-mark, and a press-mark is not a hand and is not a man and cannot be asked a single question."

"Write the two hands in," said Mara Vey. "That is a fact and it is checkable by anybody who has the two leaves, and there are two people in this world who have the two leaves and one of us is in this room and neither of us can produce either of them in a court."

And at the fourth hour she did the other thing.

It is the eighth ruled line in a served schedule. Seven villages by name, in the county's spelling, figures separately, `sum not stated` in the total line, and eight ruled lines with the eighth ruled and empty, and a reason under it copied in the hand of a county boy of nineteen who asked whether it would still be on the sheet in a year.

She had looked at it for about a minute and a half on the twenty-ninth of Fallowmonth, with a lamp, and had written in the day-book that she wanted to fill it in and did not.

She looked at it now for about the same length of time, with no lamp, in a room she had been in for two days, and the thing she was looking at was a second empty cell, in a different document, in a different country, in a different hand.

*Eighth of Embermonth, YR 312, fourth hour. I have looked at the eighth ruled line a second time, ten days after the first, and I have looked at it with a different thing in my head, and I have wanted to fill it a second time, and I enter that I wanted to, and I enter that the wanting is not the same wanting, and I am not going to write what is in the cell and I am not going to write why.*

*And I enter, because it is the same woman doing it and it took me four days to see it: that on Monday I put a county into a Crown's form, and that on the ninth line of a leaf in this office's press there is a cell with nothing in it that has been empty for seven years, and that I have now been in one room with three empty cells in one week, and that a woman who fills cells is how a county gets to be a thing that can be counted.*

And then, because a keeper who enters a want without a date is a keeper with a mood, she put the two entries side by side, and read them both, and wrote one line under the pair, which is the last line in the day-book that day and the second one in four years with nothing against it and no reason on it:

*On the twenty-ninth of Fallowmonth I wanted to fill that line because I did not know what was in it. Today I wanted to fill it because I think I know. A cell that a man wants to fill because he knows what goes in it is not a cell any more, it is a mouth, and this office has spent four years and a half refusing to be a mouth, and I am the keeper of it, and the person who is going to be asked about the eighth line in nine years is going to be me.*

She shut the book, and then she opened it again, and wrote one more entry, against no person, with the hour, and it is the entry that decides what a person in another country is going to have to do about this in about seven months, and which nobody in the County of Ash will ever read.

*And that the only kind of holding that has ever answered a question in this case is a holding that cannot be served, and that this office has one, and that it is a room on a canal wharf in another county entered at the price of a farthing of salt, and that it is a room with a man in it who has never been asked about anything in eleven years; and that it is not going to be used for this, and the reason is that a roll of persons in a lock-up on a wharf is a thing a creditor could sell, and the whole of this business has been four years of finding out what a creditor does with a book of other people's lives; and that if this office is right about what is wanted, then somebody has to make another one of these, in the country where the register is, out of his own book, at his own cost, on his own paper, with his own name at the head of it, in a matter in which no court in this county has jurisdiction; and that this office cannot do that, cannot order it, and will not pretend to anybody that it can.*

---

And on the same afternoon, four hundred yards down Weir Street, a boy of fourteen came into a gateway with a sheet of paper in his hand that had not come by the post.

It was four lines long and it was in a hand that is not a clerk's hand, and it had a date on it in a calendar this county does not use, and it named two people, and one of them had not given her name to anybody in this world and the other was a person the county had chosen and had never met the man the paper was written for.

The yard had four men in it. The boy read it out loud because he was fourteen and because nobody told him not to, and the fourth line was the one Tovan Kest read out again afterwards with the sheet in his own hand:

*We were asked a question by a stranger and we have written our answer down, and we do not know the answer, and the writing of it is the answer, and the names are at the top because a road in this county is kept by a hand and a road is nothing at all without a hand and a witness, and we have never had one and we are not going to be asked for one twice.*
