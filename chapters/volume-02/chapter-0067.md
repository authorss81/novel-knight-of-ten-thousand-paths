# Chapter 0067

Hesper Lound was sixty-three and he was the only physician in the County of Ash, and he had been the only one for nineteen years, and he had a room over a chandler's shop in Kilnholt with a table in it and a drawer in the table, and on the twelfth day of the twenty-three days he was going to be in this county, which was the thirty-third day of the rolling — the thirty-third day of the rolling, and Aren Kest had stopped being able to think in days of the rolling by about the fifth — a man who was a sett-cutter was let into it. He had stopped being able to think in days of the rolling by about the fifth, the way a man stops thinking in weeks.

He was let in by a girl of about fourteen who was carrying a basket of fired tile to a room behind and who did not ask him anything, and the reason he was let in was that the physician's door is the one door in the County of Ash that has never once been shut on anybody, and it has not been shut on anybody not because of kindness but because a physician who shuts a door is a physician who is about to find out something, and this county cannot afford to have anybody shut.

The drawer was in the table and it did not open easily and he opened it and there was about forty pages in it, and forty pages in a county of nine hundred and forty-one hearths is not a small number of words, and about four of them were written in a hand that was better than the hand of the man with the ledger.

"Sit down," said Hesper Lound. "No. Not there, that is the stool I sit on and the arm of it is off of the same as the chair at the assizes, and I would not have a man's hip in the same state as mine."

He sat on the other side of the table with the drawer between them and four pages out of forty and squared, and the physician put a finger on the top page and did not turn it over, and said:

"I wrote that in the third year of the softening and I have had it in this drawer for four years and I have read it about nine times and I have shown it to one person, once, and that was the keeper of your roll, and she read it in that chair you have not sat in, in about nine minutes, and she thanked me, and she has never asked for it again, and I have never offered. In four years. I have never offered it to a magistrate, a bench, a kiln, or a man with a ledger, and I want to tell you why before I read it, because a man who is read to thinks about the reading and not the thing, and I have watched that happen to me eleven hundred times."

"Go on."

"Because a physician's finding in this county is a piece of paper in a drawer with a man's name on the top of it, and the day I take it out of the drawer it stops being a finding and becomes a thing a magistrate may use," said Hesper Lound, "and I have watched a magistrate use a physician's page three times in nineteen years and I have never once seen one of the three improve anything, and in two of the three the physician was a fool and in the third the magistrate was a decent man who took a true thing and put it in his own instrument, which is worse, and I would rather that happened with a thing that was mine than with a thing that was his."

He squared the four pages again.

"Now you are going to ask me why I am doing it today."

"You are not," said Aren Kest.

"No," said the physician. "I am doing it because you are a stranger and you have been in this county twelve days and you came out of a road, and in nineteen years three people have come out of a road in this county and two of them were the same man and one of them was me, in the fourth year, and I would like a thing I have written to be read out loud by somebody who has not read it, and I would like it read out loud in a room with a table, and I would like to be wrong about it in front of a person and not in a drawer."

He turned the page.

---

"Four pages," he said, "and I shall do them in order, and you are to stop me wherever you like, and I shall go on, because the last one is the one I have wanted to say out loud for four years and I am not going to be stopped in front of a stranger in a room over a chandler's."

He read it out. It was in the plainest hand the man had ever read in his life, and it had almost no numbers in it, and the reason it had almost no numbers in it was, he said, that a physician who puts a number in a finding has decided that a thing can be counted, and the thing this finding is about cannot be counted and had been counted for four years.

"**One. The softening enters by the mark.** Not through the eye. Through the mark. A man is at a fire and a person reads a name off a tile in that room, and it is not that the man believes it or doubts it. It is that the man's day, which had a shape this morning, has not got a shape now, and he cannot tell you what has happened to it, and the order of it is the first thing to go, and then his trade, and then his hearth, and then his name. And a man who is read enters it. And a man who is read carries it into the marks of his household, into his wife's tile and his brother's tile and the tile of a boy in his house, and there it is quicker, because those marks are read less often and a mark that is read rarely is a mark that has not yet been read at all, and a person is safe from this only until somebody in that house opens a book.

"**Two. It travels fastest along the marks that are read most.**" He put a finger on the second page. "I have gone into this and I have got it and I am not going to soften it for a room. The marks that are read most in this county are the roll marks. Every morning a clerk sits in a room at the assizes with the roll in front of her and reads every name on it, and she does not know that she is doing anything and she is not doing anything, she is checking for a second that she is not behind, and a man whose name is read every morning is a man whose day is in that room every morning, and I have watched it for four years in about nine houses and it is the only regularity in it that I have never once been able to break.

"**Three. Therefore the cure is not fewer records.**" He came up off the page for that one and said it into the room. "I want it entered that I wrote that sentence last, and that I rewrote it eleven times, and that I took eleven months to be sure of it, and that I am certain of it and that a person is going to tell me in about a minute why it is not true. Fewer records is what a frightened office does. Fewer records is what this county has wanted to do for four years, and it wanted to do it before I had finished writing, and the magistrate's four lines about burning a list is a frightened office doing fewer records at a scale of two hundred. It cannot work. It cannot work because a record that is not read cannot be corrected, and a record that cannot be corrected is not a record, it is a claim, and this county has been living on claims for four years and the claims are what is killing it.

"**Four. The cure is records that do not agree with each other.** Three copies. Not one roll kept carefully in three chests, which is one roll, and the keeper of the roll will tell you that in nine minutes if you let her. Three copies that differ, made by three hands, kept in three places, so that no one of them is the record — they are three accounts of the same person, and they are expected to disagree, and a person is restored when two of the three agree, and a person is removed when two of the three agree otherwise, and the third can be wrong, and the third being wrong is not a fault, it is the arrangement. And no single office can do either. I have written that last part eleven times and every time it reads like a way of losing a roll. It is not. It is a way of making sure that one man, or one woman, or one locked press, or one morning of bad temper, cannot take two hundred people off a county by writing down two hundred names."

He put the four pages down and squared them and put his hand flat on them.

"Eleven years I have been the only man in this county who can be asked to look at a person," he said. "And I have written about eleven hundred pages, and four of them are on that table, and three of them have been read by somebody, and one of them has been read twice. The only thing wrong with the County of Ash is not the fever. The only thing wrong with the County of Ash is that nobody has ever read a physician's finding out loud."

---

"There," said Pernel Quist, from the doorway.

She had been in the room for the whole of it, standing behind the door with her arms folded, and she came in and put her hand flat on the last of the four pages and looked at it for about a second and a half.

"No," she said.

"Lound—"

"No, and I will say why, and I have had four years to say it and I have been saying it to myself in a chair for four years and I have never once said it to him, and I am saying it now because you have read it out loud and because you read it out loud to a man who came out of a road and a man who came out of a road is the only person in this county who has no reason to protect me, and I would like it on the point that I am saying it." She took the page off his hand. "He has said three copies. Three. And I have kept one roll for nineteen years and I have not been wrong in nineteen years, and every mistake in this room in nineteen years has been mine, and there have been nine of them, and eight of them are dead, and I know all nine, and I could not tell you where the other one is. And he wants three, in three hands, in three places, and he wants a person restored when two of the three agree."

"That is the finding."

"That is *a* finding," said Pernel Quist, "and it is a finding about a thing I have not got, and I am going to say the hard part, because I have said it to myself four years and I am tired of it. Two rolls is an admission that the first one can be wrong. This office has never admitted that anything it wrote could be wrong and it never will, because the day it admits that is the day the roll is a copy, and a copy is a second thing somebody can lose. Three rolls is not three rolls. Three rolls is a county with three offices in it, and three offices means three clerks and three sets of rules and three arguments about a man's tile instead of one, and in nine years there will be a place in this county where a woman can be struck from the count by two of three, and the two will be a room and a press, and there will be no third thing anybody can go and look at, and in nine years that will have happened, and it will have happened because a physician read a page out loud in a room with a table in it."

She put the page down and squared it, in his own habit, in his own room, and he let her.

"You are right about two of three," said Hesper Lound. "You are entirely right, and I have said so eleven times in four pages, and you have not read past the third sentence, and I would like you to have my apology for that and I am not going to repeat the sentence, because the second time a person hears it they hear it as an argument and not as a fact, and it is a fact: **two of the three may be wrong, and one of the three may be wrong, and no one of the three is the roll.**"

"And who says which two?"

"The person," said the physician. "That is the entire difference and it is the whole of what you have not got, and it is the reason I have put this in a drawer for four years and it is the reason you have read it in nine minutes and been right in nine minutes. In your office a person does not say anything. In mine a person says everything, and the two of three is the two that the person and the record agree on, and the record that disagrees with the person is the record that is wrong, and there is no such thing in this county as a record that is right and the person who is wrong, and I have looked for one for four years and I have not found it, and I have found about two hundred men for whom it would have been a very useful thing to have found."

Nobody said anything for about a minute. The physician looked at the four pages and then at the keeper of the roll, and the keeper of the roll looked at the four pages, and it was in that minute, and both of them knew it was in that minute, and eleven years of one and nineteen years of the other went into a room over a chandler's and nothing was said about it.

---

Hesper Lound turned to the third person in the room.

"You are the one who came out of the road," he said, "and I have read you four pages of a thing I wrote four years ago and you have not said one word, and you have not said one word because you are the best reader of a room in this county and the best reader of a room does not speak until the room has finished, and I have been in rooms with men like you and they are worth nothing, and I want your opinion, and I am asking you for it, and I have asked four people in nineteen years and three of them were my own patients and one of them was a magistrate and he is dead."

"Don't," said Aren Kest.

"I have not said anything yet."

"You are about to," he said, "and I am going to stop you, and I am going to do it in the plainest words I have got, and I would like them written down if anybody here keeps a record of this room, and I do not think anybody here keeps a record of this room, and I would like that entered too if there is anybody who does."

There was a pause, and Pernel Quist said, "There is not."

"Right," said Aren Kest. "Then here is the thing, and it is a refusal, and I have not made one in a room for about eleven years, and I made the last one in a hall in eleven seconds, and it was the correct one, and this one is going to take longer and be worse.

"If I tell this room not to burn that list, I am telling a county with a fever in it to keep a list, on my word. I am the man who put the list there by arguing about it in front of witnesses, or I will be, in nine years, or a hundred, and I am the man who does that in a hat. I have been doing this for eleven years and I am not going to do it again in a hat.

"And I cannot tell you what the four pages mean for the twenty-fourth of this month, because I have been in your county twelve days and I have one set of hands, and I do not know what a tile is, and I do not know what a clerk's morning is, and if I stand in this room and tell you that the reading is the disease, then in four years a magistrate with a stove and a column of figures will be able to say that the man off the road said so, and he will be able to say it with a date and a place, and he will be right, and he will be able to do more with one sentence of mine than your four pages have done in four years, and that is not a thing I am going to hand a county because somebody asked me politely in a room with a table in it."

"You have got a fortnight," said Pernel Quist.

"I have not got anything," said Aren Kest. "That is the first honest thing I have said in this county. I came in here for the fifth time in twelve days and I have been asked three times now what I think, by a magistrate and by a physician and by a woman with a basket, and three times I have had a thing I could have said, and every one of the three would have been listened to, because I am the only man in this county who came from somewhere else and there is nothing else to be listened to.

"That is the whole of what I have. **I am the reason anybody in this room has to be careful, and I have been the reason for about a year and a half, and I have never once been in a room where I could tell.**"
