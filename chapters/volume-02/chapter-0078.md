# Chapter 0078

By the fourth week he had stopped being asked what he was for, and that was the best thing that had happened to him in eleven years.

The hand of the Ninebridge had said it on the first morning — *useful and not a visitor* — and he had understood it as a courtesy, and it turned out to be a specification. In the fourth week a kiln-man at the loading end moved a board out of his way without looking up, and a boy at a rack said "fourth row" instead of "the sett-cutter," and the two hundred and six of them had been asked once each by a cord-cutter and an ash-driver in their own sheds and had answered, and about nine of them had come to the cutting shed at the low end of the rack and stood about for an hour a day and not asked him anything, which is a thing a county does when it has run out of the kind of stranger it knows how to be polite to.

What brought the two hundred and six into one room was not the struck and not the physician. It was the price.

---

The drying floor over the cart shed at the Ninebridge is ninety-one feet long and it has a stove at one end and racks down both sides and no windows that anybody opens, and on the fourth week about two hundred people were in it, which is a great many people for ninety-one feet, and they were there from the second hour to the sixth.

Everybody in the room was there because of what they were paid by. That is the whole of the room. The bench came because a bench is the only body in this county that meets. The four kilns came because a kiln is an employer and this is the employer's county. The clerk of the roll came because where a rollkeeper goes she goes with the roll. And about two hundred of the struck came because they had been asked once and had answered, and the person who had asked them had said, in a shed, *if the answer is that you want to be on the roll again, then come to the drying floor on the fourth week and stand in it*, and about two hundred of them had come.

Magistrate Colm Arreld was not in the room.

He was four days away at the assizes with a matter about a boundary and he had left a paper, and the paper is in the county's own book and it is three lines long and it is the third instrument he has refused to make and the first one he has refused in writing.

*I am not giving this room an instruction and I will not be asked for one in a room of two hundred people, because the last instrument I made in this matter is on the table and untried, and a magistrate who makes a second thing before the first has been tried is a magistrate who has decided the first was wrong, and it may not have been, and if it was then I shall hear about it from the roll and not from myself.*

Somebody read it out. Nobody argued with it. In a county that has no word for *refused* that does not also mean *lost*, a magistrate putting a paper on a table and going four days away is a thing the room can read perfectly, and it reads as *he is not here and he is not against us and he is not for us and he has gone to be in a room where the other thing is being tried*.

And it is worth saying plainly, because it is the shape of the whole of this volume and it is easier to see in a room than in a book: **nobody in that room was arguing about whether the struck should be put back on the roll. They had all four positions on that within four weeks and had had them since the twenty-second day. What they were arguing about was what putting two hundred and six people back on the roll costs, and the cost had a number in it, and the number was three times the tiles.**

---

The man at the cutting bench got up first, because he is the loudest man in this county and always has been and it is not vanity, it is that a man in a shed who is not heard is a man who is not in the room.

"Right," he said. "Nobody is going to say *we are right* and nobody is going to say *you are wrong*, because both of those are free. I am going to say a price and then I am going to sit down, and the price is the truth, and I do not want anybody in this room to answer it tonight."

He put his hand on the end of a rack.

"Two hundred and six people. Every one of them needs one tile, and not a tile a clerk has found for them — a tile with **their own right hand on the back**, pressed by them, in wet clay, because that is the only part of this county's records that is anybody's at all, and a tile without a hand on the back is a thing about a fire."

"About a third of what comes out of a rolling is fit to fire out of the hand and keep," he said. "The other two are cracked in the dry, or warped on the shelf, or soft at the edge, and they go back to the wedging shed and they are clay again by the next week. So for two hundred and six tiles that come out right you put in **six hundred and eighteen.**" He said it the way he said everything, which was like a man laying setts. "Six hundred and eighteen. And a rolling gives about forty fit to keep. So you want **fifteen rollings.**"

"And this county does five in a season," said the ash-driver.

"This county does five in a season," said the man at the cutting bench, "and it does them because it is replacing what breaks and it is firing a few hundred for new hearths, and it has done five every year for nineteen years. Fifteen is three times five. **That is where three comes from and it is not a figure anybody has chosen.**"

Nobody in the room wrote it down. Everybody in the room had already had it.

"Nine days to a rolling, a cord a day, so fifteen rollings is a hundred and thirty-five cords of ash. This county cuts about forty-five cords in a season. So you want **ninety cords more than we have ever cut in one year.** And a cord of ash at the kiln gate is two shillings and sixpence, and the cutting of ninety cords is about forty men for a day at five pence, and the clay is not bought because the clay is the pit."

He took his hand off the rack and wiped it on his coat, and the man at the cutting bench in this county had hands like a plate and had been a man at a cutting bench for nineteen years and had never once in nineteen years needed to explain a figure to a room.

---

He sat down. And a woman of about sixty who had not spoken stood up, and it was the woman who had said the sentence on the first morning, and the man at the loading end put his hand on her arm and she shook it off, and he let go.

"The ninety cords," she said, "is not the price. I want to say what the price is, and I have said in this room that the price is thirty-nine days of the wages, and he has just costed the wood and the cutting, and both of those are right, and both of those are what a kiln man can see."

"Say it then," said the man at the cutting bench. "I have not come here to be flattered."

"You know what it costs. It is ninety cords and forty men's days. The price is that **we do not have ninety cords and we cannot cut them**, because the cutting is done in the same weeks as the digging, and the digging is the season, and a man cannot be in the ash with a saw in the fourth week of the rolling and have his own hearth burnt down in the same week. And in this county a hearth is what a fire is, and a fire is what a person is, and there is no column in this county for a person."

"That is right," said the man at the cutting bench.

"So there it is. A county that fires three times the tiles has to cut three times the wood in the same weeks as the digging, and about forty men lose a day, and the county cannot pay forty men for a day it has not got, and a county that cannot pay a man for a day does not go and beg." She looked round the ninety-one feet of it. "A county that cannot pay a man for a day puts him on a list. And the kilns can do that sum in a morning, and the magistrate can do his in a year, and they are the same sum, and one of them has a law attached to it and the other one has a price, and a price is not a law but a price is the one that gets kept."

---

The clerk of the roll had said nothing for about an hour.

Then she stood up, and she had a sheet of the roll out of the box under her arm, and she did not read it, and the room understood that she was going to refuse to read it and understood why about eleven seconds later, when she said it.

"I am not going to read the roll out in this room," she said. "I have said no to that in this county's own book in a corridor nine years ago and I have never taken it back and I am not taking it back now with two hundred people in front of me, because the day I read that book out in a room is the day it is a thing that can be read out, and then it will be read out in a room where the room is not me."

"Quist," said the magistrate's room, which was empty.

"I have got a figure and it is a figure I have had for nineteen years and it is the only figure I have ever been proud of." She turned the sheet over so that nobody could see it, which is a thing a keeper does, and then she did not go on for about four seconds, which is a thing a keeper does twice.

"**Nine hundred and forty-one hearths on the roll.** A hearth is a fire and a fire is what a person is and I count this county and not myself and I have never in nineteen years been short."

She said it to about two hundred people in a drying floor and the room did not stop, and she had intended not to say it, and she said it anyway, and the reason she said it is the next thing out of her mouth and it is the reason a person does a thing they have decided not to do.

"I decided thirty seconds ago that I was not going to give you that figure in a room today, and I was wrong, and here is why, and I want it in whatever you are keeping. **I have spent nineteen years not wanting that number to be a number, and today there are two hundred people in this room who are not on it, and I would rather be the woman who said it out loud than have a stranger work it out of a kiln wall in about a minute and a half and come and ask me whether I knew.** And if the number is going to be said in this county then I am going to be the one who says it and I am going to be the one who is standing here when it is said, and that is the whole of the difference between a figure and a count and it is who is in the room."

"Then say the number of men," said Vaile.

"I have not got the number of men in this county," said Pernel Quist, "and I have said so to a stranger and to a physician and to a magistrate and I am saying it to a room, and I have said it four times now and every time somebody has looked at me as though I had refused, and I have not refused, I have answered, and the answer is *I do not know it*, and a keeper who puts a want in a cell where she has a fact is a mouth and not a cell, and I learned that from a man in another county in about a minute and a half."

The room laughed, and it was not a cruel noise.

---

It went on until about the fifth hour and then the ash-driver stood up and put the whole of it in a column on a board, in a hand that was not a clerk's hand, and the boy with the slate copied it, and the boy copied the blanks too, which is what that boy does, and it is the only thing about him that anybody has ever said.

*Two hundred and six to be put back. Each requires one tile with the person's own hand on the back. One tile in three is fit. Six hundred and eighteen into the kiln. Forty fit to a rolling. Fifteen rollings wanted. The county has done five in a season, in every year, for nineteen years. Three times the tiles.*
*Nine days to a rolling, a cord a day. A hundred and thirty-five cords wanted. Forty-five cut in a season. Ninety cords more than this county has ever cut in one year.*
*Ash at the kiln gate, two shillings and sixpence the cord: ninety cords, two thousand seven hundred pence, fifty-six marks and one shilling.*
*Cutting: about forty men, one day, five pence the day: two hundred pence, four marks and eight pence.*
*The whole of the extra season: two thousand nine hundred pence, sixty marks one shilling eightpence.*
*Men whose day is lost if it is done in the digging: about forty.*
*Men out if two kilns stand idle for a rolling: about forty.*

Nobody said anything about the last line for about half a minute.

"That is not the same forty," said the man at the loading end.

"It is not," said the ash-driver, "and it is about the same forty, and I have written both because both of them are true and the room has to have both of them or it has got a nicer story than the county does."

"It is a nicer story," said the man at the cutting bench. "A man who loses a day and a man who loses a season are not the same man and a room that adds them together has been told a story it likes. I have been in this room for an hour listening to the best argument I have heard in four years and I want it entered that the last two lines are not the same and that I do not want them added."

"Entered," said the ash-driver, and wrote it under the column, and it is in the county's own book, in a hand that is not a clerk's, on a page headed with a number that nobody had ever put at the top of a page before, and the page is a page about people and not about tiles, and it is the first one in this county.

And the last of the two hundred and six to speak was Ostry Vaile, who is fifty-eight and fires tiles at the Kilnholt kiln and has been struck eleven months and gave a room the two sentences in Ch — a sentence he has been right about for eleven months, and he said it here in a drying floor to about two hundred people and he said it once and he did not say it twice.

Then he said the third thing, and it was not a plea, and there is a reason it was not a plea, which is that he had spent a year and a bit being a plea in about nine different rooms and had learned exactly what a plea sounds like from the inside.

"I am not going to stand here and tell anybody what I want," he said. "You have all got that. What I am going to say is the thing nobody has said, and it is not a favour and it is not a threat and it is not clever. **Three of those men at the end of your column are me.** I have been struck eleven months and I have not lost a day, because a hod does not ask a man's standing, and if the cutting stops in the fourth week I will lose every day I have got, and then I will be a man who is struck and a man who is out, and there is nothing in this county that can tell the difference between those two things or anybody else either. So when the ninety cords get cut, in whichever week they get cut, there is one man in this room who will not be in the way of it, and I would like that entered in the book by somebody who is not me, and I would like it entered now, while I am able to ask."

The clerk of the roll entered it. It is the second line on that page, and it is in a keeper's hand, and it is the first entry in nineteen years in that office against a man by name that is not a removal.
