# Chapter 0066

There were about two hundred of them in the county and nine of them in the shed, and the nine were at the firing floor of the Kilnholt kiln at the change of shift on the eleventh day, which was the thirty-second day of the rolling, and the reason they were there was that a kiln-worker is a kiln-worker, and a person who cannot be counted can still push a hod, and a hod does not ask a man's standing.

The man with the ledger was there too, with the satchel and the black thumb, doing the day's count off the wall, because the tiles went onto the roll off the wall of a kiln and a tile that has not been counted off the wall is a tile that does not exist yet. He did not speak to any of the nine, and the nine did not speak to him, and that was not an accident of manners: a man who counts and a man who cannot be counted have nothing to say to each other about the number.

Aren Kest came in at the back with a sett-cutter's bag he had been carrying about the county for eleven days for no reason anybody had given him, and a man at the loading end looked at the bag and then at his hands, and said, "You're the one off the road."

"I'm the one off the road."

"Then you'll not be wanting to stand there," said the man at the loading end, pleasantly, and moved about eleven inches along the wall.

Which is the thing about this county that a person from a fen has to get used to fastest, and it is worth putting in a book if anybody ever asks. In a county where the roll has a column headed *Keeper* on it, a man reads a stranger's hands before his face. Aren Kest had been in a hall in a fen county in the fourth hour and a room in a fen county in the ninth and had been the man everyone turned to, and he walked into a kiln shed in a clay county and a man moved him eleven inches along a wall for holding a bag.

He stayed where he was.

---

"The one thing nobody in this county has ever asked us," said the man who was loading, "and I want it said that nobody has asked us, because I have been waiting eleven months to say it to somebody and you are the somebody, and you are a stranger, and a stranger is cheap."

"Go on."

"Eleven months," said the man at the loading end. "Nobody has asked me for anything. Not the magistrate, not the rollkeeper, not the man with the book. I am not owed anything. I owe nothing. My fire is not in anybody's tally and I am not on a list of anybody's debts and there is no sum against my name in this county, and I have a house, and my oldest is at a hearth that is not mine, and I have not been asked about that either, and I have not asked, because if I ask I am a man asking to be put back, and if I am put back then I am a man who was off, and the whole of what I would be saying is *look at me and see that I was off*."

"That is a hard thing to say."

"It is a very hard thing to say," said the man, "and I have practised it in my own head for eleven months and I have said four words of it out loud to a woman in a bed and she told me to go to hell, and I want it understood that the woman in the bed was right."

Nobody in the shed disagreed with him, which is what a thing that is true sounds like in a room with nine men in it.

"What do you want?" said Aren Kest.

"Nothing," said the man at the loading end. "That is what I have come to say and I have said it and I would like the stranger to write it down. **We want to be asked.** Not money. There is not a man in this room who wants money and there is a man in this room who has not had wages counted right in a fortnight. We want to be asked. That is all. Two hundred of us and eleven months, and every single thing that has happened to every single one of us has happened because nobody asked us anything, and the day somebody asks is the day it stops, and it will not stop because it is right. It will stop because it is written down."

---

Ostry Vaile was the third from the left with his hands black to the wrist, and he had said nothing for about forty minutes, and he had not said nothing in the way a man says nothing when he is waiting to speak. He had said nothing in the way a man says nothing when he has said the thing already and heard it come back to him out of another mouth with different words on it.

"You have heard it," he said, to Aren Kest, and then: "I said it in a shed in the autumn, in front of four of them, in almost those words, and I have been right ever since, and I would like to be left alone and uncopied, and that is my whole position and I am not going to be argued out of it by a man with a bag."

"No," said Aren Kest. "I wasn't going to try."

"Good."

"But I am going to say the other half of it, because you have said yours and it is the best sentence anybody has said in this county in eleven months, and a man who says the best sentence in a county gets to have the other half said back to him by somebody who has been in more rooms than he has."

"Then say it."

"Two hundred names in a book and a tile in every house is not nothing," Aren Kest said, "and I have spent eleven years on the other side of a road in a county where a whole city of people was written off, and I have got a very clear idea of what it is like to be the only man in a room who can be found, and being findable is worth more than you would think, and I am not going to pretend to you that a man who is left alone and uncopied is a man who is free, because he is not, he is a man nobody can check, and a man nobody can check is a man somebody can do anything to."

He had expected that to land. It did not land, and the reason it did not land was the black thumb and eleven months.

"Four times," said Ostry Vaile.

"Four times?"

"Four times in eleven months," said Vaile, "and I will tell you what for, and then you will know what you are talking about. Once to tell me the law had changed and I was struck from the count. Once to tell me my tile was out of true and could not be re-pressed by me. Once to tell me my mother was not on the roll, at a hearth, in a kitchen, in front of her. And once, in the winter, to ask me whether I would say that I had not seen a thing." He turned his head. "That is what being findable is. Four times somebody came and found me and every time they had a reason and it was never a reason about me. So I will say the sentence I said in the shed and I will say it to you and then I am going to go back to the fire and you can put it in whatever book you like, and I would like you to put it in a book, and I have said that out loud and I know what it sounds like coming from a man who has just asked for nothing for a page."

"Go on."

"A person who is struck has to be able to answer for himself," said Ostry Vaile. "And if he answers he goes back on it. And if he cannot answer then he was never on it, and we have been keeping a list of things."

---

The fire ticked. Nobody said anything for about nine seconds, which is long enough to be deliberate and short enough to have happened on its own.

Aren Kest stood in a firing shed in a clay county and heard the answer to the volume's question come out of the mouth of a kiln-worker of fifty-eight who had been struck eleven months and had been right for all eleven of them.

And he knew, standing there, that it was the answer. Not a good answer, not a finished answer, but the answer, in the plainest words it had been said in anywhere in nineteen years, and it was a rule, and a rule is a thing a county can do on a Tuesday. He knew it the way he knew a load path — not by thinking, by looking, in about a second and a half, before he had any words for it.

And he knew, in the same second and a half, that it was the most dangerous sentence anybody had said to him in five months, and he did not tell Vaile that, and he has not told anybody since, and the reason he has not told anybody is that he could not put the second half into words in this shed without putting it in a form, and once it was in a form in his own head it would get into his own hands, and he had watched his own hands write a list down in front of witnesses once already in another country.

---

"Show me the form," he said.

Nobody at the fire moved, and the man at the loading end said, "You are asking a struck man for his paper."

"I am asking whether there is a form."

"There is a form and it is not his and you do not get it and neither do I, and I have been struck eleven months and the form went where the form goes, and I could not tell you where, and that is not because the county is hiding something. That is because a form is in a room in the assizes and a room in the assizes is a room and I have not been in that room since the autumn." He looked at the man with the ledger, who had stopped writing. "You tell him. You have got the book in your hand and you are in this county every day of your life and you can tell him what the form says, and you will say it, and I will stand here while you say it, and I will not say one word while you say it."

The man with the ledger came four steps over with the satchel and the black thumb and stood about six feet off, which is the distance men in that county stand off each other when they are about to say a thing that has to be got right.

"It is a long sheet," he said. "Four columns and a head. The first is the device. The second is the name. The third is the hearth. The fourth is the trade. And the back has the hand on it, and the hand is the person's own and that is the only part of the whole of this county's records that is anybody's at all." He put the satchel down on the bench. "And on the front, at the foot, under all of it, there is a fifth space, and it is headed *struck*, and there is a rule over it, and the rule is the same rule as the four above, and a name goes in it and a date goes in it, and a hand that witnessed the striking goes in it, and that is the whole of what is done to a person in this county and it takes up a space about the size of my two fingers."

"And is there anything else on the sheet?"

"There is nothing else on the sheet."

"What is in the next space along?"

The man with the ledger did not answer for about four seconds, and he is a man who has counted fires for nineteen years and has never in his life been slow.

"There is no next space along," he said. "The sheet is four columns and a foot. That is all. The sheet has never had a next space along and I have entered eleven hundred of them in my life and there has never been a next space along, and the sheet is the same width it was the day the roll was begun."

"Then if I were a clerk in that room," Aren Kest said, "and a man came in and answered for himself — if he stood there and said *I am on this list and I am here* — and if the two other people he has to be witnessed by were in the room and gave their names, and if his tile was on the shelf and it agreed with the entry — what would go in the sheet?"

"Nothing," said the man with the ledger. "It would go nowhere, because there is nowhere for it to go, and I have never in nineteen years had a man come in and answer for himself, and I have never once been asked to enter that, because there is no way of entering it. I could put his name back in the *witnessed* column, and the *struck* column would still have his name and a date and a hand in it, and the roll would say two opposite things about one man, and a roll that says two opposite things about one man is not a roll, it is a fight, and in nineteen years this office has not had a fight in it and I am not the man who puts the first one there."

"Somebody has ruled a column," Aren Kest said. "Somebody in nineteen years has had a sheet cut with a space at the foot of it for a name and a date and a hand, and somebody has decided where that space is, and the space after it is not there. A column is a thing a person rules before they know what is going in it. Somebody ruled five and stopped."

"They ruled five," said the man with the ledger, "and there was nothing for a sixth, and I have been in this county nineteen years and I have never met the man who ruled them, and I have never had a reason to."

---

"You told me in the kiln on the first day that your mother has been fired twice," Aren Kest said, to Vaile, "and the second time was in the winter, and the second time there was no fire at all."

"There was no fire at all," said Vaile. "There is a board on the shelf in my sister's kitchen with a tile on it, and the tile is soft, and it has been soft since the winter, and it is the same tile a child carries, and it is the same board, and the same wet cloth, and the same press. And when I asked why it was soft my sister said because she is struck, and I said what does that mean, and she said it means it is not fired, and I said who does not fire it, and she said nobody, and I said that cannot be right, and she said it is not fired, and she said it the way you would say the tide is out."

"Pressed twice."

"Pressed twice," said Vaile. "The first time they fired her. The second time she came off the count and there is a soft one on the shelf, and I do not know who pressed it, and my sister's husband pressed it, and he did it in the morning before he went to work, and he did it with his thumb and a wet cloth, and it took him about a minute, and nobody asked her, and I did not ask her, and he did not do it for me. **He did it because it had gone out of true.** It goes out of true in a house in about three weeks, it is lying on a shelf in a kitchen in a wet board, and he came in one morning and it had gone and he fixed it, and there is nothing wrong with a man for fixing a board on a shelf. And after he had fixed it my sister had nothing that was hers, and it took me a fortnight to understand that, and I have not been able to say it in a sentence since."

The fire ticked.

"And I want it noticed," said Vaile, "that I am not the clever one here. I have been in a room where a man from the north said four careful things and every one of them was about what could go wrong with a mark in clay, and he was right, and I have got the two sentences, and I have had them since the autumn."

---

He got out the notebook that night and wrote the two of the four hundred and fifty-six out for the first time in eleven days, because it was the only number in the county that was doing any work and it had not been touched since the Friday.

*2,906 tiles on the roll. 2,450 people. 456 over.*

*It is a floor and it is the floor of a surplus and not an estimate, and I have been eleven days in this county and I have now worked out where the rest of it is and I am not going to write the figure because I cannot get it and anybody who says they can get it is doing arithmetic on a county instead of counting it.*

*The presses. That is where it is. A soft tile on a shelf in a house is not on a roll and is not on a wall in a shed and is not in a ledger and is not counted, and every house in this county has had a fire in it and not one of those fires is a kiln. I have been in about nine houses in eleven days and there were soft tiles on shelves in six of them, and in three of the six the tile had gone out of true and had been re-pressed, and in one of the three I was told about it by a man who said it the way you would say a thing you have been putting right for four years and had never once thought of as a thing anybody could be harmed by.*

*Nobody in this county has ever counted the presses. The county counts fires. There is a fire in every hearth of the nine hundred and forty-one and there is a fire in a stove in every house of the two thousand four hundred and fifty people and about a hundred and forty of the two thousand nine hundred and six tiles are the wrong ones, and a hundred and forty is not a figure anybody has, and I am not going to give it one, and I am aware that I have just said a hundred and forty about a county of nine hundred and forty-one and I am aware of what I have done.*

He stopped there, and read the two lines under it, and then wrote two more, and the two more were the ones that mattered and he took about four minutes over them.

*A cell that is ruled and has never been filled in is not the same thing as a cell that is blank because there was nothing to put in it, and there is no way to tell the two from the cell, and both look exactly the same, and a man who is given a cell has to be told which one he is looking at by somebody, and in this county the somebody is the sheet, and the sheet does not say.*

*A column is ruled before anybody knows what is going in it. Somebody in this county ruled a fifth space for a name and a date and a hand and then stopped, and there is no sixth, and the fifth is the reason two hundred people cannot get back on a roll of persons in a county of nine hundred and forty-one hearths, and it is not because anybody forbade it. It is because there is a blank at the bottom of a form and a man did not rule one line further, and ninety-four years ago, in a room in the assizes, in an afternoon, with a knife.*

*And nobody has noticed the blank in ninety-four years, because a blank on a form is not a thing a clerk looks at, and I have been a man who looks at things for nineteen years and I have never once in my life had reason to look at the bottom of a form.*

*That is all of it. I have been in this county eleven days and the whole of it is the bottom of a form, and I have got no idea what to do about that, and I have got four hundred miles and a road that will not open to take it away in.*
