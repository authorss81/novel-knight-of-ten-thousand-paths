# Chapter 0057

The seventh of Embermonth was a Tuesday and it was not a market day, and a yard on a non-market day does the only thing it has: it puts its own gear back in order. There were two men on the grindstone and a third at the bench-board with a drawknife, and the churn was still propping the top hinge off the workshop door because nobody had been to the tanner's for a hinge, and the oak shavings were four inches deep in the north corner and would be deep until a man with a barrow came for them.

There is a grindstone against the north wall and a shave horse beside it, because a yard is four men and not one trade, and both of those stones are sound and one of them is loud.

Aren Kest had been in the yard since the sixth hour because there is no better place in Merefen to be at the sixth hour, and he had been in it long enough to hear the grindstone change its note, which it does about every two hundred strokes, and long enough for the man at the drawknife to stop being surprised that he was there.

The man of sixty-one was on a bench-board with his hat on his knee.

He had a receipt in his hand and a sheet of paper on the board beside him, and he was not reading either of them, and he had been in that position for about an hour and a quarter, and when Tovan Kest came out with a lamp in his hand at the seventh hour the man said, without looking up:

"I want this witnessed, and I will not sign it in a room with one person in it, and there is a room in this town with a press in it and one keeper in it, and I have been in this county three days and I am not going back up that stair to ask a woman of twenty-nine to stand up in front of a fire and watch a registrar of thirty-four years write a letter about a box. So I am sitting on your bench-board. I will pay for the bench-board."

"Two shillings," said Tovan Kest, "for the board and nothing for the room, and if you are going to be here till the tide I will put a candle at you and that is a candle and not a fire and you will be welcome to the fire in the workshop and not in this."

"Thank you," said the man. "Halven. I am a registrar of the provincial registry and I have been in this county three days and I am going to be in it until somebody comes from two hundred miles off with a pen, and that is the whole of my business here and I am going to put it on the paper now while I have got a bench-board and four men."

"A registrar," said the man at the drawknife, and stopped, because a drawknife is not stopped by an idea.

Aren Kest had come down to the yard at the sixth hour and had heard the whole of it, because the grindstone is eleven feet from the bench-board and a man who is sixty-one and frightened of his own handwriting does not lower his voice.

---

The receipt was a single sheet with a printed head on it, and the head was the office's, and the body was in Halven's own hand, and at the foot there were two lines and a space for a signature against each.

*Received of H. Halven, registrar, one locked box, sealed. Fourth of Embermonth, YR 312.*

*Signature of the receiving officer. Date.*

The upper line had a signature on it and a date. The lower line was empty.

"There," said Halven. "That is the entire problem, and it has taken two days and it is not going to be solved, and I have decided today that I am going to write to them about it, and a letter I write about a box is a document, and a document I write about a box says that I gave a box away and that I have not had the receipt finished, and I would like somebody in this yard to see me write it and then to write their names at the foot of it as men who saw it, because in about four years there is going to be a clerk in a provincial office who wants to know where that book went and there is going to be nothing in the world that says the book left this town."

"You want four witnesses to a receipt for a box," said Tovan Kest.

"I want a document," said Halven, "and I have wanted one for eleven years and this morning I found out that the thing I have wanted for eleven years is four men in a yard on a Tuesday, and that is a trade and not a law, and I am sixty-one."

They did it at about half past seven, in the gateway, standing, the way it is done, and Halven wrote the letter and four men read it and Halven read it back and then Tovan Kest took the pencil and wrote his name at the foot in a carpenter's hand, and the man at the drawknife did not write his, and said why.

"I have not got a hand for a name," he said. "You will enter that a man at the drawknife read it and declined, and I will say the same tomorrow if you ask me again."

"Entered," said Halven, and entered it, with the hour, and then put the sheet in his coat and did not look at it again.

---

Rowan Petch came down the road at about the eighth hour and did not get as far as the gateway before Aren Kest saw the shape of him, because a man who has walked two hundred miles in three weeks does not come round a corner like a man who has come to a bench of nine with a form in a bag.

He was twenty-six. He had a leather tube and no coat over his arm, and he had the particular look of a man carrying something that is not his to carry, and he came into the gateway, having been three days on the road and paid his own way for all three of them, and he said, to Aren Kest and not to the yard:

"I have four days and I have to write a sentence and I cannot write a sentence about this, and I have been three days on the road to ask a bridgewright whether I can write a different one, and I would like to say before I say anything else that this is the second time in three years I have walked into a room where I work and said a thing my office did not ask me for, and the first time cost me an afternoon that I did not get paid for and a career that I still have."

"Which sentence," said Aren Kest.

Petch took a sheet out of the tube and put it on the bench-board and turned it round.

It was a form of a series and it was headed with a number, and it wanted one thing, and the thing it wanted was on the front in a space that had a ruled line in it and a printed head over the line.

*State the purpose for which the county file in the matter of the crossing of Alder Vale is held in this court.*

"That," said Petch. "The Office of Provincial Registration has written to the filing room and asked the provincial court to state, in a return, what the file is for. It is dated the fourth. It is returnable on the eleventh. I have four days. And I have been sitting in a filing room for two days looking at a file I have had a particular written about and trying to find out what to put under that line, and I cannot put anything under it, and the reason I cannot is that every answer I can think of is a sentence and every sentence is a claim, and I have never in six years had to put a claim in a returnable paper and I do not know how to do it and I have four days and two hundred miles of road."

"And your office has not told you what to put under it?"

"My office has told me what to put under it," said Petch. "That is the difficulty. My office has told me to put under it what the file is for, and I have looked, and the file says what it is for on the face of the writ, in the recitals, in the fourth line, and the fourth line is the answer, and I am not going to put the answer to their question in a returnable paper because the answer to their question is the one sentence in this whole business that a person could be made out of."

---

Nobody in the gateway said anything for about as long as it takes to count nine, and the grindstone went on, and the note changed and came back.

"Two questions," said Aren Kest. "You have got a wrong one and you have got a right one, and they are not the same question and they do not both fit under the line they have printed."

"Go on."

"The question on that sheet is what the file is for. That is a purpose. A purpose is not a fact about a box. A purpose is a reason somebody had, and a reason belongs to whoever had it, and if four men in a room want four reasons you get four sentences and every one of them is true." He put his hand flat on the form, on the printed head, the way a man puts his hand on a thing he is about to disagree with. "The question you can answer is what is in the box, and when it came in, and whose hand put it there, and what is on the top of it. Those are four facts. Anybody in this kingdom with the box in front of them can look at all four of them and see whether you have got them right. And a return that answers that answers nothing, and a return that answers nothing is a document, because the only thing anybody can do with a document that says *here is the box and here is when it came* is open the box."

"That is the second question," said Petch slowly. "Go on. That is not why I am here."

"That is why you are in a gateway in a fen county instead of a filing room," said Aren Kest, "and the rest of it is this. A returnable paper has every space on it filled. If the line is not filled, it is a blank, and a blank in a return gets ruled through and dated, and a ruled line with a date against it is read by whoever opens the file next as an answer of *nothing to state*. And nothing to state is an answer that closes a question, and a question that has been closed by a return is a question nobody has to ask again. Your office has asked you the question that can be closed. You cannot tell the difference between the office being wrong and the office being right, because from where you are standing the two produce the same sheet of paper, and that is the whole reason you cannot write it, and it is not a moral difficulty, it is a forms difficulty, and forms are the only thing in this business that has ever beaten anybody."

The man at the drawknife, who had not stopped drawing, said, "Ask him about the press."

---

"He is a registrar," said Petch, to the yard at large. "He has been one for thirty-four years and he is sitting on a bench-board two feet from me, and I have known him three days, and I am going to ask him, because the only man in this county who reads a press register for a living is sitting right there with his hat on his knee, and I would like it entered that I asked a question of a man in a gateway instead of in a room, because that is what my office is for and I am not in my office."

"Go on," said Halven.

"The register of the fourth press," said Petch. "There is a book in it that is entered and is not on the shelf. It was entered in the three hundred and second year and entered again as put away in the three hundred and ninth, and it came off the shelf on the nineteenth of Mudmonth, YR 301, with no name against it and no office and no authority. If my office wants to know what a file is for, that book is the nearest thing in this kingdom to an answer to a different question, and I cannot see it, and I have been in a press in your trade for—" he stopped, and worked it out, and it is a thing about a man that he stopped. "Nineteen years I have been in a press. Eleven of them in that building. And I have never once been asked what a press register is for, and I could tell you in four seconds, and the fact that nobody has ever asked me is the answer to your question and I would like it written down that I said so."

"Nobody is writing it down," said Tovan Kest. "You want it in a book and you are in a gateway."

"Then say it again at the end of the day and somebody will be still here," said Petch, and turned back to Halven.

---

Halven had been waiting for the question for about four minutes, which is a long time, and he had been enjoying it, which is a worse thing and is in the entry at the back of this book.

"A press register is not a record of a shelf," he said. "It is a record of what a man said he did with a shelf. Every line in it is in the hand of the man who did the thing, and the shelf is not in it, and the books are not in it, and the men who read the books are not in it. It is the most honest book in this kingdom and it is honest about the wrong thing, and I have been in that trade for thirty-four years and it took me thirty-four years to be able to say that without bitterness and I still have some."

"You have the entries," said Aren Kest. "Not the book. The entries. From your own hand, in your own memory."

"I have two of them in my head and I have had them in my head since the second of the year before last," said Halven. "The one I entered myself and the one I entered about myself, and they are the same line twice and I have never been able to make them into one, and I have never been able to make them into two either, and I can put them on the back of a sheet in a minute and I am going to, and you may have it, and it is worth nothing whatever."

He wrote them out on the back of the receipt, in a small clerk's hand that had got worse over nine years, and gave the sheet to Petch, and Petch read it twice and put it inside his coat and did not say thank you, and said instead:

"That is a man's memory."

"It is," said Halven. "And a man's memory is not a register, and a register does not leave the building it is in, and I have not been allowed in my own press in nine years because I am the man who put a false line in it, and they are entirely correct, and if you put my memory in a return you will have put a man's memory in a returnable paper in a provincial court, and in about four years somebody will read it and see a hand he recognises and it will be mine, and that is the last thing I want out of a book before I die."

---

"It is a bell."

Aren Kest said it before he knew he was going to, which is a thing that has happened to him four times in eleven years and every one of the four was in a room with a clerk in it.

Nobody moved.

"The file," he said. "You want to know what the county file is for, and you have been looking at the recitals on the fourth line, and the fourth line says what the file is in the matter of. It is a bell. The whole of this county's file is about a bell, and the file exists because a bell was carried off a bridge and a county was told it was lawful, and every sheet in that box is about a bell-shaped object, and I have been in a room with a Crown's man twice and the phrase they use on the printed things is *an object held in use among a company of persons*, which is a phrase four generations of clerks wrote down to avoid putting a word in a file, and they have been putting the other phrase in the file ever since, and the file is full of it, and the word itself is in the recitals of the fourth line, in the hand of a man who was not a coward, because he had a marshal's seal on the sheet in front of him and the word in a file is a thing you get made out of."

"There it is," said Petch, very quietly, and did not move, and did not write.

"Write it down," said Aren Kest.

"No," said Petch.

"That was the fourth time in eleven years and a clerk of a provincial court heard it, and you are not going to write it, and I would like it entered in a book somewhere that I said it and that you did not."

"I am not going to write it anywhere," said Petch, "and I am not going to have it entered, and I am going to tell you exactly why, because I have thought about it for two days and you are the first man I have been able to say it to. If I keep it out of a book for you, I am doing your work and not mine, and in about four years you will have been right and I will have been a man who knew, and there is no office in this kingdom that a man can be told about for keeping a word out of a file. That is the trade of a man who sends people. You are the man who sends people, and you are telling me a thing in a gateway, and I have noticed that you are the only man I have met who says the word and then stands in the room and lets it be in the room."

"Yes," said Aren Kest. "That is exactly what I am."

"So I am going to do the other thing, and I am going to do it now, out loud, so that four men in this yard hear me say it and I cannot be talked out of it tomorrow morning when I am tired and in a filing room with a line on a form." Petch put both hands flat on the bench-board. "I am going to write under that line the four facts. What is in the box. When it came in. Whose hand put it there. What is on the top of it. I am going to leave the purpose out, and I am going to leave the purpose out in terms, and I am going to end the return with a line saying that the court is asked to observe that it has been asked a question of purpose and has answered a question of contents, and that if the office requires a purpose the office must supply its own, in its own hand, under its own seal."

"That return is a stone with no mortar in it," said Tovan Kest.

"That return is the only thing I know how to write," said Petch, "and I have been a clerk for six years and I have never once filed a document that answered nothing, and I am going to file one, and I worked it out in a gateway in Merefen and not in an office two hundred miles off, and I would like that in the yard's book if the yard's book will take it, because it is the only piece of paper in this business that is going to have a town on it."

The yard's book takes a thing set and not a thing believed, and a thing set is a thing that happened, and a man in a filing room deciding what he is going to write is a thing that happened.

Tovan Kest wrote it down, in a carpenter's hand, with the date and the hour, and he wrote the four facts too, because Petch read them out loud first, and then he read back what he had written, and Petch said it was correct, and Halven said, from the bench-board, that it was the first time in nine years he had heard four facts about a box put in a book by a man who had not looked in the box, and that the man was the county's and not the office's, and that he would like that noted too.

---

The post went at the first hour on the eighth and Petch was on it, and he had a sheet of nothing in his coat and a sheet of somebody else's memory in the other pocket, and the provincial post comes in on the ninth at the fourth hour, so he has the ninth and the tenth in a filing room and three nights of an inn in front of him that he has not booked and will pay for himself, and nobody in Merefen knew he was doing any of it, which was the second time and which he entered in the yard's book himself, in his own hand, with the hour, because he had learned in a gateway that a thing set is a thing set whichever side of it you are standing on.

Aren Kest wrote the whole of it down that evening in the room over the cooper, in the flat hand, and the notebook is where the drift is kept, in figures, for the first time with the figure in print on the other side of it.

*Seventh of Embermonth, YR 312. A clerk of the provincial court walked three days to ask me a question I could not answer, and got an answer that was worse, and that is the trade and I am in it.*

*And here is the drift, in figures, for the first time with the figure in print on the other side of it. My own notebook, the second of Fallowmonth: I was out six days in this world's calendar from the fourth hour of the twenty-sixth of Longlight to the sixth hour of the second, and inside it was six months and twenty days. The printed instruction on the reverse of a form in an office's own hand, which I have now had in front of me twice, says a road held, three weeks. Three weeks is twenty-one days. Six days of absence against a warning of twenty-one days is not an error at all. It is minus fifteen days. The warning was longer than the absence.*

*And I want it written down in this order, because I have been carrying it the other way round for four months and it has made me nervous for no reason: the warning did not fail. The warning was generous and the road came back early, and the only thing that was ever going to be measured by that figure is me. Nobody in this county can check any of it. The one page that could check it is on the reverse of a form in a provincial office and it is a standard of measurement and there is nothing under it, and I have written the word that supplies it once, in this book, and nobody in this kingdom has ever heard the word.*

*And the fourth warmth was the twenty-fourth of Fallowmonth, which is the day the bell went back on the bridge and the channel opened and the road went both ways in one day, and it is not the nineteenth of Mudmonth and it is not the sixteenth of Greenmonth and it is not the second of Fallowmonth, and it is the only one of the four that I did not cause, and I have had four weeks to think about that and the best I have is that on that day four other people in this county held a line and a bell went down on its own two hands.*

*And the Sedge Reach scour has not been read since the sixth of Mudmonth and the bench's mark is due on the ninth, which is a market day, and the man who has read that scour twice is going to be in a county four hundred miles off on the ninth, and there is nobody else, and I have known that since Saturday and I have said it to nobody, and this is the fifth thing in this book I have not said to anybody and the other four were on purpose and this one is not.*

*And three shillings a week is in the county's hands, three Fridays of it, which is nine shillings, because it is paid on a Friday in arrears and there have been three Fridays since the sixteenth of Fallowmonth and I have not gone for any of them and cannot go, and a man who is owed three Fridays is a man who has been somewhere. I have written all six of these down and I am going to close the book and go down to the yard and read them over again, because a man who writes down six things on the same night and does not read them over has written them for himself and not for anybody.*
