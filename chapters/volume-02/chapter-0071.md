# Chapter 0071

The ninth of Wolfmonth was a Saturday and a market day, and the hour was the fourth, and there were four men in the yard and a fifth man not in it, and a sum on the bench-board that had three figures in it and wanted a fourth.

Tovan Kest did the sum standing up, in the gateway, with the board on the trestle and the yard going on behind him, because a man who sits down to count money is a man who finds some.

"Right," he said. "On Tuesday this yard had eleven days in it. That is in the book on the ninth page with the hour on it, which was the second hour, and I did that sum before I went up to the filing room and I wrote it without my brother's name on it and I have not thought about it since. It is eleven."

He put the stub of pencil on the board.

"On Thursday the man came back. The wall off the Fallow Elms road. He stood about where you are standing and he said he could not take the men off, because the timber is not coming till the eighteenth and a man who takes his men off a wall in Frostmonth does not get them back for the spring, and he wanted four more days on it. Four days. I said yes and I wrote it in on the Thursday and it is fifteen, and the hour on that is the fifth, and I will read you both lines because there is no point in a book you cannot hear."

Nobody in the gateway said anything, which is how a yard works.

"And then Pei asked to be put off two days instead of three."

That got a noise out of the man at the drawknife, and Tovan waited for it, because a yard's book is only worth having if the yard can hear it.

"He is not here," he said, "because he is on the water, and he asked me on Friday morning at the grindstone and I said yes, and it is a day and it is in the book, and it took us to fourteen."

He wrote the figure on the board in front of the other three, large, in a carpenter's hand that is not a clerk's hand.

"Fourteen. Which is a fortnight."

Four men looked at it.

"There," said Tovan Kest. "A fortnight, and it is the ninth of Wolfmonth, and four days ago it was eleven, and if you had asked any one of you on Tuesday what was in this yard you would have said eleven and you would have been right, and nobody in this county has heard a number about this yard in a year. I want it understood that the four days are on this board with two hours against them, and that they came from a man's mouth on a Thursday and not out of the air. **A yard that gains four days without anybody being able to say where they came from is a yard that has stopped counting**, and a yard that has stopped counting is a yard that is being run on somebody's memory, and I have been on the other side of that in this county and I did not enjoy it."

He put the pencil down.

"Eleven on Tuesday. Fifteen on Thursday. Fourteen on Friday. The book has all three and I have read all three out and that is the whole of it."

---

The rest of the yard's morning was the rest of a yard's morning, and it is worth saying what that is, because the whole of this volume happens in a place where the ordinary work is not a metaphor for anything and is not going to be.

There is a cart in the mud by the mill that has been ignored since Fallowmonth. There is a set of setts on trestles under an oiled cloth that the man at the drawknife has been re-cutting since the sixth of Wolfmonth, at four a day, because setts cut in a wet month and dried in a frost come out of the kiln proud and a proud sett is a fault and a fault in a road is a man's heel. There is a boy of thirteen who is not an apprentice and has not been told he is one, who sharpens, and who has got so good at it that the man at the drawknife has stopped correcting him and now checks about one in nine, and has never said so to him.

And there is a stack of ash-wood in the corner by the gate, eleven cords of it, cut and split and stacked the way a man stacks wood who is going to burn it and not sell it, because it came in on Thursday in three carts and it was the price of a wall.

Nobody in the yard had said anything about the wood except the man who carted it, who had said it was ash and that the man had said it was ash and that he believed him.

Tovan Kest had said nothing about it either, because it is the price of a wall and a price is not a subject.

The man at the drawknife said it, at about half past four, to nobody in particular, with a sett in his hand and his eyes on the end of it.

"That is good ash."

It was good ash. It was the best ash in the county and everybody in the yard knew it, because ash is the wood that does not split with the frost and does not rot in a wet floor and takes a wheel's weight for forty years, and a yard that burns ash is a yard that has made a decision about its own floor.

"Four hundred miles off," said the man at the drawknife, "there is a trade that would give a good price for that."

Nobody asked him what trade.

"There is a trade," he said. "I had a cousin in it. It burns wood by the cord and it does not care what a cord weighs, and there is more of it than there is of anything. Eleven cords. That is a man's year of firing and somebody four hundred miles off would take it at a price that would let me pay four men for a month without either of them asking me for it."

He went back to the sett.

That was the whole of what was said about the wood in the yard on the ninth of Wolfmonth, and it was said by a man who did not know why it mattered and was never going to find out, and the yard did not act on it, and the price of the wall in wood is going to be in somebody else's book in a country that is not this one. Ash is the fuel of a thing that has not been built yet and is not in this county.

---

The man off the west channel came in at about half past three, which is half an hour before the bell and a full hour before a market day is a good time to come into a yard, and he came in the way a carrier comes in, which is that he comes in until somebody speaks to him.

Gwil Trewhella was thirty-nine and had a barge on the western channel and two men he could not do without and about nine days of freight in him and a bad back that he had told nobody about. He had a coat on that had been made for a man who was going somewhere, and he had been going round the market for two hours hearing the same thing in four doorways.

He got it out in the gateway, in the way of a man who has been holding a thing since the mill.

"They've got a man," he said. "That's what it's this. That's what all of it's this. There's a warden of this road and he's off in the north and nobody in this county knows when he's back, and the Old Toll has been shut at the fourth hour two days a month since Mudmonth and I have turned round with a full boat twice for it. I'm not asking where he is. I don't care where he is. I'm asking whether somebody is minding it."

"I am not the road warden."

"You're the one they ask," said Trewhella. "They ask you at the mill and they ask you at the chandler's and they ask you at the weigh-house. I asked at four doors this morning and three of them said ask Kest, and the fourth said ask Kest, and I am asking."

Tovan Kest put the pencil down.

"Sit down," he said, "because you are on your feet and I have been on mine for three hours and there is a bench-board and it is a bench-board and not a bench."

He sat down on the edge of it with his hands on his knees, the way a man sits who has decided to give a thing the whole of him for a minute.

"Now. I am going to tell you the numbers I have got, and they are all I have got, and you will notice that none of them is a date."

"The number of lines," said Tovan Kest, "in my book for the week of the fourth, is one hundred and ninety-one, and I know that because I count them on the Friday and the Friday is the day I count them, and it is a rule I made in Fallowmonth and I have never broken it. The hour I read the last form that came into this yard was the seventh. I have four men and a boy and the four men are owed, in the book, in pence, a figure I could read you out in about eleven seconds and I am not going to because it is a wage book and not a book of persons, and if you take a wage figure into a place you do not have to be telling me where you are taking it, you are telling me you have not thought about it, and I have thought about it."

"And the road?"

"The road is not a trade," said Tovan Kest, "and the yard is, and that is the whole of my argument and I have been making it since I was twenty-one years old and I have never been asked to make it in a room with a bench in it until this year. A road is two ends and a date and a person at each end who has agreed to be there, and every one of those four things is a promise, and a promise is not a trade and a yard cannot make one and cannot answer for one. What I can do is cut setts and set them in a road and read where the load goes, and I have got a fortnight and I have not got a day, and a man who asks me for a day is asking me for a thing I have not got, and I would rather say that than have you find out on the water."

"That is a hard way to say a soft thing."

"It is the only way I say it."

Gwil Trewhella sat on the end of the bench-board with his hat in his hands.

"My uncle kept the west lock," he said, "not the gate lock, the lock above the mill. Thirty-one years. And the thing about a lock is that the only thing anybody ever asks you about it is when it will be open, and there is no answer to that except a name and a day, and if you do not have a name and a day then you have got a habit, and a habit is not a thing you can put in a letter." He put his hat back on. "I have turned round twice with a full boat and I have got nine days of freight in me and I cannot spend nine days on the water in a hard month, and I have not said that to anybody today and I am saying it to you, and I would like it noticed that I said it to a carpenter and not to a bench."

"You said it to a man in a gateway."

"You said it to a man in a gateway," said Gwil Trewhella, "and there were four other men in it, and one of them works for you, and I am going to remember that."

He went out into the market.

Nobody in the yard offered him a day, and nobody in the yard promised him a name, and Toban's book did not get a line about it, because a man's freight is a thing believed and a yard is not an office. But the man at the drawknife went back to his sett, and after a while, without saying anything to anybody, he split one of the eleven cords of ash-wood into a corner by itself and stacked it a different way, higher and drier, off the floor, and Tovan Kest saw him do it and did not ask him why, and it is on the record that he did not ask.

---

The man from the provincial office came up Weir Street at about ten minutes to four, on foot, with a leather bag, and he had the look of a man who has been put on a road with an address on it and has come four hours ahead of the time he was told to come.

He was not old and he was not young. He had a good coat that had been rained on and a hand that had been rained on too. He came into the gateway and stopped, because a yard is a place where you stop, and nobody told him to and nobody had to.

"Toban's yard?"

"That is the name over the gate and I have not had it over the gate for eleven years," said Tovan Kest, "and I would ask you to use the gate and not the name."

The man took the form out of the bag and gave it over.

It was a single sheet, printed, and the printing was the same printing as the schedule that had come in Fallowmonth and the dated form that had come in on the third of Embermonth, and the shape of it was the shape of the same series, and there was a difference and the difference was the whole of the document.

The last of the R.C. series had had ruled columns with words at the top of them. This one had a ruled table, and the table wanted a county, and then it wanted the hundred, and then it wanted a figure, and then it wanted, under the figure, in a small hand of the office's own printing, four words.

*How the figure was compiled.*

There was no column on it for a name. There was no box on it for a name. There was no space anywhere on the sheet for a person, and Tovan Kest turned it over and looked at the back, and the back was blank except for the office's address and the ordinary foot of the series in a small type, and he read the small type because he reads the small type.

"You are not going to answer it," said Aren Kest.

He had come in behind the man without the man hearing him, in the way a brother comes into a gateway, and he was standing about eight feet off with grey clay still in the seam of a bag that had not been repacked since Fallowmonth.

"Get off my gateway," said Tovan Kest, without turning round. "That is not a thing you say to me and you know it is not."

"I am not saying anything. I am asking whether you are going to answer it."

"There is no date on it."

"There is no date on it," said Aren Kest.

"Then it does not have to be answered."

"Yes," said Aren Kest. "And you are going to answer it, because you have answered every one they have sent you, and because a refusal is a thing a man can be cross about and an answer goes in a book, and because that is true and I have watched you do it twice and be right about it twice and get nothing for it either time."

"I got the book."

"You got the book. You got a line in a book with a date and an hour on it, in a yard, that nobody has ever read, including you." He moved about four feet and stopped. "Tovan. It is undated. Read what that is."

"It is undated."

"It is undated and it names nobody and it wants a figure, and a form that need not be answered and cannot be refused is a form that can be put through any door in the kingdom without anybody asking the man at the door first," said Aren Kest. "I am not telling you not to answer it. I am telling you what it is, and you will do what you are going to do."

Tovan Kest looked at the sheet for a while.

"Give me the pencil," he said, to nobody, and the boy gave him the pencil, which is the thing about a yard, that a man says give me the pencil and there is a pencil.

He wrote on the back of the week's sheet, in a carpenter's hand that is not a clerk's hand, and he read it back before he turned it round, and he read it out loud, because it is a thing set and a thing set is read out.

---

*Toban's yard, Weir Street, Merefen. This yard builds crossings in this county and does not inspect them, and has never inspected one, and the reason is that a thing a man builds and a thing a man inspects cannot both be his, and I say that as a man who left the bench of that guild at twenty-one years of age and has not voted in it since.*

*The number of the lines of the said form is nine, counted twice, front and back. The hour at which I read it is the fourth. The man who brought it is described as about thirty for the county town and I have not asked his name and I am entering that I did not ask it and why.*

*And against the space for a figure: this yard has no such figure, and the figure I enter is nil, and nil is a figure, and I enter it because it is true and because it is the only figure in this county that cannot be asked about a person.*

*The yard had four men and a boy on the day. None of them has had knowledge of any declared object. I am asked for the number of persons in this county and not for the number of men in a yard, and the answer to the county is the answer I gave in the third of Embermonth and it has not changed, and the difference is that this one has a column for how the figure was compiled and there is nothing on this form to compile, and if the office would like to know how a figure is compiled then the office has a method and the office can send me the method and I will answer the method with the same answer in the same hand.*

*I enter the whole of the above as a fact and not as a figure, because a refusal is a thing a man can be cross about and an answer goes in a book.*

---

He put the pencil down and pushed the sheet across the trestle.

"That is the answer," he said. "It is the same answer as the third of Embermonth and it is the same answer as the second of Fallowmonth, and there is a person in a filing room two hundred miles off who has been making the same answer for four years out of a press, and I did not get it from her and she did not get it from me and I have never seen her face, and if the two of us got it out of the same place then there is a third thing that knows and neither of us has met it, and I would rather that than not, because a thing you can find twice is a fact and a thing you can only find once is a trick."

The man from the office looked at the sheet for a while and then put it in the bag, and he did not say anything, because he was an officer of an office and not a man who argues with a carpenter in a gateway, and he was not going to be the first one.

"Very good," he said.

"Don't put that in," said Tovan Kest. "If you write *very good* in a book I cannot get at, then in four years there will be a clerk who will read a good opinion of me in a stranger's hand and he will not know what it is about and he will use it. I have watched that happen to a man in this county who was better than me at it. Write what I wrote."

"The office does not take instructions in a gateway."

"Then take the sheet back and do what you like with it, and I have entered in my own book the hour and the number of the lines and that the sheet went back out of this yard with what I wrote on the back of it and nothing else, and if the office burns it, the burning is a fact and the burning is dated."

The man went out into the market with the bag.

---

Tovan Kest entered it. That was the last thing and it took about ninety seconds, and it is the whole of what the ninth of Wolfmonth is in a book.

*Fifth of Wolfmonth, second hour. Eleven days. Without my brother's name, and the reason is that this is a thing set.*
*Fifth of Wolfmonth, fifth hour. Fifteen days, by the man's mouth on the Thursday, re-spoken, four days, timber the eighteenth.*
*Eighth of Wolfmonth, first hour. Fourteen days, Pei Tarrow put off two and not three, one day off, a fortnight.*
*A sheet from the Office of Provincial Registration, undated, wants a figure and how the figure was compiled, nine lines, read at the fourth hour, answered on the back of the week's sheet in my own hand and read out with four men in the gateway, went back out of this yard. Nil entered against the figure.*

He shut the book and put the board away and the man at the drawknife said, "You answered an undated one."

"I answered an undated one."

"Why?"

"Because there is a difference between a form a man may ignore and a form a man may not," said Tovan Kest, "and I have got a record of every one they have sent me, dated, and there is a clerk two hundred miles off who has a better record than mine, and between the two of those books there is a thing that is going to happen, and I can tell you what it is going to be and I cannot tell you when, and it is this: **they will send another one, and the next one will be dated**, and a dated one has to be answered by somebody who admits they answered it, and a dated one that wants a figure for a county is a different animal from an undated one that wants a figure for a yard, and this yard has just told them in writing that a figure for a yard is not a thing a yard can supply, and they have written down the hour."

He put the board under his arm.

"And the other thing, and it is the only other thing, and I have been not saying it for a month and I am saying it now because there are four men in this yard and two of them have been in a market all afternoon listening to a man on the west channel: there is a road in this county that was opened on the ninth of Embermonth at the fourth hour and nothing came, and it did not fail, and it is kept for the fourth and the ninth, and I do not know when it will open and I have not asked, and I have a brother in it and I have not sent him a word because I do not know the address, and I am telling you that I said that out loud, in a yard, on a market day, at about half past four, and I have never said it out loud in this yard before, and if either of you ever tells anybody in this county that I said it I will deny it in a form."
