# Chapter 0083

The rite worked for eleven weeks and in the ninth week of it a man called Ostry Vaile was put back on the roll of persons, and in that same week he began to go back to being clay, and the two of those things happened in one room in a market town and not one person in the room knew that either of them was happening.

That is the sentence the whole of the rest of this volume is standing on, and it is worth putting down in the plainest words there are, because a reader who has been waiting eleven months for a man to die is going to find this volume does not do that, and the reason is not restraint and it is not mercy. It is that a man in the County of Ash who goes back to being clay does not stop being anything. He stops being able to be asked. That is the county's word and it is the physician's word and it is the law, and the law was not written by anybody for this purpose, and the law has no column for it, and the law has been doing it for four years without a single decision having been taken by a single person in the room.

He is fifty-eight. He has been struck eleven months. He has been right about the thing he said in a shed at the change of shift in the eleventh month of a rolling, and he said it to nine men in about nineteen seconds, and it is the answer to the question this whole volume is about, and it has never once been in a form.

Nobody in this volume visits him in his house. There is a reason for that and the reason is in the law and not in anybody's hardness: a man who softens cannot be witnessed, and the county's law requires two witnesses, and so the moment anybody in this county says out loud *that man is going* they have to do something about it, and there is nothing to do, and a person who says it out loud has taken on a thing they cannot put down. So nobody says it out loud. And a man who has not been said out loud has not been said.

---

He was put back on the ninth day of the ninth week of the rite, in a room with a stove in it, by two men whose names are in the book, and it took eleven minutes, and he pressed his own tile.

Then he went back to the Kilnholt kiln and put in a day, and it was a good day, and he was a man who was on the roll and employed and had a hearth and a wage drawn at the end of a week and two witnesses whose names were in a book, and he was the only kind of the three kinds that this county can ask a question to.

And his name was in a list, and the list was read out four times a day in a hall four hundred yards from the kiln, by a girl of nineteen, with the hearth and the trade attached.

He was read eighty-four times in that hall.

The boy with the slate has the figure on the slate and the figure is eighty-four, and it is the number of sittings from the day he was put back to the last day of the eleventh week, four sittings a day, six days a week, and the boy with the slate does not add the four villages to a hall, and the four villages did not have a person put back in them in the ninth week, so the whole of it was in Kilnholt.

Eighty-four is not a large number. A clerk in this county reads the roll every morning of her working life and has read about two thousand names a year for nineteen years and has never once thought about it, and a clerk in this county has now read the name of one man eighty-four times in ten weeks in a room, with the hearth and the trade said out loud so that the room could check her, and a clerk in this county has now done the single most-read thing in the County of Ash, four times a day, for eleven weeks, and has not once asked herself what a mark that is read does.

The man with the ledger put one figure on the slate in the ninth week of the rite, because a man with a ledger puts figures on a slate, and then he took it off again with the back of his hand about four seconds later, and Fennie Loam saw him do it.

"There are four hundred and nine boards on that shelf," he said, "and the list of restorations is read four times a day, and a name in that list is read four times a day, and that is the whole of what I have got to say and I have said it out loud in a shed with about eleven people in it and I would like somebody to stop me doing arithmetic like that in public."

Nobody stopped him. There was nothing to stop. It was a figure about marks and not about people, and a man who counts fires has no reason in the world not to count a mark.

Hesper Lound wrote that down four years ago in the third year of the softening and had it in a drawer until a stranger from the north read four pages of it out loud in a room with a table in it in the twelfth month, and the second of the four pages says: the softening travels from a mark to the person who reads it, and from a person to the marks of that person's household, and travels fastest along the marks that are read most, and the marks that are read most are the roll marks, because a clerk reads the roll every morning and does not know that she is doing anything.

He was not in that hall. He was at a kiln four hundred yards away with a hod in his hands, and he did not know, and there is no instrument in this county or in any other that would have told him.

---

Aren Kest was on the charging floor of the Kilnholt kiln on the third day of that week carrying boards, because he was useful and not a visitor and had been for eight months.

He did not see it. He wants that written down, in his own hand, at the back of the notebook, and it is the reason there is a third notebook entry in this volume and not a first one, and the reason is this. He has trained himself for nineteen years, in four countries, to see where a load goes. That is a real thing and it has saved about nine men his life and it is the only thing he is for. And a person going back to being clay has no load path. There is nothing in it to trace. There is no failure and there is no strain and there is no crack and no stone moved and no timber out of true; there is a man at a firing floor and the man is a man and everything about the man is exactly where it was.

He watched for four days and he saw four things and he did not assemble them, and the reason he did not assemble them is that the four things were not the same kind of thing, and a man who has spent his life putting four things in a row because four things in a row make a load is the last man in the world to put four ordinary things in a row.

---

Here is the first of the four things, and it is the order of the day.

There is one crooked sagger ring in the Kilnholt kiln. It has been fired about a hundred times and it is a finger out of true and everybody in that shed knows which one it is and has for nineteen years. Ostry Vaile has stacked the rings at the end of a shift for nineteen years, and he has always put the crooked one on top, so that his hand goes to the same place at the end of a day's work and does not have to look.

On the third day of the ninth week of the rite he put the crooked one at the bottom.

The man at the loading end put it right. He did not say anything about it and he did not look at Vaile while he did it, and he put it on top, and he went back to his own end of the floor.

He put it right on the fourth day. And on the fifth.

On the sixth day the crooked ring was at the bottom and the man at the loading end did not put it right, and went and stood at the other end of the floor, and did not look at the rings again for the rest of the shift.

That is the whole of the first thing, and it is worth eleven lines in a book and about four seconds in a kiln, and what the man at the loading end did on the sixth day is the single most important act of judgment performed by anybody in the County of Ash in nine years, and he made it in four seconds without deciding anything, and he has not told anybody and is not going to.

---

The second thing is the trade.

There are four jobs at a rolling in a kiln of this size and one of them is the charging, and the man who does the charging at the Kilnholt kiln is thirty-one and is on the roll and has been for eleven years and has a tile of his own with his own right hand on the back of it, and that hand agrees with the clerk's entry, and both, not either.

On the fourth day of the ninth week of the rite Ostry Vaile walked onto the charging floor.

He has not stood on a charging floor in nineteen years. Every man in that shed knows that, including the two men who are not from this county and have been in it a fortnight.

He charged a rolling. It is a job of about ninety minutes and it is not a difficult job and it is not a job that a man of nineteen years at a firing floor has any business doing, and he did it well, better than the man who had been doing it, and he stood at the end of it with his hands black to the wrist and looked at the top of the kiln as though he had been standing there his whole life.

The man who was the charger was standing on the floor at the time.

On the fourth day of that week the man who was the charger was told there was no work for him at that kiln. He came in on the first day of the next week and there was no work for him. On the third day a man at the loading end told him, in about four words, that he might go home, and the man went home, and he came in again the day after because a man goes in, and there was no work for him.

Nobody decided that. There is no minute of it in the county's book and there is no column for it. A man was on a floor and there was a man on the floor and one of the two of them was fifty-eight and had been struck eleven months and had been put back nine days before, and the other one was thirty-one and had a tile and a hearth and a wage, and a kiln is a floor and there is one of each job, and the floor does not have two.

The man of thirty-one has a name. He is on the roll in three hands, correctly, and he is a man whose kiln has shut, and there is no column in the County of Ash for a man whose kiln has shut, and a man who is not on a column cannot be put on one, and a man who cannot be put on one cannot be given a vote, and a man who has not got a vote is not a witness, and a man who is not a witness cannot be restored.

Nobody said any of that in a kiln. It is not necessary to say it in a kiln. It is a thing a county does by itself and it took about four days.

---

The third thing is the hearth.

The shift at a kiln in this county changes when the man on the loading end calls the hearths out of the roll. That is how it is done and it has been done that way for nineteen years. Hearths are called in the order of the book, by number, and about forty men turn round and go out of the gate and go in.

On the second day of the last week of the ninth week of the rite, the man at the loading end called the numbers out of the book, and he got to two hundred and fourteen, which is the hearth of Ostry Vaile, and he called it, and then a beat later he called two hundred and fourteen again.

The shift changed.

Nobody in that kiln said a word about it, and eleven men at that firing floor knew exactly what had happened, and not one of them said a word to anybody, for about eleven days, and then not for another four months.

The reason is not kindness. The reason is that a man who softens cannot be witnessed by anybody including himself, and the county's law requires two other persons to witness him to keep him on the roll, and so the moment a man at a loading end says out loud *that man is going* he has said a thing that cannot be got out of the room, and he is a man with a book and a shift and a family and no other trade, and there is nothing in this county he can do about it and no magistrate in this county who will do anything about it, and a kiln is not a room where a thing like that can be entered.

So the man at the loading end called a hearth twice and changed the shift and nobody said one word, and at the end of that shift he put two hundred and fourteen into the next day's book in the right place with the right mark against it, and the two marks on the page of a kiln-man's own day-book are his own and nobody's business, and he did not know it and has never known it.

---

The fourth thing is the name, and it happened on the last day of the ninth week and it is the only one of the four that anybody in this volume ever finds out about.

The charge on a rolling that has been standing is a matter of hours and it is done in the dark, and a man who is not on the charge goes to the top of the kiln with a hod and looks down the flue.

Ostry Vaile went up there on the fourth day of that week and stood at the top for about nine minutes with the hod in his hands and did not move. Three men noticed and one of them said a joke about it and Vaile did not hear the joke, and the man who made it was not a man who would have noticed a silence, and did not.

And then a man he had not seen in a winter came up the stairs behind him, a man off a boat off a kiln at the north village who had been laid off in the twelfth month, and said:

"You're the one that was struck."

"I am," said Vaile.

"And you're back."

"Two hours ago," said Vaile, which was not true. It was a week. It was six days and about four hours, and the number was on a slate in a hall four hundred yards away, and the man off the boat did not know the number and Vaile did not know the number either.

"What's your name?"

"Ostry Vaile."

"Ostry Vaile," said the man, and nodded, and went down the stairs.

And then he stopped on the stairs, because he is a man who has been laid off in a winter and is therefore a man who thinks about work, and he said:

"Say, which kiln are you at?"

And Ostry Vaile stood at the top of the Kilnholt kiln in the dark with a hod of fuel in his hands and about nine inches of his own life left to decide what to say, and he said:

"That is the one I am at."

---

Nobody in this volume goes into his house. There is a fire in it in Kilnholt and it is on the roll as hearth two hundred and fourteen and it is his, and he is in it most nights, and nobody calls on him because a man who is soft is not a man anybody calls on, and the reason is not that nobody wants to. It is that a call is a thing you note down.

Aren Kest found out about the name on the fourth day of that week, and he found out about it by accident, because he was on the stairs with two boards and the man off the boat told him, and it took about four seconds, and then he stood on the stairs for about a minute and a half with two boards in his arms.

Then he went and asked the man with the ledger, because that is the man in this county you ask.

"Vaile," he said. "The roll."

The man with the ledger had the book and he had the satchel and the black thumb, and he turned to two hundred and fourteen, and he did not have to look for long, and he read out what was there, in a flat voice, in the way a man reads out a line he has read out four times.

"*Ostry Vaile. Hearth two hundred and fourteen. Trade: kiln-worker, Kilnholt.*"

"Take that in hand," said Aren Kest. "Read it again."

It is the same. It is exactly right. The mark on the back of the tile on the shelf in the room behind agrees with the clerk's entry, and it was pressed by him with his own right hand in wet clay on the ninth day of the ninth week of the rite, and it stands up in a dish because a man in a leather apron taught him to press the dish and not the mark, and it can be read at four feet on a wet morning, which is more than can be said for anything else in the County of Ash.

Three books. Three hands. The rollkeeper's office at the assizes and the hand of the Ninebridge and a fair copy on a shelf four hundred miles off in a city that is not on any map. All three of them have him. **All three of them are right about him.**

"That is a good entry," said the man with the ledger. "I have been keeping that roll nineteen years and there is not a better entry in it. It is the best entry in the book and I will put it in front of anybody."

"I am not doubting it."

"No," said the man with the ledger. "You are not, and that is what I want to say to you and I have been wanting to say it to you since the ninth week. **You are not doubting it, and that is the whole of the trouble with you, and I have known that since the first day and I have not said it.**"

He shut the book.

Kilnholt is a market town of about nine hundred hearths and there are four kilns in it. The fourth column of a tile in this county is a trade, and the trade is written in the county's own word, and the county's word for a man who fires clay is *kiln-worker, Kilnholt*, and that has been the wording for nineteen years, and the column has never once had to say which of the four, because a tile is of a person and a kiln is not a person, and the roll is of persons.

There is no kiln named on the roll of this county. There are about forty dies in it and one set of sagger rings. There is a fourth column and it says the right thing.

He fires clay. He does not know where.

---

Nobody said the word for it. Nobody in that kiln, nobody in that hall, nobody in the room behind with the box under the table, and nobody in the flat over a chandler's where a man of sixty-three sat with a drawer in a table in it and put his hand on the wrist of a kiln-worker on the fourth day of that week for about a second, and said nothing, and wrote nothing, and came out of that room having done a thing that looked to everybody in the county like kindness.

It was not kindness. A person who softens cannot be witnessed by anybody including himself, and this county's law requires two other persons to witness him, and so the finding cannot be entered and the name cannot be put to it, and a physician in this county who enters a soft person in a book has entered a person that the law has already removed by operation, and there is no column and there is not going to be one.

He did the kind thing. He was not able to do the other thing. He has been in this county sixty-three years and he has had a drawer in a table for four years and he has known the whole of this since the third year, and he did not write it down, and the reason he did not write it down is the same shape as the reason the man at the loading end did not say a word, and the shape is this: a county does not remove people by deciding to. It removes them by having a rule, and the rule is four lines long, and the fifth space at the foot of a sheet takes a name and a date and a hand, and it is about the size of a clerk's two fingers, and there is no sixth space, and the sheet is the same width it was the day the roll was begun, and somebody ruled five and stopped, ninety-four years ago, in a room in the assizes, in an afternoon, with a knife.

Nobody in that room said one word, and the room was right, and the roll was right, and the tile was right, and there was nothing whatever in the County of Ash that was wrong.
