# Chapter 0088

The two hundred and six went on strike in the ninth month and it was over in six days, and the thing that ended it was not a favour and was not a wage and was written by the county in the county's own hand three years after it was pressed into a tile, and it is the fourth of five lines that a woman with a roll of tile-cutters' tools read out to a man off a road on the twenty-third day of a rolling in the first month he was in this county.

It is on the face of a tile and it is in a book at the assizes and the fourth line is twenty-two words long.

*A person who is struck may answer; and if a person who is struck answers, the person is restored.*

The county had been carrying that fourth line out of a room since the sixth month in a way a stranger had proposed in a hat, and a bench had voted on the price of it in the fourth month, and a physician had read four pages about it, and a girl of nineteen had read four hundred names out loud four times a day for eleven weeks, and none of that had put the line anywhere. The line was still a condition of a road. It was a thing the county had written down in order to be left alone by, and the county had been left alone by, and four hundred and nine people had been put back on the roll in the meantime by a method that a magistrate had permitted in a room and that a woman at a rack had taught a girl to do with a wet cloth.

Term four was not in force. It had never been in force. It was a line on a tile.

---

The strike was six days long and it cost a rolling.

Ostry Vaile's line on the page headed with a number — *I would like that entered in the book by somebody who was not me, and I would like it entered now, while I am able to ask* — turned out to be the reason there was a strike at all, because a man who has been struck eleven months and has a wage and has said out loud that a hod does not ask a man's standing is the only man in this county who could stand up in a kiln shed and say the thing that was said in a kiln shed in the ninth month, and what was said was about five sentences long and two of them were about the fourth line.

What the six days cost is on a board in the ash-driver's hand and the boy with the slate copied it including the blanks, and it is four lines and two of them are the same two lines that are in every price column this county has ever written, being *men whose day is lost* and *men out*, and the man at the cutting bench was not there to insist they were not the same and said afterwards that they were not the same and he was not going to be at the board about it.

A charged rolling is nine days of clay and cannot wait. It went back into the wedging shed on the third day and the wedging shed has a floor and a bank and about nine feet of headroom and is the scarcest room in the County of Ash, and the floor of it is the scarce part, and a room that is the floor of a strike is a room that has to be aired afterwards whatever anybody does in it.

Nobody in that strike argued about whether they were right.

That is worth saying plainly, because it is the fourth time in this county that the room has costed a thing instead of arguing about it, and it is the first time the thing being costed was a refusal. The man at the cutting bench did not say *we are right*. The cord-cutter did not say *we are right*. The ash-driver wrote down what a rolling costs when it is charged and not fired, in four lines, in his own hand, and read them out, and then said the thing that made the bench move in about nine minutes:

"You have got five lines pressed in clay and two of them are about the road and the fourth one is about a person, and they have been in this county for three years and nobody has put them anywhere, and about four hundred of us have been put back on the roll by a method that is not on the list. **You have had a rule in this county for three years and you have been working round it the way a man works round a stone on a shoulder.**

"So put it in the book. Not because we are right. Because it is the best thing anybody in this county has written down and you are the only body in it that can put it anywhere, and if you do not do it in the next six days we are going home and we are not coming back for a bench to find out why, because we have found out why about four times and the answer is always a room."

---

The bench put it in the book.

The magistrate did it himself, in the long room, with the clerk, and it is the shortest minute in the county's own book and it is two hundred and forty-one words long and it is the first entry in nineteen years in that office that is a rule and not a decision.

*I am asked to minute that the fourth line of the terms pressed in the term of this rolling and entered in a book at this assizes, being that a person who is struck may answer, and if a person who is struck answers, the person is restored, is by this bench adopted as a rule of this county, and is in force from this hour, and is not conditional upon the road, and is not conditional upon any office, and is to be read at the person, with the book in the room, and the person's own tile on the table, and two witnesses whose names are in the book.*

*It is entered that this county has had that line for three years and has not put it anywhere, and that the reason is not a reason and is that nobody in this county could put a line anywhere, there being no office in this county to put one and no writ to answer and no Crown's man and no Crown's schedule carrying this county, and that a line that cannot be put anywhere is a line that is not in force however long it is written down.*

*It is further entered, at the request of the keeper of the roll, and in her own words, and she asks that it not be improved on: that in about nine years a man will ask which clerk decided this, and that there is no clerk, and that there is going to be no clerk, and that a rule with no office behind it is a thing that can be argued with, and that is the whole of the argument for it and not one part against.*

That is the last entry in the minute and it is the rollkeeper's and she asked for it to be minuted in her own words, and the reason she asked is that in the twenty-second week of the first month a stranger came into that room and asked her to be one of three hands, and she said no in one word, and the reason she said no was that she is the anchor, and the reason she is the anchor is that the roll is one book in one room in one building and it is the only thing in this county that does not move.

And a man who is an anchor cannot be a witness, because a witness that is also the anchor is a second reading of the same hand, and he wrote that in a notebook in two counties and was right three times, and the county wrote it into a minute in a keeper's mouth without ever hearing it, and nobody in that room knew where any of it had come from.

---

The bench then did the thing that the whole of this is standing on and it did it without a stranger, and it took nine days, and it was designed by a county that had been cut off from the world in the fifth year of a calendar nobody in it uses.

The re-reading was broken up.

Eight villages and the market town, separately, at the same time, over nine days. The roll does not leave Kilnholt; the roll is the anchor and the anchor does not move. What moves is nine lists, one to each place, and the lists are not a copy of the roll and they are not a list of names on the roll. **A list is the names of the people in that place, in the order they are in that place, written out by a man of that place in that place's own hand, and a person is on it if two people who are on it say he is, and a person who is on the roll of persons is on it or he is not.**

It is slower than the rite and it is better than the rite, and those two things are the same fact, and the difference between them is the whole of what happened in this county in the ninth month.

The rite took eleven minutes a person and was run in one room at the assizes by a method a man off the road proposed in a hat in the sixth month, at the fourth hour, to about sixty people, and it was carried out at the end of a long table by a clerk of nineteen and a boy with a slate, and every one of those three things was in the room at the time.

This takes about forty minutes a person, because a person has to be found and asked and brought and put back and there is no stove in eight of the nine places, and it is done at the same time in nine places, over nine days, and it is done by the county's own people, and there is no office reading anything in it, and there is no stranger in the room.

"There is not one thing on the nine boards that says my name on it," said the man at the loading end, in the long room, on the fourth day, reading two of them off the wall. "**The rite took eleven minutes a person and I could do it. This takes forty minutes a person and I cannot do any of it, and that is not a fault in it. That is the whole of it.**

"The rite can be run by one man in one room and if that man goes out of the county then it stops, and it stops today, and he is four hundred yards off in a shed this afternoon and it is going to be going in nine places without him in it. The county's is nine times slower per man and it can be run by anybody in this county who can read and count, and about nine hundred people in this county can read and count, and there is not one of the nine hundred who has to be him, and in about nine years that is the only thing anybody will be able to say about it that matters."

And then he read the number off the slate, because the boy with the slate had it and the boy copies the blanks.

---

It worked.

Four hundred and nine people in nine days, in nine places, with the book in nine copies and the tile on the table and two witnesses out of the local list, and about eleven of the nine hundred and forty-one hearths' names did not agree between the local list and the roll, and the eleven were got by hand in a day, and there is no column in this county for a name that is on a roll and is not in a house, and there is not going to be one, and the man with the ledger went to about nine houses himself in the ninth month and did not tell anybody he was going and told the boy with the slate afterwards.

And a kiln at the north village was re-lit in the ninth month, on two thirds, and about thirteen men stayed out, and the thirteen are thirteen and the number is written down.

---

And on the ninth of the ninth month the road did not open.

Nobody in that county said the word *failed* and nobody was going to, and the reason of that is a sentence that came into the county on the first morning of the first month a stranger was in it, in a woman's mouth, on a bridge, in a market, in about four minutes, and the hand of the Ninebridge has had it since and has not improved on it and has said so to a room of about nine people in the ninth month.

The witness at the Ash end of the line was a person the county chose. The county chooses him, under the fifth of its own terms, and it chose him in the first month of the year, and he was on the roll, and he was not the hand, and the line opened on the fourth day of the first month of the year because he was there.

He was struck in the eighth month. Not put back, not restored — **struck, in the eighth month, on a list of about two hundred that had been made in the fifth month and had not been through a room yet, and the county's own reading of its own rule took nine days and his place in the order was on the eighth day of them, and the road's day was the ninth.** He had stood at that end of the line on the fourth day of the first month of the year, when the line opened, because he was there.

A man who is struck cannot be a witness. A man who is struck has no standing, cannot be witnessed, cannot witness, cannot be named at a kiln, and a witness at an end of a line is a person the county can stand there, and a person the county cannot stand there is not a witness, and a line with a hand at each end and a witness at one end and nobody at the other end is not a road that has failed. **It is a road that is not there.**

And the county had eleven weeks to replace him and the county did not replace him in time, and the reason it did not replace him in time is the reason the county built the thing that emptied the line: the county's own answer was slow and local and made by people who are not him, and there is no office in this county to keep a list of people who have been asked to stand somewhere, and the bench is a body that argues about a road and does not hold a roll of witnesses, and nobody asked anybody to hold a roll of witnesses, because the fifth of the county's own terms says the witness at Ash's end is chosen by Ash and does not say that Ash has to keep a list.

So the county asked a man in the ninth month, on about the fourth day, in a kiln shed, at the change of shift, and the man said no.

Cobb Rennick is fifty-two and he is a tile-cutter at the Ninebridge kiln and he has been on the roll nineteen years and there is a tile of his own in a box with his own right hand on the back of it, pressed by him, and it agrees with the clerk's entry, and both, not either. He was struck in the sixth month of the year and put back in the seventh week of the rite and he has been at the Ninebridge kiln since.

"I am not going to stand at the end of that line," he said, "and I am going to say why in about nine sentences, and then I am going to go back to the charging floor, and I would like it entered that I said it in nine sentences and not twenty."

Magistrate Colm Arreld said, "Entered," and the clerk wrote it down in a flat unhurried hand, and nobody improved it.

"I have got a tile," said Cobb Rennick. "It is six inches by four and it is fired and it is in a box and the back of it has my own hand in it, and I pressed that hand, and the hand agrees with the book, and I have got both and I have not got a third. That is the only thing I own in this county and I have owned it nineteen years.

"From the sixth month of this year to the ninth week of the rite I stood in a hall four times a day and had my name read out of a sheet by a girl of nineteen who was very good at it, with my hearth and my trade attached so that the room could check her. That was four hundred times, near enough. And a man off the road came in at the first hour one morning and put his hand on the sheet and took it off her, and nobody thanked him, and the county has told me for four months that that was a good thing, and I have said so too, because it was.

"And now the county wants me to stand at the end of a bridge in a hat, and not be the hand, and be answerable, and I have worked out what that job is in about four minutes and the job is this. **Somebody is going to read my name in a room and I am going to be the man who answers for it.** That is what a witness is. You stand there so that your name can be read, and the reading is the mark, and the mark is the thing that gets read, and I have been a mark for four months and I did not choose it and it has not been a good four months for me, and the woman who kept the road told me in the ninth week that being right about a thing is the whole of what happens and the county pays for it, and she was talking about herself.

"So I am not going to stand at the end of that line. **I have been the mouth of this county for four months and I have had my name read out of a sheet until I could not hear it. I want to be a mark and not a mouth.** I have got a tile with my hand on it and it does not get read, and that is not a fault in the tile, and I am fifty-two and I have a kiln and a wage and a hearth and I have no reason in the world to stand on a bridge in a hat for a thing that has already happened to me once this year."

The clerk read it back and asked whether he wanted the word *mouth* taken out.

"Leave it," said Cobb Rennick. "Leave it in. If a clerk in nine years reads that and cannot follow it, then a clerk in nine years has not stood in a hall four times a day."

---

There was no third candidate and there was no time for one and the bench did not name one, because the bench does not hold a list of people who have been asked to stand somewhere, and it has never been asked to, and nobody ever thought of asking it, and the fifth of the county's own terms says the witness at Ash's end is chosen by Ash and does not say a word about a list.

The hand of the Ninebridge was on the second span with a roll of tools at her hip and she was there at the fourth hour on the ninth of the ninth month, because a hand is a hand, and a hand turns up, and a hand has been turning up on that bridge at the fourth hour in the morning for nine years whether there is anything to do or not.

And there was no witness at that end.

"**It did not fail.**" She said it before anybody had said anything, standing in the middle of the ninth span with her hand flat on a parapet, to about eleven people and a boy and a man off the road. "Nobody in this county is going to say the word *failed* about that line and I have said so first so that it is in somebody's mouth before anybody else gets it out, and I am not being proud. **A road is a road. A road does not try.** There is a road and there is a day and there is a man who stands at the end of it, and today there was no man at the end of it, and the road was not there, and the two of those are the same size and the same shape and the same absence, and a man who has to write down that a road failed is a man who has found a way of making it somebody's fault.

"Four hundred years, somebody at Kilnholt says," said the man at the loading end.

"Four hundred years for the road and sixty for the tiling, and **do not do the arithmetic in front of the magistrate**," said the hand of the Ninebridge. "I know the number. I have swept round it for nine years and the kerb I set in my first year has a kiln's mark on the back of it and you can turn it over with a heel and read it. And a road that is four hundred years old has been shut a hundred times and has not failed once, and I have been here nine years and I have watched it shut twice, and both times somebody stood about looking as though the river had done it."

---

They went back up to the wedging shed at about the sixth hour and about twenty men stood in the doorway because the floor of that room will not take twenty pairs of boots, and the boy with the slate was in there with a cloth over his arm and a piece of chalk and he had been helping at the copying, and the rollkeeper was on the bank of that room with her back against a wall, which is a thing a keeper does in a wedging shed.

The nine lists were on the boards along the wall, drying, and they had taken a day and a half of that floor, and the floor is the scarce part of that room, and a day and a half of the wedging shed is nine days of wedging, and the wedging is the spring's clay, and the bench had entered it in the county's own book in one line: *the cost of the reading, being a day and a half of the wedging shed.*

A county of nine hundred and forty-one hearths, four hundred and nine people put back on a roll that is in three hands, two kilns of four going, about thirteen men out with the number written down, a rule in a book that three years ago was only a line in clay, and nine days, and a day and a half of the only room in the county that has a floor.

And it was a day and a half of that floor, and a floor, and nine men copying lists of the people who lived in eight villages and a market town onto boards with a piece of chalk, and keeping a piece of paper.
