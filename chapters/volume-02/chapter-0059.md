# Chapter 0059

He came up out of the road on clay steps at about the fourth hour of the ninth of Embermonth, and the first thing he did, before he had got his hands off the step, was put his right foot back where it had been, to find the edge.

There was no edge.

The road behind him was about the length of a cart and then it was the third step of a stair, and the stair was cut clay, and the clay was wet to about the height of a hand, and there was a mark on the riser at his left knee where somebody had cut a line across it and then cut it again a little to the right, which is what a person does to a step they have to find in the dark.

The weather was wrong. That was the first thing and it stayed the first thing for the whole of the day. He had come out of a fen four hundred miles behind him, out of flat grey light off standing water, and what was in front of him was light off a hill, and it came down flat and yellow and stopped at four hundred yards as though it had been laid on with a trowel, and everything in it was the colour of a kiln.

The river was not a river. He had been on a river for eleven years and he knew what a river is from its noise, and this one made almost none: a low moving sound with a hiss in it, off the right, and the water was the colour of milk where it came over a weir and the colour of a thumbnail where it did not. It came through a working ground. He could see the cut banks on the far side and the white scars where clay had been taken out of them, and the smell, which was not a smell anybody would call a smell until they had been in it for a day, of wet clay and ash-wood smoke and something else that he did not have a name for and worked out about two hours later was nine days of kiln.

The bridge was nine spans. He was standing at the foot of it in the middle of a market, and the market was on the bridge because a market is on a bridge, and the bridge had four hundred yards of tiled kerb down both sides with a fired tile set into the face of every kerb stone, edge out, the way a set is set, one to a stone, and the sun was low enough to show that every one of them carried something on it.

He had been out of the fen five minutes and he had already looked at two thousand tiles.

He had a page in his hand. It had come with him, out of the road, folded twice, and it was a leaf of a book and not a sheet, and the writing on it was in the hand of the toll-man on the north steps of a city four hundred miles off, and it had been written at the third hour that morning while both ends of a line were awake, and it said:

*Line ninety-four, and the weather is filled in by the person who is here and it is a wet morning with a wind off the ash and I do not know what to call the wind so I have called it ash. A road that is refused is worth more than a road that is open. I have written that in this book twice this year and I am going to write it a third time and I would like the man from the north to know that I have written it three times, because a thing written once is a saying and a thing written three times in a book is a practice. A county that refuses a road has something to refuse it with. We have a bell and a book and nine men on a bench, and it has taken four generations to build any one of the three, and on the day it went we rang it, and I am not a clever man and the ringing is the whole of what we have and it is not nothing.*

He read it twice on the steps, and then he put it inside his coat against the notebook, and then he did the thing he had been doing for eleven years, which was to add a line of his own to somebody else's book in his own head, and what he added was: *and this county has nothing to refuse with, and has never been asked for anything, and the county on the other side of this river has something to refuse with and has never refused anything, and I do not know yet which of the two of us is in worse order and I have been in both of them.*

---

On the coping of the second span there was a bell.

It was six inches by four and about the thickness of two fingers, and it was hung on a short iron, and there was a rope on it hanging loose in two loops, and there was no yoke, and no headstock, and no wheel, and no striker of any kind, and it was not bronze and it was not cast: it was clay, and it was fired, and it was grey.

He was two hundred yards off it and it was against that flat yellow light and it hung exactly where a bell hangs, and he read a mark on it.

A hand laid flat on a book.

He has seen that device four times and it has never once been in the same country twice: in wax on a storehouse door by the fish weir in his own town; in grey wax on a post at the back of a yard, over a doorway nobody can see from the road, never used, in no book; on the back of a letter in a locked room over a cooper that has no chain of custody on it and is never going to have one; and in the finding of a magistrate of his own county in her own hand. It is the seal of an office that cannot be produced, and it is also the thing a marshal's office puts on a door to say that a thing inside it is being held, and as far as he has ever been able to find out it is nobody's.

And there is nobody in this market town to tell. He stood on the second span of a bridge four hundred miles from any city he had ever been in and looked at a mark he could name in this or any light on a good day, and he did not say the name, and the reason he did not say the name is the reason he has said four other things in four other rooms in eleven years and been in a file about them four times: **a name in a room is a thing that goes into a book.** He has watched one bell-shaped seal go into a county file off a single stone. He has been the man who said the name in a room five times.

So he put it in the place where he keeps the things he knows he is not going to be able to use, which is about four inches behind his left eye, and he went on.

A woman was on the fourth span doing the ordinary work of a road.

She had a leather apron on over a coat and a set of tile-cutters' tools in a roll at her hip, and she was kneeling at the kerb with her hand on the third tile from the mooring ring, and she was not doing anything to it. She was doing the thing a person does to a thing they have looked at nine thousand times, which is to put a hand on it.

He stopped about four yards off her and he did not come closer, because the four yards was a thing he had learned in a ferry slip from a woman who had put a spike in a man's hand for coming closer.

"You are the one who came out of the road," she said, without getting up.

"Yes."

"Then you can hear me and I cannot hear you at this distance and neither of us has to change that." She took her hand off the tile. "I am going to ask you one question and I want the answer in the words you would use to a person and not in the words you would use to a room. Are you the one who sends people?"

"No."

"That is what the last one said too," she said.

He waited, because a person who says that has said it for a reason, and the reason is always the next thing.

"Who was the last one?"

"A man," she said. "He did not give a date, and I did not ask him for one, and you will not get one out of me either, and that is not a county's answer, that is mine, and I will tell you why I am telling you that: because you asked in the right order. You asked who and not when. Everybody asks when."

There was a second man on the bridge, four spans back, on the north side, and he had not been there when Aren Kest came up the steps, and he was not moving now. He was about sixty and he had a ledger under his arm and a satchel on a strap, and his right thumb was black to the second joint, and he had been black to the second joint for a long time, because the black goes into the creases and the creases do not come back in a fortnight.

He had a slate. He had written something on it, and he was holding it flat against the satchel because there was a wind off the ash.

The market went on. Somebody was selling fired tiles off a barrow at the south end, four to a shilling, and the man selling them was shouting about a kiln mark, and a woman with a barrow of kindling wood went past the second span and stopped and looked at the stranger and did not say anything and went on, and that is the whole of how a market receives a man.

The four hundred yards of tiled kerb went up the bridge on both sides, and a road in this county was a record: every span was edged in fired tile, one tile to a kerb stone, the device impressed, the kiln's mark on the back, and a man who wanted to know what was under his feet could turn a stone over with a heel and read the date and the kiln and the man whose mark was on the back of it.

He understood, standing on the second span, that he was standing in a county that had put its roads in clay because it could not think of another way to stop a road being taken away, and that it had done it a hundred years before the men who built it had any reason to, and that the road it is set in is four hundred years old and this tiling is sixty, and that the tiles in it are the tiles of men who are dead and whose names are on the backs of them.

"Where did you come out?" the woman said. "Not who. Where."

"Off a stair," he said. "Down there. I put my foot back and there was no edge."

"I know there was no edge," she said. "I have seen it twice. It comes out at the third step and it goes about the length of a cart and a man cannot tell whether he has come out of a road or off a road, and both of the men it did that to were told by their own offices that they had opened a door, and one of them went home and one of them did not."

"Then you know what it is."

"I know what I have seen in this county," she said, "and I have seen a thing come out of a stair twice, and I am a person who cuts tiles for a road, and there is no column in this county for what you are and I am not going to invent one at four hours in the morning on a market day."

---

"Then the road does not open," he said.

"No."

He had it. He had been in four rooms about it in eleven years and he had never once had it said to him by a person who was not a keeper, a magistrate, a steward or a marshal, and it took about a second and a half, and what it took was not the refusal.

It was the arithmetic of the thing. A line and two hands. A date. A witness at each end who is not the person keeping the road, or it does not open, and it does not fail. He was standing on a bridge in a market town in a county that had not agreed to be a road, in a market, on a day that was not the ninth of anything, with one end of a line in a fen four hundred miles behind him and **no end of it at this side at all.** The hand was not here and the witness was not here and the date meant nothing to anybody in this county, and the whole of the arrangement was true, and every part of it that mattered was on one end.

A road that is not the road is a door. He had written that on the third of Embermonth in a notebook and had a page in a filing room that said the same thing in a fair hand, and both of them had been written by people who had never had it happen to them.

He did not argue. He wanted to say four things and he did not say any of them, and the reason he did not say them is that every one of the four was a reason why he should be let in, and a man who gives reasons to be let in is asking a favour and a favour can be refused and then there is a refused favour instead of a refused road, and a refused favour is a smaller thing and a meaner thing and it is what half the men who came to that city offered it.

He said instead, out loud, on a bridge, to a woman of about forty with a set of tile-cutters' tools at her hip:

"You did not open it and you were right. I am not the man who sends anybody. And it made no difference, which is how I know you were right."

The woman on the fourth span looked at him for about as long as it takes to count nine, and then she said the thing that was going to be in four books in about nine months.

"Get off my kerb," she said. "You are standing on a tile and it has got a mark on it and you have your weight on it and the mark is the only account of the man that cut it and he has been dead thirty years, and you are a heavy man and a wet morning."

He got off the kerb.

---

The man with the ledger came up the bridge at about half past four, which is the hour the market breaks, and he had the slate under his arm and a stub of pencil, and he came to within about six feet of Aren Kest and stopped, and he did not ask who he was.

"Right," he said, to the woman, and to the air. "And the name."

"No."

"It wants a name. It has always wanted a name."

"Then it will have got a space and nothing in it," she said, "and a space with nothing in it is not a lie, and I am not going to be the first thing this county has written down that cannot be got back."

The man with the ledger stood with the pencil about an inch off the slate for a moment, and then he wrote, and Aren Kest could not see what he wrote and did not try, and the man read it back to her in the voice of a man reading a line out of a ledger to a person who has not consented to it and is going to consent to it.

*The twenty-second day of the rolling, being the ninth day after the low water, and the year here is counted from the first firing, which is not your new year and I do not know what to call your new year and I have written it as I count and you may correct it if you can find anybody here who knows. A man out of a road. Asked. Said no. Said he was not the man who sends. Named nothing. Said nothing that anybody here can check. Put his weight on a kerb tile of the Ninebridge and was put off it by the hand.*

And then he said, "Hand, then," with the pencil going to the column, and she said no again, and he said, "There is a column," and she said:

"There is a column and there is nothing in it and both of those are the truth, and you have written nine lines in this book this month and the county has had a road open across the Kiln since before I could walk and nobody has ever asked who keeps it, and the answer to that is that everybody keeps it and nobody is named, and that is how you have a road. Put a name in that column and in four years you will have one name on a road in a county of nine hundred and forty-one hearths, and he will be the man who keeps it, and when he is gone there will be a column and a name and nobody on the bridge, and there will be a clerk two hundred miles off writing that the hand of the Ninebridge is a person."

She said all of that in about nine seconds, standing up, in the voice of somebody who has cut four hundred yards of kerb and has never once been asked a question in her life and has thought about the answer for eleven years.

Aren Kest, who had come four hundred miles on a road that would not open, heard a county refuse to be copied, and did not say one word, because there was nothing in him to say, and because the last time in his life he had been in a room with a refusal in it that was better than his own argument, a bench of nine had refused a Crown's inspection on a deck in the rain and had gone on holding the stone for four generations.

He went back down the clay steps on the side of them he had come up, and put his hand on the coping going down, which is what a man does on a stair in a country he is a stranger in, and the stone was cold, and there was no warmth in it whatever, and which is a fact he wrote down four months later about a hand and a habit and did not know was a fact yet.

The man with the ledger did not follow him. He put the slate in the satchel and the ledger under his arm and went along the kerb line on the north side, counting, with the black thumb going from tile to tile, the way a man does who counts fires and not himself.
