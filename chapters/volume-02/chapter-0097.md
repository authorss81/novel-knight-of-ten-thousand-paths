# Chapter 0097

The twenty-fourth of Greenmonth was a Wednesday and it was not a market day, and the road was shut.

That is the first thing and it is the whole of the day, and everything else happened in the fortnight that the shutting buys: eleven tons of stone going down to the Sedge Reach on four flat carts, a fortnight with the Sedge Reach closed to carts and open to people on foot, a bench of nine asked for money for the third time and saying yes for the third time, and a bill in a room over a weigh-house with a line in it that had no figure against it.

Tovan's yard had fourteen days in it and Pei Tarrow was in it, and the fourteen was the same figure the yard had on the fourth of Hearthmonth and the same figure it has carried through every month since, and Tovan Kest wrote it in the week-book on the Monday with the hour against it and nobody in the yard said anything about it, because the last time the yard was a fortnight and the fortnight was the fourth of Hearthmonth, a man said one sentence to his brother at a gate with his hand on a rope, in daylight, with the yard going on round them, so that on the day he said nothing about it his brother would not think it was his brother, and the fortnight has held for a hundred and ten days, and nobody has been put off, and the boy is still in it.

**The four days are still on the page, and they are in a hand Tovan Kest's brother would know, and they are four entries, three of them are set out here and the fourth is dated the day he came back, and they are not tidied into one.**

*Fifth of Wolfmonth, second hour. Eleven days, and the stone is gone and the wall is going, and I have told him this morning and I am not going to tell him again.*
*Seventh of Wolfmonth, fifth hour. Fifteen days, and the wall is re-spoken for four days, and I have written those four days as days and not as a figure, and I know what I am doing and I am doing it in front of four men.*
*Eighth of Wolfmonth, first hour. Fourteen days, and Tarrow put off two days and not three, and one day off comes off, and the yard is a fortnight.*

He had put the page in the press in the guild hall over the wharf in the ninth of Frostmonth, with the rest of the correction, where anybody could come and read it, and eleven people had, and two of them had been angry with him, and one of them had said it was the only honest page in the book.

---

**The bench sat at the fourth hour on the twenty-fourth and asked for money for the third time, and it said yes for the third time, and the whole of that took six minutes and there is a page in the guild's roll that is the shortest thing in it.**

The ask was for the scour. The bench had asked in the fourth of Frostmonth and been given two marks and a subscription book; it had asked again in the ninth of Hearthmonth and been given the same, out of the same chest, in the same words. There was a subscription book in a market in this town and it had been open since the fourth of Frostmonth and it had four names in it and two of them were the same man twice.

"Third time," said the bench-clerk, who was a careful man of nineteen years' standing in a copyist's trade and had not been a member of the bench and never would be. "Asked. Declined, with a chest and a book. I am obliged to put to the bench that this is the third asking."

"Yes," said the reeve.

"Will the bench ask a fourth time."

"Yes," said the reeve, and then looked at the middle of the long table for long enough for the room to wait. "And I am going to say one thing before it votes, and I said a thing in this hall in the fourth of Frostmonth that was about a bench asked three times and saying yes three times, and I would like the minute to carry that I said it in the ninth month and the bench voted for the thing in spite of it, because the man who said it was right and the bench knows it and a bench that votes for a thing in spite of the fact that it is a subscription is a bench. Tovan Kest said it in this hall in the fourth of Frostmonth. He is not a member of this bench and he has not been since he was twenty-one and he is not standing for anything. He was right, and I would like it in the minute that he was right, and I would like the minute to say that the bench knows it and asked anyway, because that is a different thing from not knowing."

Nobody improved it. The bench-clerk read it back. The bench asked a third time, and said yes a third time, and the chest had two marks in it, and the book was in a market, and the sum wanted was more than the bench has had in a year, and the bench entered the work in the roll and entered nothing else, and that is the third asking and there is going to be a fourth and everybody in the hall knew it.

---

**The Sedge Reach was read on the twenty-fourth, at about the seventh hour, by the guild's man with the levels, alone, and Tovan Kest stood next to him because the bench minuted that a man of the yard should stand there, and neither of them said one word about the third reading for about nine minutes.**

There is a quarter of an inch of weather in the two numbers that ring has and there is a third number now.

The third number is not a third number. The third number is the same two numbers with a hole under them, and the guild's man with the levels wrote it in his own book in his own hand, and it is this:

*Scour at the third pier, nose. Two fingers at the nose and falling away, and eleven fingers at the tail of the hole at low water of yesterday, and a washtub, and it is a washtub and not a scour and has been since about the sixth of Mudmonth, and there is nothing in this county to put in a washtub and the honest repair is eleven tons of stone and a fortnight with the road shut, and the fortnight begins today because the bench named today, and I would like it entered that this is the third reading of this year and the first one I have taken with anybody else in the room, and the reason there is anybody else in the room is that the man who read it twice before me went out of the county at the fourth hour of a market morning and was gone eighty-eight days.*

"It is a washtub," said the guild's man with the levels. "It has been a washtub since the sixth of Mudmonth, and that is most of a year. Nobody has read it. There is a bench that has asked for money twice and been told no twice, and a man in this county who read it twice in this year went out of the county, and that is the whole of how a washtub happens."

"Then it is on the bill," said Tovan Kest. "It has to be on the bill, and if it is on the bill it has to have a figure against it, and if it has a figure against it somebody has to pay it, and there is nobody in this county who has eleven tons of stone or a fortnight."

"There is you."

"There is me with fourteen days and four men and a road shut on the Sedge Reach for the fortnight, which means I cannot get to it, and that is the first time in nineteen years that a shut road has been a problem instead of a wage."

---

**The bill went into a room over a weigh-house on the twenty-fourth at about the fourth hour, and it is the trust's account for the absence, and the absence is the two crossings and nothing else, at one hundred and fourteen days, and the eleven markets are inside the hundred and fourteen days and the ninth of Embermonth is inside the eleven and is not a twelfth market.**

It is in a clerk's hand and it is a column and it foots, because a money column that does not foot is a speech.

*To the clerk of the county, the account of the Bellweather crossing trust in abeyance, for the absence of the road warden of this county, being one hundred and fourteen days, being the two crossings and nothing else.*

*One. Markets lost or held late and thin, by custom, eleven of them: twenty-nine marks, nineteen shillings and sixpence.*

*Two. Tolls not taken on the shut wharf road, one hundred and fourteen days: three marks, nineteen shillings.*

*Three. Three Tuesday barges held off at the Old Toll: four marks, two shillings.*

*Four. Cartage, stone, lime, oak and nails, for the scour at the Sedge Reach: two marks, nine shillings.*

*Five.* — and the clerk ruled the line and wrote under the number and then left the space for a figure, and the space stayed empty — *The scour at the third pier, nose, uninspected from the ninth of Embermonth.*

*Total: fifty marks, one shilling and sixpence.*

It foots in pence, and a person who cannot add it should not be in the room, and it adds: sixteen hundred and twenty-six, three hundred and seventy-two, two hundred and sixteen, two hundred and four. Sixteen hundred and twenty-six and three hundred and seventy-two is one thousand nine hundred and ninety-eight. Two hundred and sixteen makes two thousand two hundred and fourteen. Two hundred and four makes two thousand four hundred and eighteen. And two thousand four hundred and eighteen pence is fifty marks and one shilling and sixpence, and the line with the empty space in it is not in the total because there is nothing in it to total, and a clerk who has left a space empty in a column has made a hole in a document on purpose and knows it.

**Nobody in that office can pay any of it.** The trust is in abeyance, so it has no owner, so the tolls are suspended by an instrument that a court has found lawful and has said it has nothing further to say about at any time, and the chest in the guild hall has two marks in it, and a subscription book has been open in a market since the fourth of Frostmonth, and the total is two thousand four hundred and eighteen pence.

And in a different book, in a different hand, and never in the same sentence as anything above, three other figures, none of which anybody in that office is going to be paid:

*Owed by the trust in abeyance to a man for eleven days' crew pay and his own account of the sixth of Mudmonth: eleven marks and fourpence.*

*Owed on a cutting contract at eight pence a day, carried if the cutting fails: two marks and three quarters.*

*Owed for a copying post at three shillings a week, unclaimed, sixteen weeks and two days: twelve marks and tenpence.*

The third of those is the one with a cage in it.

---

**It had been a post for sixteen weeks and two days and the post had gone in Thawmonth and a man had been put in it, and the county's surveyor had taken a man on, and the three shillings a week for sixteen weeks and two days is twelve marks and tenpence and it is in the county's hands and there is no post for it to be in.**

"A post at three shillings a week," said Mara Vey, "is thirty-six pence a week, and it is a cage, and a cage is a place a man is sent for and also a place a man is sent away from."

"You wrote that out," said Aren Kest, "in Fallowmonth, in four lines, in a day-book, and you wrote out the first half of it and not the second half, and I have read the four lines, and you did write the first half."

"I wrote the first half," said Mara Vey, "and I did not know the second half was coming, and I am not going to pretend now that I did not see it coming, because I have been the woman who sees things coming for four years and a half and it is the only thing I am good at and this time I did not."

**The scour under the nose of the third pier had been a washtub for most of a year, and the line with no figure against it had been on that bill for two days, and somebody read it on the twenty-fourth of Greenmonth at about the fourth hour, and the person who read it was not in the office and had not been in the office for eighty-eight days and was standing on a deck in the rain at that hour counting load points out of a habit he has never told anybody about.**

He came in at the fifth hour and put a wet sleeve on the table and read the line, and read it twice, and then said:

"That is the best thing anybody in this county has done this year and it is one line with nothing in it."

"It is a hole in a document," said Mara Vey.

"It is a hole in a document that has been printed and sent to four offices, and every office that has had it has looked at it and nobody has asked what a scour at the nose of a pier is, and the answer is that a scour at the nose of a pier is the ground under the ground under a bridge, and the ground under a bridge is the only thing in this county that nobody reads, and a county that does not read it does not have to pay for it, and that is not a conspiracy. **That is what a bill is.** A bill is a list of the things somebody was too tired to look at, and it is the only list in the world that is honest about being one, and you have printed yours and sent it out and you have left a line empty on purpose, and in about nine years somebody is going to look at that line and understand that the woman who left it empty is the reason a bridge is still up."

She wrote the hour against it. Against no person.

---

**And the last thing that happened on the twenty-fourth of Greenmonth happened in a yard on Weir Street at about the ninth hour, and there were four men in it and a boy of fifteen at a bench-board, and the road was shut a fortnight and the yard was a fortnight, and Tovan Kest said the sentence.**

He had it ready. That is the thing about it and he had known for about four days that he had it ready, and he had written nothing down, because a reason is a thing believed and a yard's book holds things set.

"You stood in a keeper's room on the second of this month," he said, "and you did five things in the wrong order, and I watched all five, and I have thought about it every day since and I have got it down to about nine words and I am going to say them once."

"Go on."

"**I am not going to re-teach you your own brother.**"

Nobody in that yard moved. The boy of fifteen at the bench-board did not look up, and the man at the drawknife put a tool down about half an inch, and neither of them said a word, and there is not one person in that yard who was ever going to use the word brother about those two again.

"You can still do all of it," said Tovan Kest. "That is the part I have got to be fair about, and I am going to be fair about it because you would know in about four seconds if I was not. You can do every one of the six or seven things. I have watched you do them. You cannot do them in the order, and I do not know what the order was, and if I told you what I think the order was you would do it my way and not yours, and in about a year you would be doing a thing in a yard that is not a morning.

"And I am not going to sit there at about the sixth hour on a Tuesday and say it out loud in a room over a cooper, six or seven times, for a year, **because that is how a man re-teaches a person a thing, and I have watched one person be re-taught one thing in my life and it took about four months and it cost both of them, and one of them is standing in front of me with a fever's gap in him and a bag with grey clay in the seam of it.**"

"I did not ask you to say it."

"I know you did not ask me to say it," said Tovan Kest. "**That is the whole of it and I want it written down that I know that and I said it anyway, and I have been keeping that sentence since the fourth of Hearthmonth, when I said a different one, and I have had a hundred and ten days with it, and I have decided that the one I said then was worse, because it was about a thing you would lose, and this one is about a thing I am not going to do, and a man can be held to a thing he is not going to do.**"

"And is that all of it."

"That is all of it," said Tovan Kest, "and it is spent, and I am not going to improve on it, and you are not going to ask me about it, and if you do I will tell you what I tell the bench when they ask me a fourth time, which is the number."

Then he went back to the bench-board and the yard went on, and the road was shut for a fortnight, and the fourteen days was still fourteen days on the Monday, and the fourth of Hearthmonth had not cost anybody a man, and Tarrow was still in it, and a boy of fifteen copied a line of a bill into a yard's book with the hour against it and copied the empty space under the number as well, because that is what the boy does.
