# Chapter 0061

It was the first day of the twenty-three days he was going to be in the County of Ash, and the county's own date for it was the twenty-second day of the rolling, being the ninth day after the low water, and the year here was counted from the first firing and was not any new year that anybody four hundred miles off could check. He had a piece of paper in a coat that gave him that date in three parts and a boy of fourteen had carried it up Weir Street on the eighth of the last month, and he had looked at it once a day since and it had not got any easier to check and it was the only calendar he had.

The walk to Kilnholt's kiln shed took fifty minutes and she spent eleven of them saying nothing, and then said one thing.

"You will want to know where the county is," she said, at about the fourth of the ten, on a street where the light was coming off a hill and stopping at four hundred yards.

"I wrote *north*," Aren Kest said. "In a column, in a book, two hundred miles off."

"You wrote it in a book," she said, "and the man that wrote it is not standing in the street. It is his county now for about a year, and then he will want it back, and there will be a letter about it."

Then she said nothing else for the rest of the walk, and he understood that this was not rudeness. She had four hundred yards of tiled kerb on a nine-span bridge and she was walking a stranger to a kiln shed instead of doing it, and a person does one of those things or the other on any given morning and the morning decides.

---

The kiln shed is the third building in from the low end of a street that runs down to the Kiln, and it is called a shed and not a building, because in the County of Ash a thing that makes smoke is not a building and a thing that does not is.

The smell got to him about thirty paces off. He had walked into nine days of kiln on a bridge four hours earlier and had thought that was the smell of a county. This was the same smell with a person in it: wet ash-wood, peat, hot stone, and under all of it a flat mineral sourness that was the clay itself cooking, and he could not put a name to it then and named it later, twice, and both times it was wrong.

There were four kilns in this county. Two of them were at Kilnholt, and the one in the shed was a rolling kiln, which means it is shut for nine days at a time and takes a cord of wood a day for the nine days, and every day of the nine the men cut and split and carry, and the ninth day they pull the fire out and let it die, and it is opened on the eleventh day off the fire, and a man does not count days off a fire in his head, he counts them off the kiln, and the kiln has a mark on it for it.

The eleventh day off the fire is the counting day, and it was the eleventh day off the fire, and that is a different eleven from any other eleven in this county and a man who has been here a week can tell them apart and a man who has been here a day cannot. Two hundred yards of stacked tile stood in ranks against the north wall under a roof of boards, banded in fours with withies, and the withies were green, which meant they had been cut inside the year, and the smell of the ash-wood in them was the only green thing in the county.

He looked for a load path.

It took him about four seconds and it was the first thing in this county that came naturally to him, and there was nothing. There was no arch and no span and no wall that anybody had ever doubted. There was a shed and a kiln in it and nine men standing about in the ordinary way that men stand about on the eleventh day off a fire when the fire is out and the work is counting and the pay is on Friday.

Nine men, and he counted them twice, and there were nine.

There was nothing in the shed that carried a load that anybody had ever doubted. He stood in a field of fired clay with a man who could read the weight of a thing by the sound it made coming down, and there was not one thing in the building he would have put his hand on.

"You will want to sit," said the man with the ledger, "and you will want to be quiet, and if you are not quiet I will ask you to be quiet once and then I will ask you again."

He was the same man from the bridge. Sixty or thereabouts, a satchel on a strap, the ledger under his arm, and his right thumb black to the second joint, and the black had gone into the creases so deep that the creases had stopped being creases and had become the colour.

The count went on for about two hours and it was the most competent thing Aren Kest had seen in nineteen years, and he had spent eleven of them in a place where a man with a bar was the best there was.

Tiles are fired in fours. A firing is charged in fours and a rolling is charged in fours, and the whole of the county's output for nineteen years is a multiple of four, and the four is not a preference: a kiln is stacked four to a face and a face is what a kiln man can lift without his hands on the fire, and the count is a count of faces and not of tiles, and every tile in the county has three brothers except the ones that were the last of a firing and were put in a hand-barrow because the kiln was cold and the day was short.

"Read it out," the man said, to nobody in particular, in the voice of a man who has read it out about four hundred times.

A boy of about fourteen with a slate and a stub of pencil stood up off a packing bench. He was reading from a slate, and the slate had columns ruled on it in chalk because a slate does not take ink and a man who has been counting for nineteen years does not rule a slate in ink, because ink costs and dies.

"The whole of the fourth kiln, this season, being the first firing, eleven days off the fire: one thousand six hundred and eighty."

"Put it."

"Put it," said the man, and the boy put it.

"The whole of the second kiln, this season, being the second firing, eleven days off the fire: one thousand two hundred and twenty-six."

"Put it."

And then the boy said the number that had been standing in the county's ledger for nineteen years, and he said it in the flat voice of a boy reading a line out of a ledger to a room that has heard it four hundred times.

"Two thousand nine hundred and six."

"Put it," said the man, and the boy put it, and the man went along the ranks with the black thumb going from tile to tile, the way a man does who counts fires and does not count himself, and about a third of the way back he stopped and came and stood in front of Aren Kest.

"Right," he said. "The roll of persons of this county is nine hundred and forty-one hearths, and there are two thousand nine hundred and six tiles on the roll of persons fired to date, and that is the count, and the count is done, and it is now your business to be quiet."

"May I ask a question?"

"You may ask a question. You will get one answer and it will be a number, and then you will be quiet, because I have got nine men in here on a Friday's count and a stranger on a Monday's walk and I do not do the two at once."

"What are the tiles for?"

The man with the ledger looked at him for about as long as it takes to count nine.

"They are for people," he said.

"Then how many people are there?"

"Nine hundred and forty-one hearths," said the man with the ledger, "and a hearth is a fire, and a fire is what a person has, and there is no column in this county for a person, and there has not been one since the roll was begun, and I have never wanted one."

Aren Kest stood in a shed with fired clay stacked to the roof in a county four hundred miles from a fen, and he did the thing his whole trade was made of. He looked at a figure on a slate and at a number in a man's mouth, and the number was two thousand nine hundred and six, and the other was nine hundred and forty-one, and he divided the one by the other, because that is what a man does with two numbers.

"Two thousand nine hundred and six tiles in fours is seven hundred and twenty-six and two over," he said. "Nine hundred and forty-one hearths in fours is two hundred and thirty-five and one over."

"Yes," said the man with the ledger. "That is the one tile in this county that has not got three brothers and has not had since the first firing, and I have never known why there was one and I have never cared."

"That is not a whole number of people either," said Aren Kest. "Nine hundred and forty-one is a fire. How many people are there?"

And the man with the ledger did not answer him.

He stood in the middle of his own shed with the ledger under his arm, and he looked at a stranger, and the thing that went across his face was not surprise. It was the particular stillness of a man who has been asked a question he is able to answer and has discovered, in the time it takes to find the answer, that nobody in nineteen years has thought to ask it, and that he has a number ready, and that the number has been in his head the whole time.

He held up his right hand, flat, and looked at the black thumb, and then he said:

"This county has always counted fires," he said, "and has never counted itself. I want that said in the order it is true and not as a sermon, because I count and I do not preach, and there is one of these in this shed and it is not me."

"Then give me the number."

"Two thousand four hundred and fifty," said the man with the ledger.

And then he said the second number, out loud, in the middle of a shed, to a room, having said the first one for the first time in nineteen years, and he said it in the voice of a man reading a line out of a ledger.

"Four hundred and fifty-six."

And he stopped.

He did not say what it was. He did not go on. He put his hand on the ledger and his thumb on the edge of it, and he did not explain, and he did not look at the boy, and he did not look at the men at the packing bench, and after about two seconds he said, in a completely different voice, as though the first thing had not been said:

"Two hundred and forty faces on the far wall, being the second kiln's first firing and not on the roll, and I have not counted those because they are for a house and not for a person, and that is the difference and I am not going to talk about the difference, and if you ask me one more question in this shed I will count you out of the county myself."

Nobody in the shed had moved.

They had all heard the first number and about four of them had heard the second, and a stock count on the eleventh day off a fire is a thing a room of nine men does with its hands while its ears are on the kiln, and the first number was a number, and the second number was a number, and a man who has been in a trade for a long time does not stop what he is doing because of a number.

Aren Kest did it in about a minute and a half, and he did it the way he had done every sum he had ever done in his life, without ceremony, out loud, because a sum is a fact and a fact said quietly is a thing a man can be wrong about.

He said: "Two thousand four hundred and fifty."

Then he said: "Two thousand nine hundred and six."

Then he said: "Four hundred and fifty-six."

Then he said, because he was a man who answers the question asked and adds the load-bearing fact unprompted, and because nobody else in the room was going to say it and the sentence was in his mouth and it was true:

"There are four hundred and fifty-six more tiles fired on the roll of this county than there are people in it."

The room stopped.

Not all at once, and not the way a room stops when a man is a stranger and says something strange. The man at the packing bench put a tile down on the bench instead of taking the next one off the stack, and left it there. The boy with the slate did not put a number down and did not read the next one. A man by the kiln mouth turned his head about nine inches. Five of the nine went still in the particular way of men who have been told a thing they already knew and have known it in a different shape, and the two that did not go still were the man with the ledger and a man of about fifty-eight standing third from the left with his hands black to the wrist.

Nobody said anything at all for about eleven seconds, and the kiln ticked, which is a thing a cooling kiln does, and it ticks for days.

The man of fifty-eight came forward and put his own hand, flat, on the side of the nearest rank of tiles, the way you put a hand on a horse you have owned a long time.

"That is right," he said.

Nobody asked him how he knew.

"That is right," he said again, "and I have known since the fourth rolling and I have not said it, and I am not saying it now because he said it, and I would like to say it once in a room on my own and there is going to be no such room, and that is what this county is."

"It is your mother," said the man with the ledger, from the middle of the shed, in the voice of a clerk reading a line.

"It is my mother," said Ostry Vaile, "and she has been fired twice. She was struck off the count eleven months ago, same as me, same week, same list, and the first time they made her a tile and fired it, and the second time was in the winter, and the second time there was no fire at all. There was a soft tile on a board and somebody re-pressed it, and it was my sister's husband, and he did it in the morning before he went, and he did it with his thumb and a wet cloth, and nobody asked her, and I did not ask her, because I was on the list and I was not asked either, and that is the arrangement, and she has been in the house since the winter and she is in the house and I know her and there is nothing in this county that can be asked about her."

He said all of that in about nineteen seconds to a room of nine men in a kiln shed, and then he put his hand back on the rank of tiles and said:

"That is the four hundred and fifty-six and you can have it, and it is not mine, and I am not going to say what else it is, and you can all get back to the count."

And they did. The man at the packing bench took the tile off the bench and put it on the stack, in the wrong place, and the boy with the slate read the next number, and the man by the kiln mouth turned his head back to the kiln, and about four minutes later a man in the corner who had not moved the whole time said, to nobody, "Two hundred and forty on the far wall," and started counting the second kiln's first firing.

Aren Kest stood in a shed in a county of nine hundred and forty-one hearths and had the number and could not put it down anywhere, and that was the whole of his trade: there is nothing in this to look at. No load. No span. No arch with a bad course in it. A number, said out loud by a man who counted fires, checked out loud by a man who could add, in a room of nine people of whom five were on a list, and the room had stopped, and then the room had gone back to work, and the count was finished at about the seventh hour with the total the same as it had been at the eleventh hour because a count is a count and nothing in that shed had changed except the number of people in the room who knew it.

On the way out she said the only other thing she said to him that day.

"We did not ask you anything," she said. "Not one thing. You have been in this county four hours and a man has told you the size of it and a man has told you what is wrong with it, and you have not been asked a single question about who you are or what you are for, and that is not courtesy and it is not a trick. We do not ask. There is no column for it and there has never been one, and the only man in this county who would have filled one is standing in his own shed with a ledger under his arm and would not put his own name in a cell."

"He would not put a name in a cell this morning either," Aren Kest said.

"No," she said. "He refused me twice yesterday and he will refuse me again, and he is right, and I have been right for eleven years, and neither of us has ever been asked."
