# Chapter 0082

The rite worked for eleven weeks and nobody in the County of Ash noticed anything wrong in it.

That is the sentence the whole thing is built on, and it is worth saying exactly what it means, because a man who has spent eleven months looking for the thing that has gone wrong is going to spend the next four months of it waiting for somebody to say it out loud, and nobody does, and one person does say something else in front of about four hundred people and is not listened to.

The work was fast. It was local. It was unpaid. It was done, in the end, by the county's own people, and not one of the sixty people who had been in the hall when it was proposed was doing any of it by the fourth week, which is the only part of the whole business that a person from a fen would have got right on the first morning.

---

The room was the long room at the assizes and it was open four times a day, from the second hour to the sixth, and it was open on the days the kilns were not, which in that season was most of them, and the room held a long table and about nine chairs and a stove and a shelf, and the shelf was for the boards.

A board is a plank about the length of a forearm with a wet cloth over a soft tile on it, and there are about four hundred of them in this county at any one time, and they are kept on racks in the cutting shed at the north end of the Ninebridge and they are the only object in the County of Ash that a person owns and does not own, because the tile on a board carries a mark pressed into it by the owner's own hand and the county's law does not have one word about a soft tile and never has.

The rite took about eleven minutes a person.

The person came in and stood at the table. They said their own name, because the clerk had learned by the third day that a person who is asked *are you on this list* and says yes is answering about a list, and a person who is asked *what is your name* and says it is answering about themselves, and the difference between those two is the whole of the fourth term and there is no form anywhere in this county that asks it.

Then the person pressed their own tile. Not the dish — the tile. The rule in this trade, which Aren Kest had learned on the fortieth board of a rack in his fourth week in this county and had written down in a notebook and had told nobody, is: do not press the tile, press the dish. Press around the mark and the dish comes up against the mark and the mark stands up in it, and a mark that stands up in a dish can be read at four feet on a wet morning. And a press took about a minute, because a press is not a job, it is a thing a four-year-old can do.

Then the two witnesses put their hands onto two other tiles, and pressed, and the two other tiles were theirs.

Then the three went onto a board together and the board went onto a shelf, and the clerk wrote the date and the hour on the board's edge in a flat unhurried hand, and the clerk wrote nothing else.

**And the office did not read the roll.**

That is what made it work. There was a book on the table in a box and the box was shut and the two witnesses' names came out of the book before the person came in, by the person who was on the door, and the person on the door said them out loud and nobody checked them against anything, and about one time in nine the person on the door said a name that was not in the book and the room found out, and about one time in nine that was the whole of the day's trouble.

Nobody in this county has ever described a piece of civic work as being watched before. They would not have the words. A thing done by a court is heard, and a thing done by a bench is heard, and a thing done by a fire is seen, and this was none of those, and what it was, in the county's own eventual word for the kind of work it was, was that it was a thing people had to stand in.

---

The restorations were read out.

That was not part of the proposal. Nobody proposed it and nobody voted on it and there is no minute of it anywhere. It came about because a room with a queue outside it needs to know who is in it, and the only list that says who is in it is the list of the people who have been put back, and a person who cannot read a book in a wet shed is going to be told what is happening, and the county told him.

It was read at every sitting. Four times a day.

And it was read by Fennie Loam, who is nineteen, who is apprenticed to the clerk of the assizes at three shillings a week and who copies the roll, and who had been the person in that room who knew how two hundred and thirty-one names were spelt, and how their hearths were set out, and what their trades were in the county's own abbreviations, better than anybody else alive, because she copies them every day of her working life and because her hand has never once put a wrong stroke in a name.

She did not know that was a job she had.

Nobody told her. He read it first, on the first day and the second day, the first sitting of the four, standing at the end of the long table with the sheet in his hand, because the clerk of the roll had refused to read the roll out in a room — for the second time in nineteen years, and she was right twice — and the clerk of the assizes could not leave the room because a clerk of an assizes does not leave a room while a list is being read in it, and the magistrate does not read lists, and the physician is a physician, and a man from the north who has been in a clay county for seven months can read.

He read it for two days and then he stopped, and he did not say why, and she took it off him on the third morning without being asked and without a word being said about it, in the way a person takes a hod off another person.

Nobody told her that was a job she had.

She read it properly. That is the thing that has to be said about her and it is the whole of her. She did not run names. She read the name, and the hearth, and the trade, at the speed of a woman reading out a row of things she has copied four hundred times, and she got every one of them right, and she put a comma where a comma goes, and she did not put a sentence round it, and she did not say *and this one* or *and here is somebody you will remember*, and when a name in the list was one the room knew she let the room make its own noise about it and did not help.

And she got better. That is the other half of it. She got better in the fourth week, and by the eighth week the two men on the door were reading the names in with her under their breath, half a beat behind, and by the eleventh week a man at the back of the queue was reading them before she got to them, and she noticed that and slowed down very slightly to let him keep up, and that was the most generous thing anybody did in that room in eleven weeks and she has no idea she did it.

---

It is the only piece of civic work anybody has ever described as being watched rather than heard, and it is watched, and it is watched about four hundred times a day, and nobody in the County of Ash knows what that is.

There is a physician in this county of sixty-three who wrote four pages in the third year of the softening and had them in a drawer for four years, and the second of the four pages says that the softening enters by the mark and travels from a mark to the person who reads it, and from a person to the marks of that person's household, and travels fastest along the marks that are read most, and that the marks read most in this county are the roll marks, because a clerk reads the roll every morning and does not know that she is doing anything.

**A person is safe only until somebody in that house opens a book.**

Nobody in that room opened a book. That is the thing. The whole of the finding was about houses, and about a mark being read, and about a family, and about a person sitting at a table with something open in front of them. And for nineteen years this county had been the safest place on this river, because nobody here could read and nobody here had a book, and a county that cannot read cannot carry a disease that travels along reading, and the roll was read by one woman in one room in nineteen years and she read it to herself and to nobody else.

And they opened it. Four times a day. Two hundred and thirty-one names in the sixth week and four hundred and something by the eleventh, and every one of them said out loud in a room, in a voice belonging to a girl of nineteen, with the hearth and the trade attached, so that the room could check her, and checking her was the point.

Fennie Loam read four hundred people's names four times a day for eleven weeks and she was very good at it and she did not know that was a job she had.

---

He asked her one question on the eleventh day, in a corridor, in about eleven seconds, and it was the only thing he said to her in eleven weeks and she thought nothing of it for about a year.

"Who reads it when you are not here."

And she said, "The man on the door, and he is no good at it, he got Hesper Lound's second hearth wrong twice in one day and the boy with the slate said so, and then on the day after that the man at the loading end read it, and he reads it like a man reading a price, and the women do not like it."

"Right," said Aren Kest.

"You read it," said Fennie Loam. "The first two days. The first sitting. I was in the room and you were at the end of the table and you were better at it than anybody in this county has ever been at anything, including me, and you have been here seven months and you do not know one of these two hundred and thirty-one names."

"I do not know one of them," he said.

"You know how to say them," she said, "which is not the same and is worth more in that room, and I have been thinking about it for eight weeks and I have got to the bottom of it and the bottom of it is that you read it twice a day and you have read it twice a day for eight weeks and you have a fever, and a man reading a list of two hundred and thirty names four times a day in a wet room in the worst of a season is a man who is going to be ill in the ninth month instead of the eleventh, and if you go down in the ninth month I will be doing this alone in the tenth and I would like to know now whether that is the plan."

He did not answer that for about four seconds, and the four seconds were the whole of what happened in that corridor, and it is worth saying what they were: a man being asked by a person who had worked him out, and finding out that he had been worked out, and being glad about it, and being ashamed about it in about the same half-second.

"You read it on the first two days," said Fennie Loam, "and then you stopped, and I have never worked out why, and I have stopped trying, because you are a grown man off a road and there is one word for a grown man off a road and I am nineteen and I get four times a day out of it and I am not going to be the person who asks."

"Yes," said Aren Kest. "That is the correct arrangement and I would have said so myself in a week and you have got there in a day, and I am going to say the rest of it now and then I am going to go and do the bottom of the rack, and the rest of it is: I read it twice a day and I was good at it, and being good at it was the reason I stopped, and that is the wrong reason and you would have been within your rights to say so."

"I would not have known it was the wrong reason," said Fennie Loam, "and I would have said it was the right one, and I would have been wrong in a way that made you feel better, and you have just saved me about a year of that and I do not think you meant to."

She went off with the copy under her arm, because it was the fourth of the month and the copy had to be in by the fourth, and he stood in the corridor afterwards for about a minute and a half.

What he wrote in the notebook that night, in the flat hand, in the back, was that the fastest line in this county is a list of restorations read aloud four times a day, and that he read it twice a day for two days and gave it to a girl of nineteen, and that the reading did not stop when he gave it up, which is the only good thing in the business and is also the reason there is going to be nothing to be done about it.

---

Bel was at the fourth row.

She is four. She is smaller than anybody at that rack and better at the check round than anybody at that rack, for being small and for being new, and the check round is this: you go down a board and you put a finger on the mark and you see whether the mark has walked off the edge, and if it has you say *that one wants pressing* and you do not press it, you only say so, and then a person comes and presses it properly around the mark with a wet cloth in about a minute, and a press is not a job, it is a thing a four-year-old can do.

She did the fourth row for eleven weeks. She said *that one wants pressing* about nine times a day and she was right nine times in a row more often than not, and on the sixth week she began to say it on the boards at the third row as well without being asked, because the third row belonged to a man who was at a kiln and could not come, and nobody told her to and nobody told her not to.

Nobody looked at what was on her own board. It is the same board it has always been on, in the fourth row, with a cloth over it, and it is a child's tile and a child carries it soft for seven years, and there are about four hundred soft tiles in this county and one of them belongs to her and it is the only one of the four hundred that nobody has ever turned over, because the mark on the back of a soft tile is nobody's business until the seventh year, and a four-year-old's mark is a four-year-old's, and this is the one rule in the County of Ash that has never been broken and has never been written down.

---

A man of about fifty came on the thirty-first day and had not spoken to anybody since the autumn.

Nobody knew that except the two men who walked with him and one of them told the clerk of the roll before he came in, and the reason the clerk of the roll did not enter it is that a man who has not spoken in nine months has not got a fact to enter.

He stood at the table. He said his name. He put his thumb into the wet clay on his own tile and pressed, and he pressed for about four minutes instead of about one minute, and nobody in the room said anything about the four minutes, and after a while the man at the loading end, who was standing at the back with his arms folded, said, out loud, to nobody:

"He is doing it exactly right."

And about eleven people in that room said, together, "Yes."

And that was the loudest thing that happened in eleven weeks, and it happened because eleven people had watched a man do a thing slowly and carefully, and it was not the two minutes that moved them. It was that a man who has not spoken for nine months came into a room and put his own hand on a piece of clay, and did it in front of two witnesses, and the two witnesses watched him do it, and the room watched him do it, and nobody asked him a single question about himself in the whole of it.

**A person can check a rule, and a person who can check is a person who has been asked.**

Nobody said it. It was not in the room. It was in a corridor on the eleventh day, and then in a notebook, and then it was nowhere.

---

The number came off a board, because a board is a thing a county can count.

The boy with the slate — he is fifteen and he has been copying the blanks on this county's price column since the fourth week of the long middle, and copying the blanks is the only thing anybody has ever said about him, and he has never once been asked to stop — stood at the end of the shelf on the last morning of the eleventh week and counted the boards, and he counted them twice, because the second time is the time that counts, and the boy knows that, and nobody has ever taught him that.

He put it on the slate and he showed it to the man with the ledger, who looked at it for a while and then went and got the roll, and the roll does not have a column for it, and the roll has never had a column for it, and the man with the ledger stood there with the book shut in his hands for about a minute and a half.

*Four hundred and nine boards.*

The man with the ledger said four things about that number and three of them were about tiles and one of them was not.

"A soft tile goes out of true in a house in about three weeks," he said. "It lies in a wet board on a shelf and the face creeps and the mark walks off the edge, and a person comes on a day off and presses it round the mark with a wet cloth and it takes about a minute, and a press is not a job. There are about four hundred soft tiles in this county at this hour. There are four hundred and nine boards on that shelf, and eleven weeks is a quarter of a year, and a quarter of a year is at least three presses a house, and a county that puts four hundred and nine boards through one room in one quarter has either got nine hundred and forty-one hearths wrong or has never once counted its boards in nineteen years."

"And it has not," said the ash-driver, from the back.

"It has not," said the man with the ledger. "And there is no column for it and I am not making one this afternoon, because the last four things anybody in this county tried to make a column for came out as six lines with nothing under them, and that was in a room in the seventeenth week and we are all still living in it."

He wrote it on the slate under the figure, in his own hand, and the boy with the slate copied it, and the boy copied the space between the two lines as well, because the boy copies the blanks, and a man in a hall in nine years will be able to look at that slate and see exactly how much of it the county did not know.

**Four hundred and nine.**

That is the figure the eleven weeks made, and it is the only figure anybody in either country is going to be proud of, and it is the figure that is going to appear on a sheet of paper four hundred words long in a fair hand, itemised, in a filing room in a fen county, in a month, and it is going to be a line on a bill, and a clerk is going to check it.
