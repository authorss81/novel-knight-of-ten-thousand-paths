# Chapter 0065

He took four days, and the four days were not the difficulty.

The difficulty was a thing he had not had to think about in nineteen years, which was that everything he had ever been taught to do had been taught to him by a shortage. There was not enough room in the joint, so the load went round. There was not enough iron in the old span, so it went into the new. There was not enough of him to argue with a magistrate, so he had learned to be the one man in a room who had a use for what he was saying, and every trade he had ever entered had taught him the same sentence, which was *take what is there and do not waste a movement*.

And then you are given a board with half an inch of dish in it, and the half an inch has been the right answer for a thousand years, and every movement you make in it is waste, and the way to do it is to be less than the thing.

The first two days he ruined four tiles.

Not badly. Badly enough. On the first day he pressed in the middle, and the dish came up and the edge of the mark went soft and smeared, and he put the board on the bench and looked at it for about a minute and then went and fetched the woman from the door, and she looked at it and said, "That tile and the entry in the roll do not agree, and there is a woman at the third hearth down whose name is on the entry and who has not done anything wrong, and she is off the roll as of about a quarter to six this evening, and when the clerk sees it she will be back on if she can be witnessed and the tile can be brought true, and if she cannot be witnessed then this is a soft person and it was not the softening that did it, it was you, and I do not know which of those is worse and neither do you."

"Press it again."

"Press it again," she said, "and I have watched you and there is one part of you that knows the answer and about three parts of you that are going to keep putting a hand on it, and I have seen men take four days to get to the place where they stop."

On the second day he pressed too much, and the back of the tile came up under his knuckle, and he turned it over, and there was his knuckle, in the clay, on the back of a tile, in a circle, with the whorls in it.

"That is a thumbprint," he said. "That is mine."

"It is yours," said the woman, "and the back of that tile is where the kiln's mark goes, and the kiln's mark is how you know which kiln fired a thing, and this county cannot prove where a tile came from without it, and if a tile has got a knuckle on the back of it then that tile cannot be traced, and a tile that cannot be traced is a tile that a man who wanted to be on this roll could not be kept off it, and the only people in this county who would want to be on this roll are the people who have been put off it, and they are two hundred and six."

He put the tile down.

"So it is the same both ways."

"It is the same both ways and I want you to hold on to that, because a man in this county who does not hold on to it is going to come to the conclusion that a tile is a sort of trap, and it is not a trap, it is a record, and a record does not have an opinion about who you are. But everything I have just told you is a thing a person could do on purpose, and a person could do it to a woman at the third hearth down, and there is no law in this county about a tile. There is a law about the roll and there is a law about the list and there is a four-line act about burning a list in a market square, and there is nothing whatever in nineteen years of this county about a soft tile, and I have told you what it is, and I have told you because you are going to find out and I would rather you had it from a person than from a story."

---

On the third day, Bel pressed her own tile wrong.

She was four and she had been at the low end of the rack for two days going through the boards on her check round, and there was one at the back of the fourth row that had gone out of true in the night, which happens, and she took it out and put it on her knee and wetted the cloth and put it over the face and pressed, once, hard, in the middle, the way children do, because it is faster.

He saw it from about eight feet away and he did not say anything for the length of nine.

He counted nine, and he watched the child press a flat place into a woman's dish, and he understood that in about a hundred and forty days that tile would be a grey nothing and that a woman who was on the roll of persons of the County of Ash would be off it, and that she would not know why, and that nobody in the county would ever be able to tell her, and that the reason she would not know is that a child of four had made a mistake in a shed in about a second and half.

He said one word.

"Again."

Bel looked up. She looked at the tile, and at her hand, and at the tile, and she said, "That's the right bit," and she put the cloth back over the face and pressed, once more, around the mark, and not on it, and the dish came up and the mark stood up in it, and she put the board back in the rack in the fourth row and went back to the check round without another word.

The woman of the Ninebridge was standing in the doorway and had been standing there for about a minute and a half and she had seen the whole of it, and she said nothing then either, and Aren Kest understood about half an hour later, on a later day, that she had been about to tell a child to put a board back where it came from, and had not, and that the reason she had not was that a four-year-old had been told by a stranger that she had it wrong, and had not argued, and had put it right.

"It is the only thing I have been useful for in ten days," he said.

"It is the only thing you have been useful for," she said, "and I would like it on the record that I would have got there in about a week and that a week in this county is the difference between a person on a roll and a person not, and I am not being kind, I am being accurate, and I am also aware that I have a stranger on a board in a shed of mine and he is about to be the reason a four-year-old can read her own tile, and I have thought about that for a minute and a half and I have decided to have it, and I have decided it because of the tiling and not because of the child."

---

The man at the cutting bench was called nothing and asked nothing and had been cutting tile for the Ninebridge for nineteen years, and on the third day he watched Aren Kest press for about a quarter of an hour, and on the fourth day he asked the woman, in front of him, in a voice of about the volume of a man laying a tile down:

"How long has the man from the north been doing that?"

"Four days."

"Four days," the man said, and went on cutting.

And in the afternoon of the same day, when Aren Kest was on the fourth row and out of earshot, he heard the woman say:

"He is a setter. He cuts setts and he sets them in a road and he reads where the load goes, and the clerk wrote that against him on Friday out of a ledger, and I have seen it."

"And a setter is a presser?"

"A setter is a man who has spent nineteen years making a thing true against a thing out of true, at a scale where a mistake is a man in a hole," said the woman. "And I do not know why a man like that is doing a child's job in a shed, and neither does he, and neither do you, and we are all three going to be extremely careful about it in the same way, and I would like it noticed that this is the third person in this county to say a careful thing to him and the first two of them have not been careful at all, and one of them wrote *north*."

The suspicion was not lifted. He could feel it going on into the fourth day like a fire in a bank, and it was correct that it should not be lifted, because the truth was that he had learned the thing in four days, and no man learns a thing in four days, and the woman of the Ninebridge had said as much on the first morning, and the man at the cutting bench knew exactly what he was being told by a stranger who would not go away.

---

On the fourth day he got it, and he got it on about the fortieth board, and nothing happened when he got it.

That is the part he wrote down. He got it and there was no moment. His hand came down through the wet cloth onto a dish that had been out of true for two years, and the thumb stayed off the mark, and the heel of the hand took the low side, and the fingers took the high side, and he brought the whole of the dish up against the mark without touching the mark, and the mark stood up in it, and his hand came off.

And the thing his hand had been doing was a hand setting a thing true against a thing out of true.

He put the board down and looked at his own fingers and had a sensation he had had four times in eleven years and had never once been able to account for. He had it on the third slip of a ferry yard in a city four hundred miles off, at the fourth hour, on a garboard, with a hull-hand of eighteen and a woman who made him say the terms out loud. He had it in a filing room over a salt merchant, in a clamp with a warp in it, a small thing, and he had watched his own hands do it without him. He had carried it in his hands out of another country and nine hundred feet of open air and a crack in a bridge and it had not done anything for him in nineteen years except in rooms where a man wanted a bridge held.

And it was here. On the right side of the world. For the first time.

What he had done on the first day, in the kiln shed, looking for a load path and finding nothing, was the other thing. The other thing needs somewhere to be. It needs a place, and somebody who has agreed that the place is the place, and a promise, and a witness who is not the man doing it, and if any of those three is missing then it does not fail, it does not happen, and nobody is to say afterwards that it failed. That is the whole trade, and that is what he had spent eleven years of his life learning, and none of it is in this shed and none of it is on a board.

A motion is not that. A motion is in his hand and it needs a wet cloth and a board and four days and a shelf with a hand's width of room on it, and it can be done to any mark in the world by anybody with a hand, and there is no promise in it and no witness and no person at the far end who has agreed.

He sat on the low stool at the end of the rack with a cloth in his hand for about a quarter of an hour and did not re-press a single tile, and what he was doing was not a religious act or a revelation or a stage or a rank. He was a sett-cutter from a fen county sitting in a shed in a clay county, and he had found a second use for the hand he already had, and there was nobody in either country to teach it to him and there was nobody in either country who knew he had it, and it was the only thing he had brought four hundred miles that any use at all in the place he had brought it to.

---

On the afternoon of the fourth day she sent him up the bridge to take a look at a run of kerb where the bedding had gone, because there were four stones loose on the second span and a man's heel turns a stone over and reads the back of it, and he went up on his knees along the line of the kerb with his face about nine inches off the tiles, which is how a man reads a road, and which in this county is also how a man reads a record.

And the coping of the second span is about three feet above the line of the kerb, and he put his hand up on it to get himself along, and it was cold.

It was six inches by four and about the thickness of two fingers, and it was grey, and it was fired, and it was clay, and there was a rope on it hanging in two loops and no yoke, and from four feet away, in daylight, on the fourth day he had been in the county, it was not a bell. It had been a bell from two hundred yards in bad light on the first morning and it was not one now.

There was a mark on it.

A hand laid flat on a book.

He had seen that device four times in eleven years in three countries and he could name it in this or any light: in wax on a storehouse door by the fish weir in his own town; in grey wax on a post at the back of a yard, over a doorway nobody can see from the road, never used, in no book; on the back of a letter in a locked room over a cooper that had no chain of custody on it and was never going to have one; and in the finding of a magistrate of his own county in her own hand.

He put his hand back on the coping. He did not touch the tile. He did not put his fingers in the rope, and he did not turn it over, and he did not go and find out about the back of it, and there is a man four hundred miles off who is going to spend nine months six days in a county trying to find out what a mark is, and there is a man on a coping in this county four feet away from one who is going to know it in about a second and half.

The rope was newer than the tile. That was the one fact he got, and he got it without touching anything, because a rope that has been out in the weather for nineteen years goes the colour of the coping and a rope that is a year old is the colour of ash-wood.

On the afternoon of the fifth day, standing at the fourth span with her roll of tools at her hip, the woman of the Ninebridge said:

"Everybody who comes to this town asks me about that."

He waited.

"About the coping."

"I have been asked by a factor's man and by a man off a boat and by two women off a coach and by a man who said he was from a county office two winters ago, and the answers are all the same and the answer is that it is on the coping, and it has been on the coping, and nobody in this county knows what it is, and it is not mine and I do not keep it and I would not have it off there for the two hundred and forty tiles a year that would cost." She looked at him. "You have been staring at it for two days. What is it?"

There it was. There was the room, and the clerk in it, and the fifth time in eleven years.

He had said *bell* in a gateway in a fen county in front of a provincial clerk who would not write it down, and he had said a name of a thing in a county court and a hall and a filing room and a bench, and he had said it in a room with a clerk in it four times in eleven years and the record of it was in four files in two countries, and there was no office in this kingdom that a man could be told about for it, and the reason he had never said it in this county was not courage and not caution and not the road: it was that there is no bell in the County of Ash and there has never been one, because nothing was ever refused here, and a bell is a thing a county puts up for the day it says no, and a county that has never said no has nothing on the coping, and if he told this woman that the object on her coping was the device of an office that cannot be produced then the next thing that would happen is that the object would be carried off to a county two hundred miles off and put in a box with a chain of custody on it, and in nine years there would be a house in another country that had a room in it for one clay tile out of this county, and there would be a person whose job it was.

He said: "I don't know."

And she looked at him for about two seconds and said, "You don't know," and then she said, "Good," and gave him back his cloth, and told him that the fourth row wanted doing before the seventh hour and that the second run on the third span had gone out of true on the north side and would want seeing to, and they went at it, and neither of them said another word about it, and a thing a person has said out loud and been believed, and has been believed wrongly, is a debt and not a release.

That night he wrote four lines in the back of the notebook, and the first three were about tile, and the fourth was this:

*She asked me what is on the coping and I said I did not know. I have not told anybody a false thing in this county in ten days and I have just done it, and I did it well, and she thanked me for it, and she is the only person in this county who has thanked me for anything, and the reason I did it is that I have watched one bell-shaped seal go into a county file off a single stone, and I have been the man who said the name in a room five times, and I am not going to be six. If there is anybody in Merefen who ever asks me what is on the coping of the Ninebridge I am going to have to say the same thing again, and I am going to have to keep saying it, and there is a man four hundred miles off who is going to be asking a room a question about a mark in about nine months, and I am going to be a man who has lied to a woman about it in a shed by a rack of wet clay, and that is the correct order of operations and I did it in it deliberately and I would do it again.*

*Also: a sett-cutter from a fen county is a man who sets things true against things out of true, and I have found out in ten days in a county that counts its fires that that is not a trade, it is a hand, and it is a different hand from the one in the arch, and I do not know what the other one is and I am not going to put it in this book until somebody can be asked whether it should go in a book at all.*
