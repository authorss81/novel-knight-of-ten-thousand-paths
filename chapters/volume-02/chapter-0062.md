# Chapter 0062

The Roll of Persons of the County of Ash is kept at the assizes at Kilnholt, in a long room on the ground floor with three windows and a stove and forty-one shelves, and the shelves go up past where a man can read the labels without a stool, and every label is a hearth number, and a hearth number is a fire.

Pernel Quist had been in the job nineteen years and she was forty-four and she had the room the way a woman of about that age has a kitchen she has had since she was twenty-five: entirely, and with a place in it for everything including the things that have gone wrong.

She did not ask him anything. He came in on the second day's morning, the twenty-third day of the rolling, on the off chance of a man who has walked a country once looking at its books, and she was at the long table with a sheet of account paper over her arm, and she said, without looking up:

"Sit there. Not that chair, that one has a leg out of true and I have not got to it because I have not got to it in four years and I would rather you noticed than that I told you."

He sat in the other chair.

"You are the man out of the road," she said.

"I am."

"You are the one the clerk has written *north* against." She put the paper down. "He came in on Friday evening and told four people and by the second of this month there were eleven people in Kilnholt who knew a stranger had come out of a stair at the Ninebridge, and by the ninth there will be a version with a bar in it, and by the twelfth somebody will have seen the tile on the coping and told you it is a bell. It is not a bell. Do not let anybody show you a story about it."

"I have seen it," he said.

"Then you already have the story," she said, "and you did not have it from me, which is how I like it."

---

She brought out four tiles, and put them down on the long table in a row, and did not explain them, and waited, and he understood that this was the introduction.

Seal-tile. Six inches by four, fired clay, and a fired tile is a permanent thing: it cannot be scratched out, it cannot be argued with, it does not take a second mark, and a man who has one in his hand has in his hand the only account of himself that will be there in fifty years.

The first face carried a device. Pressed, not cut, with a die, and the die is a piece of bone or a piece of hardwood with the device cut into the end of it, and the county has about forty of them and they are older than most of the men who use them.

The second face carried a name, and it was written in wet clay with a stick, and writing in wet clay on a tile is a thing you do once and you cannot do twice, and a person who can write their own name in wet clay can do it again.

The third face carried a hearth. The fourth face carried a trade.

And the fifth face — the reverse of the tile, and Aren Kest turned it over and looked at it for a while before he said anything at all — carried a hand.

A right hand, pressed in, palm down, the fingers splayed, taken in wet clay before the firing, and every whorl and crease in it, and it is not a signature, and it cannot be forged by anybody who has not had the hand.

"That is the person's own," said Pernel Quist. "Not mine, not the county's, not a witness's. It is pressed by the person themselves, in the clay, in this room, with their own hand on it, and I have never in nineteen years known a hand that could not tell me who pressed it, and I have never known a person who could not tell me which of their hands it was. That is the whole of what I do. I take the mark and I enter it, and the two have to agree, and if they do not agree then one of the two is wrong and we go and find out which."

"And if the tile and the mark agree and the person cannot answer?"

"That has never happened," she said.

---

"Then tell me the law," he said, "and tell it in the order it is used and not in the order it is written, and I have read a great many laws and I would rather have yours."

She gave it to him in four sentences and it took her less time than he expected, because she had given it a great many times and had got it down to the bone.

"A person is on the roll of persons if two other persons witness them and their tile agrees with the entry this office holds. That is the whole of it. Not two witnesses and a tile: two witnesses *and* a tile. The witnesses are what keeps a name on the roll when the person is not standing there, and the tile is what keeps the witnesses honest, and between the two of them a name in this county has never been lost. In nineteen years I have had to strike a name off this roll nine times and eight of them were dead."

"And the ninth?"

"The ninth is a mistake I made in my second year," she said, "and the person is alive, and I have never corrected it on the page, and I have corrected it every morning for eighteen years, and you can decide for yourself which of those two things is the job."

She let that sit for exactly as long as it needed to sit, which was about four seconds, and he understood that she had been letting it sit for four seconds for eighteen years, and that this was a technique and not a manner.

"Now the thing you are going to ask me about," she said, "so let us have it and then we can have the rest of the day."

"I was going to ask you what happens to a person who goes soft."

"They lose the order of their own day," she said. "Then their trade. Then their hearth. Then their own name. That is the county's word for it and it is a good word and I have never improved on it. A person is going back to being clay. That is Hesper Lound's phrase and he is the physician and he is sixty-three and he has been wrong about most things he has had an opinion on since about the year before last, and I have told him so in a room with the door shut, and he agreed with me, and then he wrote four pages and put them in a drawer and I have not read them again since I read them once."

"You read it once."

"I read it once and I have thought about it for four years," she said, "and I am going to tell you what I concluded, because you will hear it in about a week from somebody who has not thought about it for four years, and I would rather you had mine first. He wrote it about marks. He wrote that a mark that is read travels into the person it is read from, and that a person who has been read travels it into the marks of their household, and that it travels fastest along the marks that are read most. That is the finding. It is a good finding. It is four pages of the best thinking any man has done in this county in my time and it is true, and I have read it twice."

"And then?"

"And then it is a finding about reading," said Pernel Quist, "and not a finding about a roll."

She said it in the flat voice she used for numbers, and she said it the way a woman says a thing she has examined for four years and is satisfied with, and every word of it was correct.

"A roll is not read. A roll is witnessed. Those are two different acts and only one of them is a thing a man can do to you at a distance, and when he does it to you, that is the county, and that is the law, and that is nineteen years and nine mistakes and eight of them dead and a room with forty-one shelves in it and a stove. And I will put a physician's finding about reading against a rollkeeper's finding about witnessing, on any day of the week, and I will put mine in the room, and it will be the right one to put in the room, because mine is about a roll and his is about a mark." She picked up the four tiles and stacked them. "And there is a thing underneath that I have had four years to look at and which I think he is not seeing, and I will say it to you because you will not repeat it. His finding would be excellent against this office. It is a very poor answer to the question he is actually asking, and the question he is actually asking is *how do I make a man stop reading a mark* and the answer to that is not to give him fewer marks. It is to give him marks he cannot get at. And there is not one thing in this room that I can do that with."

She looked at him then, and he understood that she had not said any of this in nineteen years, and that she had said all of it to a stranger within four minutes of his sitting down, and that both of those were true, and that the reason was not that he was a man she trusted.

It was that she had been rehearsing it on the walk to the table.

---

"So tell me what a struck person is," he said, "because from the shed yesterday I have a sentence and no sentence."

"A struck person is a person struck from the count."

"That is the whole of it?"

"That is the whole of what is done," said Pernel Quist. "It is not a punishment and it is not a sentence and it is not a list of anybody's faults, and I will tell you the thing about it that nobody in this county will say out loud, which is that a man can be struck from the count for being ill, and a man can be struck from the count for being absent, and a man can be struck from the count for being difficult, and there is no appeal because there is nothing to appeal against, and the count is the count."

"And the roll?"

"And the roll is the roll."

"You said a person is struck from the count. Not from the roll."

"I said exactly what I said," she said, "and I have said it nineteen times a year for eleven months and I will say it again this afternoon, and I would like you to notice that the reason I can say it nineteen times a year is that it is a thing about a figure, and a figure is not a person, and if you ask me in four days' time what the difference is I will not be able to tell you, and neither will the magistrate, and neither will the man with the ledger, and it has never once occurred to any of us to ask, because the two words are the same length and they sit next to each other on the same page and a person who has to look at both of them to see which one he wants has not got a job."

He tried it four ways over the next two days, in a long room with three windows and forty-one shelves, and she gave him four true answers and not one of them was the difference.

He asked her, on the second day, what a struck person may not do. She gave him the five without being asked twice, and he wrote them on the back of a sheet because he had not been given a copy and had not asked for one: **cannot witness, cannot be witnessed, cannot be married into a hearth, cannot be given a name at a kiln, cannot be buried in this county's ground.** And he said: then where, and she said: north of the low water, in the peat, where there is no ground and no grave, and there is a marker driven in with the kiln mark on it and no name on it, because there is no name to put on it, and a man who puts a name on a marker in the peat puts a name in a book somewhere and there is a clerk two counties off who has never heard of him.

He asked her on the third day what a struck person is. She said: alive, and employed, and at a kiln four days in the week. He asked her what happens to a person who softens. She said: a person who softens cannot be witnessed by anybody, including themselves, and the law requires two witnesses to stay on the roll, and so a person who softens comes off the roll, and nobody decides it, and nobody has to.

He said: "Does the magistrate know that?"

"The magistrate knows it," said Pernel Quist, "and it is the reason for the law, and it is the one thing in this county that is not hidden and I will tell you why it is not hidden, because it is the only piece of it I am proud of." She put her hand flat on the long table. "There is no step in this. A man does not decide. A clerk does not decide. I do not decide and I could not if I wanted to, because the only instrument I have is a column with *witnessed* at the top of it and I have never once entered a man in that column without two people standing in front of me and giving their names, and if one of them cannot give his name then I write down that he could not, and the law does the rest of it by itself while I am writing. That is the best thing about this office. I have never had to be the one."

"You have had to be the one about a hundred and ninety times in eleven months."

"Yes," she said. "And I will tell you what that was. It was a list with names on it, brought to me by a magistrate with three signatures at the foot of it, and I entered the names in a column headed *struck* and I did not choose them and I could not unchoose them, and I would do it again this afternoon, and I have read every one of those two hundred entries in this room since I made them and I know what two hundred people are worth and I have not been asked to put that in a column and I have not put it in a column."

---

He got the tile count out of her on the third day, the twenty-fourth day of the rolling, and it took about a minute, because she has it in a hand that is not a book.

"Two thousand nine hundred and six."

"And how many people?"

"Nine hundred and forty-one hearths," said Pernel Quist, "which is the county's figure, and the county's figure is the fire and not the person, and it has been the fire since the roll was begun and there is nobody left alive who remembers it being anything else."

"There are two thousand four hundred and fifty people in this county," Aren Kest said, "said out loud on the first morning in a kiln shed by the only man who had it, and there are two thousand nine hundred and six tiles on your roll of persons, and that is four hundred and fifty-six, and the roll has never had the people in it and there is nowhere on this shelf to put them."

Pernel Quist went along the shelves. She went the whole way along them, all forty-one, with a lamp in her hand, at twenty past the fifth hour, and she ran a finger along the label-slots, and she came back to the table and sat down and said:

"There is no column for it. I have looked. I have looked twice and both times I was looking for something else, and I have not looked a third time in nineteen years, because a woman who goes looking for a thing she is going to find does not find it as a searcher. She finds it as a person who wanted it. And when that happens the thing that goes in the column is a thing a person wanted, and not a thing a person counted."

"Then you have known the number of people in this county was missing for as long as you have been in the room."

"I have known there was no column for it for nineteen years," she said. "I did not know what the number was until a man from a county that is not on any map said it out loud in a kiln shed in front of nine people, and one of the nine was a man on the list, and he has been on the list eleven months, and his mother has been fired twice." She squared the account paper on the table. "And I would like it noted in this room, in no book, that I did not know, and that if you had come here on Friday instead of Monday I would have told you nine hundred and forty-one and been right, and that a man who has been right for nineteen years is a harder thing to be around than a man who has been wrong once."

---

At about the seventh hour she put the four tiles back in their box and shut the lid, and then she said the thing that he carried out of the assizes and wrote in the back of the notebook on the same evening.

"You asked me on your first morning what a struck person is, and I told you they are struck from the count, and you have asked me every day since what the difference is between the count and the roll, and here is the best argument I have got, and I have had four days to look for a better one, and this is the best one."

"Go on."

"The tile is the account of a person that does not need them," she said. "A person can be asked a question. A person can refuse the answer. A person can lie, and can change what they said, and can be cross, and can be ill, and can be nobody at home on the day you come. And the tile is fired, and it is in this room, and it says the thing whether the person is in front of you or not. And that is not a claim about them and it is not a punishment and it is not a judgement, and it is the only reason there is a county at all. Four years ago a thing was taken out of this county and we lost the register, and it was decided then, by people in this room who are dead, that we would fire everything, so that the next time, if there was a next time, we would have written it down, and I have thought about that decision every day for four years and I have not once found the seam in it, and it is the best argument I have, and I want you to have it from me before you have it from the kiln, because you are going to hear the other one very soon and the other one is shorter and it is in a drawer and it is four years old and it was written by a man who is alive and lives two streets from here and is not wrong."

"What's the other one?"

"He has not shown it to me since the first time," said Pernel Quist, "and I have not asked him for it, and if you have come here to be given the county's history you have come on a bad two days, and if you have come here to be useful you should know that in nineteen years nobody has ever come here to be useful. They come to be got at, and then they are got at, and then they go."
