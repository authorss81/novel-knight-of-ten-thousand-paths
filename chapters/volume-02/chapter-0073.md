# Chapter 0073

The twenty-first of Wolfmonth was a Thursday, and it was not a market day, and the moot hall in Merefen has no market in it on a Thursday, and about eleven people came in and went out between the second hour and the fourth.

Lysa Fenn had been in that room for nine years and had never once had a form in it that she could not answer, and on the twenty-first of Wolfmonth there was one on her table that she could not answer and could not refuse and could not pass on, and it had been on her desk since the fifteenth of Greenmonth without an answer on it, and it was going to expire in eighteen days, and she was frightened of it, and she was frightened of it alone in the room, at about the third hour, with the stove going and the clerk out at the other end of the room with his back to her on purpose, which is a thing a clerk does when he has been told to.

She wrote down what she was frightened of, in her own book, before he came, and she did not improve it afterwards.

*I am frightened of three things and two of them are about the same thing.*

*One. I have no instrument. I have a finding of the fourth of Greenmonth with my name at the bottom of it and five clauses, and a finding is not a weapon, it is a notice, and the only clause of the five that has ever cost that office anything is the fifth, and the fifth says that any office thereafter acting in the matter is on notice that this court asked and was not answered, and that the asking is dated. That office has been putting paper at this court since the ninth of Longlight and it has not once read the fifth clause, and it is not going to, and there is nothing I can do about a clause nobody reads.*

*Two. That clause is the only reason I have, and it is a reason that requires somebody else to look, and this county has one man in it who reads forms for a living and he is a registrar of sixty-one who sold a book to this office in Greenmonth for a shield, and so the number of people in this county who will read the fifth clause is a man who has been bought.*

*Three, and this is the one I am actually frightened of, and I am writing it first because if I write the other two first I will talk myself out of it. **There is a county, somewhere, that a person could not be served in, and I have known about it since the ninth of Fallowmonth and I have not written it down anywhere and neither has he.** And this paper on my table does not name a person. It wants a number. And a number is not refused by saying there is no person, because a number is a fact about a place, and I have just worked out in about nine minutes that I cannot stop this by anything a person in this county does, and that the only thing left is the shape of the paper itself.*

That last paragraph was the good one and she did not know it yet, and it took her another half hour and a man coming into the room.

---

She had sent for Aren Kest at the second hour, and he came at about the half hour, and he came in wet, because the twenty-first of Wolfmonth was the one day in that month with rain in it, and he came in with his coat already open and stood about four feet into the room and stopped, which is a thing a man does in a magistrate's room and cannot help.

"Sit down," said Lysa Fenn. "That chair. The other one has a leg out of true and I have had it eleven years."

"You told Mara Vey's office that."

"I told the clerk of this court that, and the clerk of this court repeated it to a copyist in a filing room, and I have spent nine years finding out that a magistrate says things out loud in a room and the room is not the room she said them in. Sit down."

He sat down.

"I want your account of the ninth of Wolfmonth," she said, "and I want it in the words you used in the yard and not the words you would use in here, and I am asking you as a person who was served with a paper and not as a witness, and there is no difference, and I am going to be told there is by somebody in about four years."

"The ninth of Wolfmonth was a market day," said Aren Kest. "A man came up Weir Street on foot with a leather bag at about ten minutes to four. He was not old and he was not young. He had a good coat that had been rained on. The form was one sheet, of the same series as the schedule and the dated one from the third of Embermonth, and it was a ruled table and it wanted a county, and then the hundred, and then a figure, and then under the figure four words: *how the figure was compiled.*"

"And a name?"

"There is no name on it anywhere, front or back. There is no box for one. I turned it over."

"And you answered it."

"I answered it in my brother's yard, in pencil, on the back of the week's wage sheet, and I read it out with four men in the gateway, and I entered a figure of nil against the figure, and the hour was the fourth, and the number of lines on the form was nine."

Lysa Fenn wrote none of it down.

"You did not write it down," he said.

"I am not writing it down because I have not yet worked out whether I am entitled to, and I have been wrong about that once," she said, "and the once was in a different county and cost a man his post. Tell me the last thing."

"The last thing he said was that I should not write *very good* in a book I cannot get at, because in four years there will be a clerk who will read a good opinion of me in a stranger's hand and he will not know what it is about and he will use it."

"That is the most use anybody has made of this office in eleven months," said Lysa Fenn, "and it was said in a gateway by a carpenter, and I am going to be thinking about it for a year, and I have not written it down, and I have written that I have not written it down, and I do that on purpose every time now because the not writing is the part that is a decision and I learned that in Fallowmonth from a woman who does it better than I do."

---

Then she turned the paper round and put it in front of him.

It was one sheet of the same series, printed, addressed to the county court at Merefen, and it asked the court to return a figure of the persons in this county who had had knowledge of a declared object, and the returnable date on it was the ninth of Frostmonth, and there was no name on it and no box for a name.

"Eighteen days," she said.

"You have eighteen days."

"I have eighteen days and I have no instrument and I am not going to answer it, and I do not know yet whether answering it is a defeat or a surrender, and I have spent this morning working out that there is a third thing and I have got it and I want you to hear it before I say it out loud to the clerk, because if I say it to the clerk it is a thing I have decided and if I say it to you first it is a thing I am trying."

"I am not a court."

"You are the only person in this county who has been served with a paper and been given a figure to put in it and has done the thing that this instrument is asking for by accident, and I want to say it to you first because if I am wrong I would rather be wrong to one person than to a room."

"Go on."

"A returnable paper has a party." She put her finger on the head of it. "That is the whole thing. A return is an answer. An answer is given by somebody. The office is entitled to know who has answered, and to proceed against whoever has not, and to enforce against a party in default, and every one of those powers is a power over a person or a body that can be named and can be sued and can be fined, and there is a court in this kingdom that will help the office with any of it, because I am the one, and I would have to help it, and I would have no lawful ground to refuse."

"This paper names no one."

"This paper names no one," said Lysa Fenn, "and it does not name a body either. It names a county. **A county is not a party.** A county is not a person. It cannot sue and it cannot be sued. It has no officer in this kingdom that I am aware of, and I have read the charter, the county list, and that office's own schedule, and if I am wrong about a county not being able to be a party, then somebody in that building is a great deal better informed than this court, and I have been wrong before."

"So the paper has no party."

"**The paper has no party. A returnable paper with no party has nobody who can be in default, and a paper with nobody in default at the end of its own stated period has not been answered and has not been refused, and it expires.** Not refused — refused is an act and an act can be appealed. Expired. It goes into a file as a thing that ran out. And the office will have to issue another one, because its purpose is not served by a paper nobody could answer, and the next one will have to do one of two things: it will name a person, or it will name an office."

"And if it names an office," said Aren Kest, "then the office has to exist."

"Then the office has to exist, in a schedule, with a keeper, and a county court that has searched the town charter, the county's list, and the Crown's schedule of provincial offices, and found no such office, and has said so in a finding with a date on it, in Greenmonth, with my name at the bottom of it in my own hand." She stopped. "Do you see the fifth clause."

"I see it," said Aren Kest. "Any office thereafter acting in the matter is on notice that this court asked and was not answered, and the asking is dated. The fourth of Greenmonth, or thereabouts, and I will not be more exact in a room."

"A finding of the fourth of Greenmonth," said Lysa Fenn, "and this paper is dated the twelfth of that month — it is a week after the asking, and it has been on this table since the fifteenth of that month, and it is three days on the road like everything else, and it is dated, and it is dated after the asking. **So the office is already on notice and does not know it, and if it issues a fresh instrument it will issue it on notice, and the notice is a document in this county with a date on it and a signature at the foot of it, and there is not one clerk in that building who has ever read the fifth clause of my finding because the fifth clause is on the second page and nobody in that office has ever had a reason to turn to the second page of a finding of a county court.**"

There was a fire in the stove and it ticked about eleven times.

"That is the whole of my morning," she said.

---

And then Lysa Fenn sat back in her chair and said the two things, in that order, and she was not proud of the order and she did not arrange it.

"I am delighted," she said.

"Ma'am—"

"Let me finish, and then I will be frightened and then you can go, and the order matters, and I have decided the order and I am telling you that I have decided it, because a woman who is frightened first and then delighted afterwards is a woman who has arranged her own feelings, and I would rather be delighted first and frightened afterwards and mean it."

She put both hands flat on the table.

"Nobody has ever beaten an instrument of that office with a form of that office's own making, in this court or anywhere else, and I have been a magistrate for nine years and I have read about forty of these things and I have never once found the answer in the paper. I found it in the paper in about nine minutes this morning, and it took nine minutes, and it is a thing a first-year clerk in that building could have found, and they have not found it in eleven months, and I would like a moment of that, and I am having it, and you are in the room while I have it and I am sorry."

"That is not the half I would have thought of."

"No. Here is the other half, and it is the half I actually brought you here for, and I have not said it in this room to a person and I am saying it to you, and I am not going to say it to the clerk at all." She straightened the sheet in front of him, which did not need it. "**I have just built the best answer in this county and I have built it out of the fact that this court is not a person.** That is the whole of the mechanism. There is no justice in it. There is no wrong in it. There is not one person in this county whose name was in that thing, and not one person is safer tomorrow than they were this morning, and I have beaten it, and I have beaten it by being an institution that cannot be fined. And in about four years somebody with a good head and a clean sheet is going to make this office a person, and a person can be a party, and a person can be in default, and a person can be fined, and a person can be shut, and then this answer of mine does not exist, and the instrument goes on exactly where it is."

"And you cannot stop that."

"I cannot stop that," said Lysa Fenn, "and I have been frightened of it since the fifth of Greenmonth and I have not written it down, and I am writing it down now because you are a man who writes things down and because a fact written down can be argued with and a fact held in a head can only be carried, and I have been carrying one for the best part of half a year and it is heavier than it was at the beginning of the rolling. So: in about four years somebody will make this office a body corporate, and the day they do it, this answer dies, and the answer that replaces it will be worse, because it will be worked out by a man in a hurry who wants to look as though a court had a position."

"And you will be dismissed."

"Or not," she said, "and that is the funny part and I have got quite attached to it. There is no procedure in that office for withdrawing anything, so an endorsement once given stands for ever; and there is no procedure in this court for dismissing a magistrate except a petition of the county, which takes two years and needs a majority; and a woman can be removed from an office by a man in a room by writing something, or she can be removed by forty households in a county by a petition, and the two are not the same thing and only one of them has ever happened to anybody I have read about. **So the answer is: make the office a person and you get a thing that can be closed in an afternoon. Leave it as it is and it is the county that has to turn me out, and that is a thing that takes a clerk to write and a market day to be argued on.**"

"I am not sure that is a victory."

"It is not a victory," said Lysa Fenn. "It is four years. I have nine of the other kind behind me and I am telling you honestly that I would take four more."

---

And then she said the thing she had not come to say, and she said it flatly, and she said it the way a woman says a thing she has said before.

"There is one more item on my desk and it is not this paper and it is not urgent, and I am going to do it now because you are in the room and because in nine years I would like there to be a witness to the fact that I did it while you were sitting there."

She turned to a second sheet on her desk and pushed it across.

"I received this on the eighteenth. It is in writing and it is signed and it asks this court to state whether it holds any record relating to a declared object in the matter of Alder Vale, in the year three hundred and one, in the matter of salvage."

"And you will not."

"I will not state the contents, I will not confirm the contents, I will not deny the contents, and I will not state whether a filing of that date exists," said Lysa Fenn, "and I am going to say the reason out loud so that there is a copy of the reason in this room, and the reason is that a filing of eleven years standing in that matter is the only account a certain person has of a certain thing, and this court is not going to be the office that tells a stranger it is there. A person is not a document and a document is not a person, and an office that cannot tell the difference will find out which one it has been running, and it will find out from a Crown's man in a leather bag."

"You told me on the second of Greenmonth," said Aren Kest, "in a room with a clerk in it, that you had an eleven-year-old salvage filing out of a filing room in YR three hundred and one and that you would not discuss it, and I said nothing."

"You said nothing," said Lysa Fenn, "and I had told you first, on purpose, before you could be in a room where it mattered, and that was the first of three times, and the second was in Greenmonth and the third was in Longlight, and I have told you three times in advance so that this morning, when it came, you would not be surprised, and you have not been surprised, and you have said nothing, twice, and both times correctly."

"I have had practice," he said.

"Then you will not mind me saying that this is the fourth time and that it is the last, and that if it comes a fifth time I will answer it, and I have told you now that I will answer it, in advance, so that the fifth time is not a surprise either." She wrote on the sheet: *No. The court holds nothing in that matter which it is willing to describe. The court does not deny that a record exists and does not admit that one does. This court declines to answer a question which can only be answered by disclosing a record of eleven years standing which is the property of a person and not of an office.* And beneath it, in the same hand: *The reason is stated on the file because the court declines questions in terms and does not decline them in silence, and a refusal without a reason is a thing a person has to remember.*

"Do not write the file number on this," he said.

"I am not writing the file number on anything," said Lysa Fenn. "If a person is ruined by a number, the number goes in the office and the person stays in the room."

---

The clerk came back at about the fourth hour and Lysa Fenn gave him the paper and told him what to write, and he wrote it in a flat unhurried hand and did not look up and did not improve it, and it is in the book at the Merefen county court in the ordinary way.

And Aren Kest sat in the chair with a leg out of true and watched a woman of about fifty do four things in an hour that he had not seen anybody do in five months.

She had found the answer to a paper in the shape of the paper and not in the law, and had said out loud, in the right order, what it was worth and what it would cost. She had refused a thing once, in writing, with a reason at the foot of it, after telling him three times in advance that it was coming, and had told him in advance that the fifth time would be answered, so that the answering would not be a surprise either. And she had done all of it without once being told to by anybody, and while frightened, and in that order after the first one.

He had been the best reader of a room in this case for about a year and a half and he had built most of what he knew about the trade on being that, and he had said out loud on the ninth of Embermonth, in a gateway in a clay county four hundred miles off, that he was not going to be a voice in a hat.

He watched her be both and understood — not was told, not concluded, understood, in about the length of a fire ticking eleven times — that this was what competent looked like when it was not him.

He did not say any of it. He was not going to say any of it, and he knew he was not going to, and the knowing was the whole of the thing, because a sentence said in this room at the fourth hour of the twenty-first of Wolfmonth would be in a file in nine years in somebody else's hand, and she had the finding and the paper and the refusal, and she had all of it, and she did not need him to tell her what it was.

He got up and put his coat on.

"Ma'am," he said. "The ninth of Frostmonth."

"The ninth of Frostmonth," said Lysa Fenn. "And I will tell you what is going to happen on the ninth of Frostmonth, and I am telling you now so that you cannot say in four years that I kept it. There is going to be an instrument from that office with a figure on it, in this county, and it is going to have a day on it, and it is not going to be the paper on my desk. It is going to be a figure, and a figure is not a writ, and a figure is not returnable, and a figure names nobody, and I will not be able to do what I did this morning, because this court cannot refuse a number without saying that a number is not a record, and if this court says that, then this court has said it, and it is in a book, and it is the one sentence I have got that I have never said."

"You think it is coming."

"I know it is coming, because they have stopped asking who and started asking how many, and how many is the cheapest instrument that office has ever had, and I have said that sentence out loud in this room this morning to a man who does not know what I know and cannot be told, and it went nowhere, and I am putting it in a book instead. If I am right it is the only entry I will ever make about the day I was frightened and got it right, and if I am wrong then it is a lie of about half a year, and I can live with half a year of a lie, and I could not live with not having written it down."

She wrote it down. It was four lines and it took her the length of a hymn and the clerk did not see it and it was in her own book and it was not in the court's book, and the difference between those two things is the difference between a magistrate and a woman, and she had been living in that difference for nine years.

"Go home," she said. "You have got about a fortnight and there is a bench in it and I have heard about it, and I am not going to ask you anything about it, and that is not modesty, and if you want to know what it is not, it is that I have been served with a form by an office that is doing its job and I have nine days to lose a woman of thirty-three in a provincial file and I have not got the room for a road."

The clerk said, at the end of the hall, on the stair, without turning round: "That was a good morning, ma'am."

"It was an ordinary morning," said Lysa Fenn, "and it was a good one, and I would like the second of those two things entered and the first of them not."
