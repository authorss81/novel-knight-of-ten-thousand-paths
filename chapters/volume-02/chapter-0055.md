# Chapter 0055

The endorsement came on the fifth of Embermonth at about the ninth hour, and it came by the county post in a grey wrapper with a seal on it, and it was four lines long, and it was printed on the back of a sheet that Aren Kest had read in a gateway four days earlier.

*Endorsement of record, R.C. series, reverse.*

*Where an officer of a Crown registry has been shown to hold a volume entered upon a press register and not upon the shelf, and has disclosed the same of his own motion, the office will endorse the record so made and will discharge any process depending upon the absence of the volume; and the endorsement is not to be withdrawn, there being no procedure in this office for the withdrawal of an endorsement.*

*Discharges: the writ of enquiry in the matter of the records of the reeve of Merefen, returnable the ninth of Rainmonth. Number: 4 of the season. Office: marshal's emergency office.*

The endorsement was dated the fourth, which was the day before, and it was signed by an officer whose name Halven read and then put face down on the desk, and he read it twice, and the second time he read it with his thumb on the number.

"You have been busy," he said.

"I have been a form," said Halven.

---

He was sixty-one and he had been a registrar for thirty-four years and he had the manner of a man who prices things for a living and is not embarrassed about it, and he had come up the stair of the Bellweather Claims Archive on the Sunday morning of the fifth of Embermonth with the grey wrapper in his hand and a locked box under his arm, which he set down in the middle of the filing room floor, and unlocked, and showed her what was in it, because he wanted it in a room where a book was.

"I am going to tell you the trade first," he said, "because you will want to check it and I would rather you checked the trade than the story."

"Say it."

"A registrar who has been shown to hold a volume that is entered on a press register and is not on the shelf has committed an offence that has no name and no penalty and that a provincial court cannot try, because a press register is an internal record of a registry and not a public book, and because the Crown's own court of the province has no jurisdiction over its own clerks in a matter of its own housekeeping." He put his hands behind his back. "I have committed it. I entered that volume on the fourth press register in YR 302 in my own hand and I entered it again as put away in YR 309 in my own hand, and it was not put away, and I knew it was not put away, and I have said so once in this building, in a filing room, in Greenmonth, to a woman with a press, and she wrote it down and made a copyist attest it, and I have thought about that once a day for about eight months."

"And now somebody has offered you a legal shield for it," said Mara Vey.

"Somebody has offered me four lines on the back of a form for it," said Halven, "and I have been a registrar for thirty-four years and I know exactly what four lines on the back of a form is worth, and it is worth the writ, and the writ is the only thing in this matter that has ever had teeth in this county, and your office is a stone lighter for the thirtieth day of Fallowmonth."

He crouched and opened the box properly, and inside it was a book in a buckram that had been bound about a hundred and forty years ago and had been repaired twice, and there was a press-mark on the spine in white, and the press-mark was **press four, die eleven**, and Mara Vey looked at it for about a second and a half and then wrote the date and the hour at the top of a clean sheet, which is what she does.

"And you will want to know what is in it," said Halven, "because you are the only person in this county who will ever be able to say what is in it, and I have read it, and I have been sitting on it since Hearthmonth of the year before last, and I am sixty-one and I have a wife who is ill and I have not slept properly about a shelf."

---

The book is called *Anchors, schedules and dispositions, Alder Vale*, and it is a schedule book, and schedules are dull in a way that is not accidental.

The first leaf he turned to had three bells on it.

*Alder Vale crossing. Bells: three.*

*One. Removed. No office against the removal.*
*Two. Removed. No office against the removal.*
*Three. RETAINED.*

"There," said Halven. "That is a hundred and forty years of a crossing in three lines, and two of them have been taken off a bridge in the last eleven years by people who have never been able to say who told them to, and the third is a bell that is standing on a parapet four hundred miles from here with a band on it that this town cut in Fallowmonth, and it has been retained for a hundred and forty years by an office that has never been asked by anybody what retaining it means."

"Then it is a record of the office's own intention," said Mara Vey, "and the office's intention is not a lawful ground and a registrar is not a person who gets to decide what a county keeps on a bridge."

"I am aware," said Halven, "and I have never once said it out loud in a room with a clerk in it, and I have said a great deal in rooms with no clerk in them, and that is the whole of my moral position at sixty-one and it took me thirty-four years and a mistake."

He turned the leaf, and the second leaf was the one that is the whole of this volume, and he held it up by the edge and did not hand it over, and she copied it standing up, in the fair hand, in nine minutes, and the room was quiet enough to hear the pen.

The heading was *Dispositions, by county, of scheduled crossings and dependent places*, and there was a column headed *county* and a column headed *disposition* and a column headed *date*, and the county column ran for nine lines of the page and then went on, and Halven put his thumb on the top of the column and moved it down.

"Read the county column," he said. "Not the dispositions. I will give you the dispositions when I have to. Read the counties."

She read the county column, twice, because the second time she was looking for a shape and not for names.

Alder Vale. And then eleven others.

"Ash," she said.

"Ash," said Halven. "I have had that word in my head since I copied that column out for myself in Hearthmonth of the year before last, and I have looked for it ever since, and I have never found it on a map in this kingdom, and I have asked four people in this trade and three of them have never heard of it and one of them told me that there is a river called the Ash in the west and that it is not a county and that I should stop asking."

"And the disposition against it."

"Read it out."

She read it out.

"**FIRED.**"

"Yes."

"And that is the whole of the entry. A county, a word, and a date, and the date is the fifteenth of Greenmonth, YR 305, which is four years after the writ at Alder Vale, and the office is not against it and the person is not against it, and the word is the whole of it."

"FIRED," said Mara Vey. "That is a kiln word. That is what you do to a brick in this county. My grandfather fired bricks and there is a kiln on the Sable that has a firing on it on a board in the yard and they write FIRED on the board when the kiln is cold."

"Yes," said Halven. "And I have sat here for about a minute hoping you would say that, because I have been hoping somebody would say that for two years, and you have said it, and I am going to hand this book to a man from an office in about an hour and go home and I would like it on the record that the word was read out in this room as a word about bricks by the keeper of this office and by me, and that neither of us knew, and that I want both of those things written down, including the part where I did not know."

---

She wrote it down, both halves, with the hour.

"Eight columns," she said, going over the sheet. "Eight counties and a word. You have read this whole book."

"I have read this leaf eleven times and I have not read the book, because I am a man of sixty-one and it is four hundred leaves and I do not speak the county names." He crouched again at the box. "The endorsement came on Saturday. The man from the office came with it. He is in the lane at this moment with a cart and a locked box and a receipt for the box and not for the contents, which is the correct form and which I made him use, because a receipt for the contents is a receipt for the thing and a receipt for the box is a receipt for a box, and the day somebody asks me where the book went I am going to be able to say that a man took a box out of this room and that the contents of the box were a matter of my office and not of the room."

"You have already sold it," said Mara Vey.

"I sold it on Saturday at about the eleventh hour for four shillings and an endorsement with a number on it, and the four shillings are in my own pocket and not in this office, and I am telling you that because you will find it out otherwise and I would rather you found it out from me in a room with a book in it."

---

And then he did the thing that made him viable for another nine volumes, and he did it standing up, and he did it without being asked, and he asked for it in a form.

"I want to disclose this," he said. "I want it disclosed, today, in this office, on the office's paper, with the number of this office on it, and I want it to say that I offered the volume and that it was taken and that I disclosed it of my own motion and not on a fee, and that the endorsement was the consideration. I want every one of those five things in it and I want the word *fee* in it, because the word *fee* is the word that will keep me from being a man who was paid, and I am sixty-one, and the reason is not that I am a good man. The reason is that I have read that book four hundred leaves back and front over nine years and I have known since Hearthmonth of the year before last what was in the ninth line of a schedule, and I could not get anybody to read it, and I have had a bad winter and my wife is ill and I have nobody in this trade to say it to, and there is a woman in this room who will write down whatever I say and will not improve it."

"Why?" said Mara Vey. "You had it for eleven years. You disclosed the gap in Hearthmonth and nobody came. Why now?"

"Because in Longlight a woman asked for a shield for this office and refused it in a room with a clerk in it, and on the ninth of Longlight a clerk wrote a phrase into a printed space on the back of a returned paper in order to refuse the paper properly, and the phrase is the office this man has spent thirty-four years inside of, and it got into a county file for nothing." He nodded at the floor, at the box. "And on the twenty-seventh of Fallowmonth a marshal offered the same shield to the road warden of this county, and the road warden said no in ninety seconds in a room with a clerk in it, and the record of the refusal is in the same file as the writ this endorsement is about to discharge. So it is in the same county file. It is in the same county file as this endorsement, on the same page, in the same book, because the file is a file. And when some clerk opens that file in about four years for any reason at all, they will find the marshal's shield taken by one man and refused by the other, and there will be a number on the endorsement, and they will look up the number."

He straightened up.

"And when they look up the number they will find a man who took it and wrote down that he took it, and that is the only thing I have got, and I am aware that it is a small thing, and I have been a man in an office for thirty-four years and it is the first thing I have ever done that is a small thing."

---

Mara Vey wrote the disclosure in the fair hand and read it back and made him listen to it, and then she read back the five things and made him hear those too, and he corrected one of them, which was that the consideration was not four shillings and an endorsement, it was **the endorsement**, and the four shillings were a fee for the box and for a morning's work and were nothing.

"That is a different sentence and it is a better one," she said, "and it is also true, and I am going to enter it as you have said it, and I am going to enter that I asked you which of the two it was, and that you said the second, and that you did it in about a second, which means you had it ready, which is not a thing I am going to enter and which I am going to think about for a year."

She entered the disclosure, and the hour, and the five things, and then she wrote one line of her own under it, which is the second rule of the office, and which is that no combined figure is entered in this office:

*Entered on the office's own account, against no person, at the tenth hour of the fifth of Embermonth, YR 312, on the disclosure of the registrar of the provincial registry, made by him without request: that a volume entered on the press register of the fourth press and not upon the shelf was offered by him to the Office of Provincial Registration and was taken by that office on the fourth of Embermonth, YR 312, in consideration of an endorsement numbered 4 of the season, which endorsement discharges the writ of enquiry in the matter of the records of the reeve of Merefen and cannot be withdrawn, there being no procedure in that office for the withdrawal of an endorsement; and that the volume went out of this county on the day in a locked box, under a receipt for the box, and there is no receipt in this county; and that the office holds a copy of one leaf of it made in the fair hand in nine minutes in this room with the volume present, and not the volume.*

*And that this office has no opinion on whether the disclosure was made of his own motion and it is entered as his statement and not as the observation of this office, and that the office has no power to compel him to make it and no power to protect him from having made it, and that a document held by a person is a thing that person can be destroyed with and this office is entered in four years and a half and has learned that the worst thing that has ever happened to a record in this business happened to a person.*

Then she wrote the one line the volume turns on, which is not evidence and which is going to be in about nine years read by somebody who does not know what it means.

*And that the said copy of one leaf carries a column headed county, and eleven counties, and eight of them are counties this office has no knowledge of and one of them is a county called Ash of which this office holds a second paper in the day-book, received on the first of Fallowmonth, from a road that was not asked for, in a hand that is not a clerk's hand; and that a word in the disposition column against that county is FIRED; and that the word was read out in this room at about the eleventh hour of this day as a word about bricks by the keeper of this office and by the registrar, and that neither of them knew what it was, and that both of them have said so in the record, and that the county which does know is four hundred miles off in a direction this county has no map for, and has a roll of persons of nine hundred and forty-one hearths, and does not know it has ever been written down.*

---

At the second hour of the afternoon a man put a locked box on a cart in a lane in Merefen and drove out of the town, and the box was on the cart at about the fourth hour of the third of Wolfmonth on a shelf in an office two hundred miles off, on an entry in a shelf register that had a date and a name against it, both of them new, and both of them the first new entries on that shelf in eleven years.

And a woman in a filing room over a weigh-house put a sheet of nine minutes' copying into a press, and it is the first document in this business that has a county in it that no court in this kingdom has ever had before, and she put the hour on it, and she did not total it with anything.
