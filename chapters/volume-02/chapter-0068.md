# Chapter 0068

They had all four of them in the room with the stove on the sixteenth day, which was the thirty-seventh day of the rolling, and that had not happened before and it was not going to happen again, and the reason it had not happened before was that a magistrate's room holds a magistrate and a clerk and a rollkeeper when there is a press to open, and it had not held a physician since a man called Ostrand was alive.

The clerk was at the end of the table with the pen. Pernel Quist had come with a box of tiles under her arm, because where a rollkeeper goes she goes with the roll or a part of it, and she had a long sheet out of the box and it was on the table inside out up so that nobody would see what was on it, which is a thing a keeper does when she is in a room she has not chosen.

Hesper Lound sat with his hat on his knee, which he had done in nineteen years of this county without once being asked to.

Colm Arreld had the four pages of the finding on the table in front of him, and he had them squared, and he had them squared wrong — he had squared them to the table and not to each other, so the top one was about a finger's width out, and Aren Kest looked at that for about four seconds and did not say anything about it, because a man who corrects a magistrate's stationery on his first morning in a room is a man who has come in to be a clerk.

"You have read it," said Arreld.

"I have read it twice," said Hesper Lound, "and I have corrected it in two places, and both corrections are in my own hand on the copy that is in your drawer, and if you have copied it out you will have the errors and not the corrections."

"Then I have the errors," said Arreld, "and I have had them for a week, and I have had them copied out twice, and I want you to hear what I have made of them before I say anything else, because I have made something of them and I would like to know whether I have made it right."

---

"You and I have said the same thing," said Colm Arreld, "in two languages, for four years, and neither of us has known it, and I have got a witness to it now and it is a man with a sett-cutter's bag who does not belong to either of us."

The fire ticked.

"You wrote: the softening enters by the mark, and it travels fastest along the marks that are read most, and the marks that are read most are the roll marks, because a clerk reads the roll every morning." He put his hand flat on the four pages. "I wrote a column of numbers in a copying book and a column of numbers in a second one, and my column says forty-one in four years and a hundred and twelve in eleven months, and the difference between those two columns is the difference between a county with a list in it and a county without, and the difference between my two columns is a list. So we are both saying that the county has got a mark and the mark is being read. Yours is a physician saying it in the third year and putting it in a drawer. Mine is a magistrate saying it in a list of two hundred and six names under four lines with three signatures at the foot of it, and mine has a law attached to it, and a law has a day in it, and your four pages do not have a day in them, and that is the whole of the difference between us and it is not a difference in what we think."

Nobody in the room said anything.

"I am not going to insult you," said Arreld, to the physician, and he said it in the voice of a man who is not going to insult him and is going to be worse. "Four years is a long time to have said it and not said it. I do not think that is a fault. I think a man who has a physician's finding in a drawer for four years has been waiting for a room with a table in it, and I have not had one, and that is my fault and I have had four years to have it and I have not, and I am not going to be a better man about it than you have been about yours. What I am going to do is put the two together, and I am going to do it in a fortnight when this list is a year old, and I am going to do it in public, and I am going to say the words *on the advice of the physician to this county*, and that is a thing a magistrate is allowed to say and it is the first time in four years that anybody in this county has said either of those words about anything."

"The cure is not fewer records," said Hesper Lound.

"The cure is not fewer records for a *person*," said Arreld, "and I have never once in nine years proposed to take a person off this county's roll for a fever, and the physician will tell you the same and he did tell me so in the first year, and I have not forgotten it, and it is the reason this is a list of persons to be struck from the count and not a list of persons to be killed, and I know how that sounds and I have heard it and I have a copy of the four lines in a drawer of my own and they say *the clerk of the roll shall destroy the tiles of every person so named, and shall enter the destruction in the roll, and not the name*, and the man who put that in is dead and I have read it four hundred times and it is the only sentence in this county I would put on a stone."

He put the four pages down and squared them — wrong again, the same way — and said:

"So: fewer marks, and not one person fewer. That is the reading of your finding I have taken, and I would like the man who came out of the road to tell me whether I have read it right, because he is the only person in this room who has nothing to lose by telling me."

And it is the second time, and it was the correct time, and it was the worst of the three, and it was not asked to be polite. It was asked by a decent man who wanted his instrument to have a physician's name on it, and who had found one, and who was handing a stranger the chance to put a second name on it.

---

Aren Kest looked at the four pages on the table, and at the column of figures Arreld had put beside them, and at the three signatures at the foot of four lines in a folder, and he had a very clear idea for about two seconds of what the whole of the next four years in this county was going to be.

He was going to be in a file. Not his name — he would be a man who came out of a road, and a county of clay does not put a road in a file; but the sentence he said in this room on the sixteenth day of a rolling, and the date, and the four men who heard it, and there would be a file in four years, and the file would be opened by a clerk in a room over something, and the sentence would be read, and it would be the sentence. That was the cost. He knew it exactly and he knew it in about two seconds and he had had two seconds about it four times in eleven years and every one of the four times he had been right.

And the sentence he would have said was *you are burning the wrong thing, and the record is not the disease, the reading of the record is the disease, and what you are about to do is fewer records, and fewer records is what his fourth page says cannot work.*

And the sentence was right, and the magistrate was right, and the physician was right, and a county with a fever in it was about to destroy two hundred and six fired tiles in a market square, and a man who could add, in a minute and a half, in a kiln shed, on the first day of a rolling, was not going to be able to stop it with a sentence, and would have wanted to stop it with a sentence, and every sentence he said in this room was a thing a magistrate could pick up and put on a fire.

So he did not say it.

"I have been asked three times in sixteen days," he said, "what I think. Twice by people who wanted to be helped by the answer. This is the third and it is the best of the three and it is the one I am going to say no to, and I am going to say no to it in a way that costs me the only thing I have got in this county, and I want that in the room while it is happening."

Nobody stopped him.

"In a fortnight, when you burn that list, two hundred and six people will lose the only account of themselves that exists in the world. Not the only account anybody likes. The only one. Their hand is pressed in the back of those tiles in wet clay by their own hand and there is nothing else anywhere that anybody can go and look at, and in a year the soft ones will have gone soft in the houses and in nine years there will be a fire in Kilnholt with nothing in the account of it, and you have described that to me yourself in this room and you were right to describe it and it is the worst thing I have heard a decent man say with a straight face.

"And I cannot tell this room not to do it. I want to be very careful about that, because if I tell this room not to burn that list, I am telling a county with a fever in it to keep a list, on my word. **I am the man who puts lists into counties by arguing about them in front of witnesses.** I have been doing it for eleven years and I did it in a hall in a fen in the fourth month of this year and I talked four hundred men who had never heard of each other into a thing I wanted, and it worked, and there is a person in this case who will tell you in four years that the man off the road did it. I am not going to be a reason for anything in this county. I am not going to be a voice. And I am not going to do it in a hat."

The fire ticked for about eleven seconds.

"So I am going to say the one thing I am allowed to say, and then I am going to stop talking in rooms in this county, and the one thing is not an opinion and it is not advice and you may put it in a drawer with his."

---

"**I am asking you for seven days**," said Aren Kest.

And the asking was the whole of it, and it was the first time in nineteen years that he had asked a room for anything, and the difference between asking and telling is the difference between a thing a community can refuse and a thing it must obey, and he had spent five months getting good at the second one.

"Seven days, and here is the whole of what I would do with them, and it is not enough, and I am telling you that in this room before you agree to it, because a man who asks for a week and does not say what is wrong with the week is a man buying something."

"One," he said. "The list is not burned on the day it is a year. It goes back into the press with a week on it instead of a day, and no law in this county cares about a week, and a man who says in nine years that there was never such a list is a man who is wrong by a week, and a man who is wrong by a week is a man anybody can say is wrong."

"Two. In the seven days, every one of the two hundred and six is asked, once, by somebody who is not the office and not the magistrate and not a clerk. In his own shed. By a person who works with him. He is asked whether he is on that list. And he is not asked whether he wants to be struck and he is not asked whether he wants to be put back, because a man who is asked what he wants is a man who has to know the answer, and a man who has to know the answer is a man who is being asked about the county instead of about himself.

"Three. The answers are written down by the man who asked, in his own hand, and they do not go into the roll and they do not go into any room in this building, and no name and no answer goes anywhere that anybody can produce it.

"Four. The tiles are counted at the kilns by the people who made them, in front of anybody who cares to stand and look, and anybody who cares to stand and look is a person who has not stood in a kiln shed in his life and would like to."

He stopped.

"That is the week. It is not a fix and I am not offering it as one. In seven days nobody in this county will be put back on the roll, because there is no column and I have said so in a room, and I have said it standing up. What there will be, is two hundred and six people who have been asked, once, by a person, and who have answered, and who have been written down by a person who is not an office."

---

"Three is wrong," said Hesper Lound.

"I know it is."

"It is wrong and I would like it written down that I said so in the room on the sixteenth day of the rolling and that the man who asked for the week took it as wrong and kept it, and that is the whole of the difference between a physician and a magistrate and it is the only thing I have ever been able to give this county." He put his hat on the table. "A mark that is not read cannot carry it, and an answer that is written down in a shed by a man and carried home in that man's pocket is a mark, and it will be read, and not this year and not by a clerk in a room but by the man who asked, at his own table, and then in his own household, because that is how a mark works, and a mark that is written down and not kept anywhere is a mark that is read more often and not less. You have asked this county to be asked, and being asked is a fire, and a fire does not stop being a fire because it is a good fire."

"Then it is a mistake."

"It is a *smaller* mistake than a fire in a market square," said the physician, "and I have been wrong about most things for nineteen years and I am not going to be right about this on the first time I have heard it, and I would like a week to think about it, which I am aware is the thing you have just asked this room for, and I am going to give it to you, and you should know that you have got it partly from the man who is going to tell you it is wrong."

Pernel Quist had not spoken for about a quarter of an hour and she was looking at the long sheet inside-out on the table, and now she put her hand on it.

"I keep the roll," she said. "Nineteen years. And I have never in nineteen years entered a thing that I could not produce, and I have built an office on the fact that a record a person can get at is the only kind worth having, and it is the whole of my trade and it is the whole of my life and it is the reason I have never once been frightened of a magistrate, because a magistrate can produce a thing from me and I can produce it from myself, and that is what makes us two sides of a thing instead of one man and a subject.

"You have asked me in this room to write down two hundred and six answers, in another man's hand, into a place I cannot get at and could not produce if a court sat here tomorrow. I have said no to the magistrate's four lines once in nine years and I said no in a corridor and I was not thanked for it. And I am going to say yes to you, and I am going to say it in this room, and I am not going to pretend to you that I think it is right, because it is not right and I want it written in whatever you have got that I said it was not right.

"A thing that cannot be produced is a thing that cannot be corrected. That is the whole of my argument and I have made it in that chair on the fourth day of this rolling, and you have heard it, and you have not improved on it, because it is not yours to improve on. It is not a roll. It is a man with a pen and a bad memory and a good heart, and in four years he will be dead or moved or tired, and then the thing is in nobody's hands at all, and the county will have spent a week on it and got a week."

"Then say no," said Aren Kest.

"No," said Pernel Quist.

---

Colm Arreld had not moved. He had the four pages in front of him and the two columns of figures beside them and he had been listening to a physician and a keeper of records tear a thing apart in his own room for about twenty minutes, and he had let them, because a magistrate who interrupts a room is a magistrate who is afraid of what the room will finish saying.

"Seven days," he said.

"Magistrate—"

"Seven days, and not one day more, and I will tell you why, and I have thought about nothing else since the sixteenth of the last rolling and I have got three reasons and two of them are wages and I am going to say the wages out loud because a man who says the wages out loud is a man who is not selling you the other thing."

He turned the sheet over and put his finger on it.

"One. The rolling ends in a fortnight by my count and three by the kiln-hands'. To break two hundred and six fired tiles and wedge the clay back is a day and a half of a wedging shed, and I have one wedging shed and it is running on the spring digging, and if I take it on the seventeenth the kiln-hands lose the last day of the rolling and about forty men lose a day of a week's wage, and a magistrate who costs this county the last day of a rolling and forty men's day of wages is a magistrate who will be asked about it in nine years by a man who is alive and has nothing else to do.

"Two. The carts for the digging come in on a schedule and the schedule is not mine, and the last two winters were the worst this county has had and I am not spending the wagons of a bad spring on an afternoon in a market square.

"Three." He put his hand flat on the four pages. "A column of soft people in this county does not turn inside a fortnight. It has not turned in a year. If I burn the list on the seventeenth and I am wrong, then I have burned two hundred and six tiles and the column is still going up, and a magistrate who has done that is finished. If I wait seven days and the column turns, then I have not needed to burn anything, and I shall have a physician's four pages and a county that counted two hundred and six answers, and I shall have kept a thing I could not put back. **I would rather be a man who waited and was wrong than a man who was right and could not do it again.** That is my reason. It is the wages twice and the wagons once and I have said all three and I would like the clerk to write all three, and I have never in nine years asked that clerk to write down a reason of mine that I did not have to give."

The clerk wrote all three down. He wrote them in a flat unhurried hand, and he did not look up, and he did not improve them, and it was the first time in nine years that anything a magistrate had said in that room had gone into the book as a reason and not as a decision.

"A week," said Aren Kest, "and not one day more."

"A week," said Arreld. "And the clerk of the roll will tell me at the end of it whether she will break the tiles, and I will ask her in my own room and not through a man off a road, and if she says no then that is the end of the matter and there is no second instrument in this county and I shall have to do it myself and out of my own office, and I shall do it badly and I shall sleep."

Pernel Quist said, "I have not said no to him yet."

"You have said no to him twice, which is not the same as saying it to me, and I know the difference, and I have been in this room nine years," said Arreld, "and I am going to ask you in it, and I am going to ask you now, and I am asking you in front of a physician and a stranger and a clerk and not in a corridor, because the last time I asked you something in a corridor it took you a fortnight and cost me a fortnight, and I have learned one thing in nine years and it is that everything I have ever done in a corridor was worse."

"That is the first sensible thing you have said to me in a year," said the keeper of the roll, "and I am not going to answer you now, and you are not to take now as a no."

"I am not taking now as anything at all," said Colm Arreld. "I am taking now as a week, which is what the man off the road asked for, and I have given it to him and not to you, and I would like that minuted in those words, because in nine years one of us is going to need it."

---

Aren Kest walked out of the assizes at about the eleventh hour into a street where the digging was going on in earnest, and he was alone for about two hundred yards, and then a woman in a leather apron came across the road out of a gateway and put herself in his way at the kerb, because she had seen him come out of that building and she wanted to know.

"You were in there an hour and a half," she said.

"Yes."

"You asked him for something."

"Seven days."

The hand of the Ninebridge looked at him for about three seconds, and then she said the thing that is the whole of what happened to him in this county in twenty-three days.

"Seven days," she said, "and I have been in this county forty-one years and the last man who asked this county for seven days was a man who came off a road about nine years ago and got them, and he used them to get a list out of the clerk of the roll and put it in a room in another county, and there is a woman there now who is called something that is not a thing she is called here, and I know that because a man came through in the autumn with a roll and I asked him a question and he did not answer it, and a county that has a clerk's name in another county's room is a county that has a hand in another county's room.

"So I will ask you the same question I asked on the bridge and I will ask it the same way, because I ask everybody the same way, and you have not answered it once in twenty days and I have not asked you once, and I am asking you now because you have just asked this county for time and time is the only thing in this county anybody ever wants from me.

"**Are you the one who sends people?**"
