# Chapter 0075

The ninth of Frostmonth was a Monday and a market day, and the election of the officers of the Merefen bridge guild was done at the fourth hour in the hall over the wharf, and for four hundred years it had not been done that way.

A guild in this county appoints. A guild has always appointed: the reeve names the warden and the warden takes it, and the bench is told afterwards, in the same minute, with the hour on it, because the custom is older than the town charter and nobody in a fen town breaks a custom on a Monday. What the guild did on the ninth of Frostmonth was vote, and the reason it voted was a column.

The roll of the guild is a sheet of vellum on a frame, nineteen hands long, and it has four columns, and the third of them was put in three months ago, and the heading at the top of it is a single word, and the word is *Keeper.*

A roll in this town has a *Keeper* column on it now and it is there because a form came down from two hundred miles off with a column with the same word at the top of it, and the town is a town that keeps what it is given, and about eleven people said that was harmless.

It was not harmless. A column headed *Keeper* wants somebody against it. That is all a column wants. It does not want an argument; it wants a hand, and it wants it in the column, and a bench that has a column like that on its own roll and appoints its officers in private has a form and an answer that do not match, and there is a clerk two hundred miles off who has been keeping a column like that for eleven months and has had it filled by nobody at all.

So on the ninth of Frostmonth the bench voted, and it was the first time in eleven years that anybody in that room had been put in an office by a vote rather than appointed, and the guild clerk said so out loud when he opened the book, and the reeve said, "Enter the reason, and enter that the reason is a column," and the clerk entered it.

---

The reeve first.

The reeve of Merefen was Aldis Fenn and had been the reeve for nine years, and the business of the reeve of a fen town is the town's witness, and the whole of the room knew it, and the whole of the room had known it for nine years.

"Do I put the reeve's name," said the guild clerk, "and the bench will vote on the reeve, and the reeve will—"

"I will not vote on my own matter or on this guild's matter," said Reeve Aldis Fenn, "and I have a casting vote when the bench is level and a casting vote is not a vote, and I have said that sentence in this hall every year for nine years and I have said it in the same words every year because a woman who improves her own reasons is a woman who has noticed that she is being asked twice."

The clerk put her name and read it back.

Eight hands. No casting vote needed, because there was nothing to cast.

"Against?"

Silence.

The clerk looked at the room and then at the bench-clerk, and the bench-clerk said, "There is no vote against. There is no vote against and no abstention and I am entering the word *none* against *against* and I am entering that I have written the word *none* and that this is the first time in the roll that it has been written, and I would like the bench to understand that I have been a clerk for nineteen years and I have never once had nothing to write in that column."

The minute went in and it was the shortest in the roll, and it read:

*Ninth of Frostmonth. Reeve elected, eight hands. The reeve did not vote on her own election. Reason entered.*

Nobody read it back out.

---

Then the roll.

"Before the warden," said the guild clerk. "The roll. There is a name for it and I have been asked to read it twice and the second time is now."

The bench of the Merefen bridge guild carries, on its roll, the names of the men of this town who may hold an office in it and vote on its matter. It had thirty-one names on it in Fallowmonth. It had thirty-one names on it on the ninth of Frostmonth until about a quarter past four, and it had thirty-two by half past, and the thirty-second was the reason the count in Chapter 76 of this business was wrong, and nobody in the hall knew that yet, and neither did he.

"Forty-seven years old," said the guild clerk. "In this county nineteen years. Sergeant of the watch, and then not. He asks to be on the roll."

"What has he done to the guild," said the foundry's man.

"He has stood in a gateway once," said the guild clerk, "at the fourth hour of a market morning in the fourth month, as a witness at this end of a road, for fourpence a week paid out of a private box, which he has not drawn, and which he says in the yard's book is not to be drawn, and the yard's book has it with the hour on it and not one word of the reason in it, and that is four months old now and it has not been improved on and I would like it minuted that it has not been improved on."

"Put it to the room," said the reeve.

Bram Ottery was standing at the back by the door, in a coat that had been good in Greenmonth.

"I want to be on the roll," he said, "because I have been nineteen years in this county and I have got no office in it and no rate in it and I have not been thanked for one hour of anything since the fourth of Greenmonth, and I am not asking for the office. **I am asking for the vote.** There is a thing in this county that is coming, and I have watched it come, and it is going to be decided in rooms like this one by people who have a vote, and I would like to be one of them while I can still hold a pen."

"That is a good reason," said the foundry's man, "and I am against it."

"Why."

"Because you are asking for a vote and you have given a reason in a gateway that four men in a yard have and this hall has not, and I do not object to a man wanting a vote. I object to a man being given a vote for a speech." He looked round. "Who is against. Hands."

Four hands against and four for and one abstaining, which was the man at the fish weir, who said, "I am not against the man. I will not vote on a man I have never worked with, and the reeve can tell this room why that is the rule in this hall, and she has said it out loud twice and I have never once improved on it."

The reeve's casting vote carried it.

"Carried," said the guild clerk. "Bram Ottery of Merefen is on the roll of this guild, and he has a vote on this guild's matter from this day, and the roll stands at thirty-two."

And that was the moment, and no single person in the hall knew it was the moment, and it was entered in two books four hundred miles apart and in neither of them as a moment.

On the fourth of Embermonth, at the fourth hour of a market morning, Reeve Aldis Fenn had asked a hall of forty how many persons in this county could say no to the Old Toll crossing and be believed and go on living in this town afterwards, and the answer had been **eleven**, and the eleven had been read out with a date and an hour on it and had been entered in a day-book against no person and had not gone on the guild roll, because a roll in that town had a *Keeper* column on it now.

On the ninth of Frostmonth, the count in that day-book was read out again in that hall, because the reeve reads it at every election now and nobody has ever asked her to stop, and it was **eleven**, and it was eleven again, and one of the eleven was in the room.

And then, about a minute later, it was **twelve.**

A count of how many persons in this county can say no to a crossing, and be believed, and go on living here afterwards, went up by one at about a quarter past four on the ninth of Frostmonth because a man was given a vote.

Nobody in the room could prove that was the wrong thing to count, and everybody in the room could see it, and the reeve read both numbers out and then read the earlier minute out over the top of them, and the two were nine days apart and they were in the same room, and she said:

"Enter both, and enter the date of each, and enter that they are not the same count, because one of them is a count of persons and one of them is a count of names on a roll, and if a clerk in two hundred years adds one of these to the other it will be a good sum of two true things and a lie."

---

Then the warden.

The office was road warden of the Merefen bridge guild. There was one of it. It carried the Sedge Reach — four timber bays, the town's crossing, a bench mark that is a quarter of an inch low and two numbers a year and no comparison of them — and it carried the wharf road, and it carried the winter work, and it had been held in four different families in forty years and the last holder had died in Thawmonth.

"Any nominations."

The foundry's man nominated the man at the fish weir, who declined in nine words and sat down. The guild's man with the levels nominated himself and withdrew in the same breath, twice, in a way that made the clerk enter it twice.

Then the man at the drawknife — who was not a member and had never been a member and was in the room because he had brought a level case for somebody — said a thing that was not a nomination and was in the book as one.

"He will do it."

"He is not a member."

"He has never asked to be a member," said the man at the drawknife. "He has been in this hall four times in six months and said the true thing every time and voted never once, and he refused a seat and a wage in a hall four hundred miles off this year in about eleven seconds, and I am not nominating him because of any of that. I am nominating him because **we have one man in this county who can read where the load goes and nobody has ever asked him whether he would, and I would rather be refused in front of nine men than find out in nine years that we never asked.**"

Four hands.

Four against.

Level, and the casting vote, and the reeve's face did not change at all.

"Carried," said the guild clerk. "Aren Kest, road warden of the Merefen bridge guild, by eight hands and a reeve's. Warden, do you accept?"

---

The hall was very quiet, and the thing about it that Aren Kest noticed, and noticed about eleven seconds later than he noticed it, was that nobody looked surprised.

That was the first thing. He had been in a hall in Longlight in the fourth month and had refused a seat and a wage in eleven seconds and two hundred people had made a noise about it. There was no noise here. The foundry's man put his hands flat on the table. The guild's man with the levels said, "Understood," which he had not said about anything. And the man at the drawknife, who had nominated him, was looking at the floor, and looking at the floor of a guild hall in a way that meant he had done the thing he had come to do and would be examined about it later and did not care.

The second thing was the mechanism.

A bench seat, a wage, a fourth camp, and a master's hut on a river four hundred miles off, refused in a hall in eleven seconds. One warden of a bridge guild, by eight hands and a reeve's, in a hall over a wharf. Same word. *Accepted.*

He stood up, and it took him longer than it should have, and he got the words out in the order he had put them in on the walk up Weir Street.

"I accept the vote and not the office."

The clerk's pen did not move.

"Ma'am, and gentlemen — I want it entered exactly in those words, and I want it entered before I say anything else, because everything else I say is a speech and a speech is what a man makes when he has been given something."

"Aren—" said the guild clerk.

"Enter it."

The clerk entered it. It is in the roll, on the ninth of Frostmonth, in a clerk's hand, and it is the fourth line of the minute, and it reads: *Aren Kest accepts the vote and not the office.*

"A vote is a thing a town gives a man," he said, "in a room, with a date on it. An office is a thing a man is held to. Those are not the same and I have been in this county eleven years and I have watched them get confused in about four places, and every one of the four was a bench that had voted something into a man's name and then, in a worse year, found out that a vote is a thing you are given and an office is a thing you answer for, and the answering is the part that is not given."

"You are the warden of the Sedge Reach," said the guild's man with the levels.

"I am the warden of the Sedge Reach and I will not be, and I am not going to argue about it, and I am not going to pretend I have not accepted it, because I have, and it is in the roll, and I would like a man in nine years who finds the roll to be able to read two lines about the same morning and have both of them be true."

"You will hold the office and not the seat," said the reeve, "and there is a difference and it is not a small one, and the guild is going to have to work out what it is, and I would like it minuted that the warden of this guild accepted the vote and did not accept the office, and that the bench does not know what to do about that, and that it is going to have to do something about it before the thaw."

"I will come when I am wanted," said Aren Kest. "I will read anything anybody puts in front of me. I will say the true thing, which is what I am for, and I will not vote, and I will not hold the crossing, and I will not be the man in the room that a thing is called to at four hours' notice at night in a hard month. **I do not intend to be the man who holds this crossing.** That is the whole of it and I have said it once."

"You have been a member of a guild roll since the fourth of Greenmonth," said the guild clerk, not unkindly. "You refused nothing."

And that was the third thing, and it went through him about two seconds late, the way a thing goes through a man who has been the best reader of a room in a case for a year and a half and who has not once been in a room where the move was his own.

Eleven seconds, in a hall, four hundred miles off, on a word he had never used before and had not known he was going to use. *No.* And he had been in this town eleven years and had never once said it in a room where it counted.

A refusal had cost him nothing. That was what it had been, all those times: a word, in a hall, against people who had no mechanism to move on the next vote, and afterwards there had been a step he had been glad of and a glass he had been asked for and a form he had not signed. He had thought, for about a year, that there was a price on the word. There was no price on the word. **The price was on the man afterwards, and the price was a bench that had learned his name was worth a vote, and he had given it to them.**

He noticed it. He said nothing about it, then or afterwards, and the reason he said nothing was that a man who stands in a room and argues that he ought to be disqualified from a vote is a man who has asked the room to do his arithmetic, and a room that does a man's arithmetic for him has stopped being a room and become a clerk, and eleven weeks ago he had watched a room stop being a room and had said nothing.

So he sat down, and the clerk read the roll, and the roll had a new name on it that had not been there in Fallowmonth, and the name was not his.

---

At about half past four, when the minutes were being read and the subscription book for the Old Toll was on the table with two marks of notes in it and three shillings and fivepence still to find, the man at the fish weir asked the question nobody had asked in the room, which is a thing that man does about twice a year and is why he is on the bench.

"The road," he said. "The one that is kept on the fourth and the ninth. It wants a witness at this end and the witness at this end is the man you have just put on the roll, and a man on the roll cannot witness a thing the roll's own bench is voting on, and if the road is opened on the fourth of Hearthmonth then this bench will have voted on it by then, because there is a bill coming and the bill wants the road shut a fortnight."

Nobody answered him.

"Which is the fourth of Hearthmonth," he said, "and I am not asking the room to settle it today. I am putting it in the book so that it is in the book, and I am going to be forty feet from that bridge at the fourth hour on the fourth of Hearthmonth with a rod in my hand whether anybody stands at this end of the line or not, because the eels do not care."

The reeve said nothing, and her clerk entered that the reeve said nothing, and it is the last line of the minute, and the minute ends there.

A road in this county is a line and two hands, a date, and a witness at each end who is not the person keeping the road. He had been a witness at one end of one of these for six months. On the ninth of Frostmonth a bench of nine, with a column headed *Keeper* on its own roll, gave him a vote and made him the man who would be held to a crossing four hundred yards from a bridge where he cut a seat once and paid for it out of his own money, and put his refusal in the fourth line of a minute in a frame on a wall, where it will be read.

He went out into Weir Street at about ten to five with the frost going and the market going with it, and he did not go to the yard, and he did not go up two flights of a stair over a weigh-house, and he walked as far as the Old Toll abutment and stood on it for about four minutes with his hands loose at his sides and not in his pockets, looking at a dressed block and forty yards of tarred hemp, which was cold, and which had nothing in it, and which he was not the man of.
