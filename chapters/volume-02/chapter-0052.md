# Chapter 0052

The paper that went north on the twenty-seventh of Longlight had been answered twice, and Mara Vey had spent the morning of the second of Embermonth discovering that she had not known, until she sat down with both of them side by side, that the answers had come from two different places.

The first answer came from Bellweather-Living. It was four lines long and it was dated the eighth of Fallowmonth, which was eleven days after the paper went, and it was in the fair hand of the woman who keeps the crossing book on the north bank, and the whole of it was about a bell.

*The sealing held through the wet and the bell is rung on the fourth and the ninth and by nobody else. The third stroke is not struck. It is leaned on, as it was leaned on the night of the refusal, and we have stopped saying that it is cracked and started saying that it is the third stroke, which is shorter and takes less time.*

*The toll at the north steps is a farthing and has been a farthing too high for eleven years and the man who keeps the gate has put it up himself once and taken it down himself once and we have decided to leave it, because a toll that is a farthing wrong is an argument somebody can have and an argument is a thing a crossing is for.*

*Line ninety-one of this book stands and the nine tons went over in one cart and the gate took the farthing and no ticket, and the man of the fourth camp has told the story eleven times since and it gets longer, and I have written down that I do not believe one word of it and that I have checked it twice.*

Mara read that one once and then again for the pleasure of it, which she allowed herself about four times a year, and the pleasure was in the second line: a bell that was cracked and is now the third stroke. Somebody four hundred miles away had gone at a fact in a book and turned it into a job, and had done it in a hand that had to be the same hand as the crossings, and had not asked the north for anything.

The second answer was four lines long as well, and it was dated the first of Fallowmonth, which was four days after the paper went, and it was in a hand that was not a clerk's hand.

*To the keeper of records at Merefen in the north, who sent a paper to a city and did not send it to us.*

*We are not the city. We are a county called Ash, on a river we call the Kiln, and we are eight villages and a market town. We had your paper because a road that had been agreed with somebody answered, and we were the somebody it answered to, and we did not know that either until we read it and found the date on it was a date we had put in a book of our own.*

*A man who fires at the kiln at Kilnholt was struck off the roll of persons of this county eleven months ago, under the county's own law, by the county's own officer, with a date. He has not gone anywhere. He is at his kiln. He cannot be found because a man who is struck cannot be asked anything, and so nobody asks him anything, and so there is nothing to find.*

*Tell us what a record owes a man who is in one and not in it. We are asking because we are a county that counts fires and has never counted itself, and because the two of us have arrived at the same question from opposite ends and we would like to know whether it is a stupid one.*

---

She read that one four times before she understood that she did not understand it, and the not understanding was not about the county.

"Person having knowledge," she said, out loud, to a filing room. "That is their word. Not ours."

It was a quarter past the ninth hour and the office was open and Sarn Oyelaran was at the long table with a wrapper and a piece of string, doing the thing she did on a Tuesday, which was to go through the week behind and check the week in front.

"Read me the third line again," said Sarn Oyelaran, without looking up.

Mara read it again.

"A roll of persons," she said. "I have been a keeper for four years and a half and I do not know what a roll of persons is."

"You do know what it is," said Sarn Oyelaran. "You know what it is in this county. Go and look at what it is in theirs."

So Mara Vey went and looked, because it is the whole of her trade and she has never once been slow at it, and she went to the county's own printed index in the press — the one nobody in Merefen reads and that exists because a county has to be able to find itself — and she found, under R, in the county's own spelling:

*roll of persons — a printed schedule of the inhabitants of a village or a liberty, made for the purpose of the assizes and the levy, in one column of names, ruled, and carried by the constable of the district and produced at the quarter sessions.*

And under it, two inches away, in the same hand, in the same type:

*roll of persons, county — see schedule of the assizes.*

"There," said Mara Vey, to the room. "A roll of persons is a list of the people of a village, in one column, carried by a constable, produced at the sessions. That is what it is in this country. That is what a Crown clerk would use it for."

"And that," said Sarn Oyelaran, still not looking up, "is a list of names. Which is the thing they do not have."

---

Then she went and looked for the county, which took the rest of the morning and part of the afternoon and cost her a shilling in fees for a map she did not need and eleven years of certainty.

There is no county of Ash in this country. She began with the county's own index and went out from there: the hundred and one odd rolls of the assizes, two gazetteers, a directory of the eastern counties, a sheet of the Crown's own circuit map with the hundred names on it in a clerk's small hand, and a rent table of the eastern hundreds. Ash is an ordinary enough word. There are eleven places in this country called Ash or Ashby or Ashtead, and one river called the Ash, and a wood called Ashwood in the west, and none of them is a county with a market town in it, and none of them is four hundred miles from a fen town that can see a kiln smoke from a roof.

She wrote the negative result down, which is a thing she does for everything, and then she wrote down the eleven near misses, and then she crossed out nothing, because a keeper who crosses out a thing she has looked for is a keeper who has stopped looking.

At the fourth hour she came back to the filing room and put both answers flat on the table and read them together, which is the only reading there is, and said out loud the thing the office has to decide by the sixth.

"This is a road or it is a forgery," she said. "If it is a road, then a paper addressed to a city in another country has been answered by a county in this one that is not on any map we hold, and there is a second line out of this county, and the second line is not the first line, and I have been a party to a road for five months without knowing there were two."

"And if it is a forgery," said Sarn Oyelaran, "then somebody has written to a keeper of records in a county of the north on the good paper of a form, in a hand, and got a date, and the date is in my day-book in a fair hand because I initialled it."

"It is not a forgery," said Mara Vey.

"You have not got anything that says it is not a forgery."

"I have got that it is in the same batch as a paper from a city four hundred miles off, on the same road, on the same days, and that it could not be faked by anybody in this county because there is no such place to fake from," she said. "And I have got that it is dated the first of Fallowmonth and the Bellweather answer is dated the eighth, and both are inside a fortnight of the paper going, and the road has been running on named days since the first of Fallowmonth. It is a road. It is a road I cannot find on a map and cannot serve a process on, and it answered a woman in a filing room, and I have known for five months that there is one door in this case and it turns out there are two and I did not order the second one and cannot close it."

She sat down and put her hands flat on the table, which is a thing she does and which Aren Kest has seen her do twice in four years, both times in that room, and neither time for this reason.

"Right," she said. "What goes in the book."

---

The office's rule is on the wall in a fair copy, and it is two rules, and the second one was added on the second of Greenmonth and reads: *that no combined figure is entered in this office.*

So the entry went in the day-book against no person and against no office, with the hour, and in the fair hand, and it went in and not into the press, because a press is a custody and this is a question and a question is not a thing to be held.

*Entered at the second hour of the second of Embermonth, YR 312, on the office's own account, against no person, against no office, with no opinion, from a paper received at this office on the first of Fallowmonth under the fourth term of an agreement to which this office is a party in its own name and not as a keeper, being a paper of four lines in a hand that is not a clerk's hand, of which the first line states that the writer is a county called Ash and is not the city to which the paper was addressed.*

*And this office has compared the said paper against the answer from Bellweather-Living of the eighth of Fallowmonth, which came by the same road on the same days in the fair hand of the person who keeps the crossing book on the north bank, and the two are on the same stock and the two are in the same hand for the address and not for the body, and this office has not been able to establish by what means one road came to be two.*

*And this office has searched for the said county in the county's own index of rolls, in two gazetteers, in a directory of the eastern counties, in a sheet of the Crown's circuit map, and in a rent table of the eastern hundreds, and there is no county of that name in this country, and the search is entered so that the day anybody finds one, the day is a day this office was right to have written the negative down.*

*And this office has not sent this paper to any person and will not, and the reason is entered here and nowhere else: that a man can enter a claim against this office and be answered in a court, and that a county cannot be served, and that a letter put to a county that is not on a map is a piece of paper with a person's handwriting on it going nowhere at the rate of the post, and that this office has four years of evidence that the difference between those two things is the whole of what an office is.*

She read all of that back to herself and was about to initial it when Sarn Oyelaran put her hand flat on the page.

"No," said Sarn Oyelaran.

"It is my entry."

"It is your entry and two thirds of it is right and the third part of it is about you and not about the paper." Sarn Oyelaran took her hand off the page. "You have written *this office cannot tell whether this is a second road or a forgery*. You have written your own ignorance into a book, with an hour on it, and initialled it, and in four years a clerk in this county or a man from an office is going to read the word *cannot* and read it as *did not look*, and that clerk or that man will be right about the sentence and wrong about the office, and there is no correcting line in a book. You taught me that. You taught me that in Greenmonth with the weights and you were right and I have not forgotten it in eight months."

"I did not look at it for nine minutes this morning before I found the county was not there."

"Then enter the nine minutes," said Sarn Oyelaran. "Enter what you did, and not what you could not do. You compared it against two things. It matched neither of them and it is on the same stock as a paper out of a city. Those are three facts and every one of them can be looked for by somebody else in ten years. *Cannot tell* is a fact about this office and it is the only one of the four that nobody can ever check."

Mara Vey sat quite still for about the length of a breath, and then she took a knife and lifted the last third of the entry off the page without tearing it, and read the four lines she had written, and put them somewhere else.

"That is right," she said. "Enter the amendment. Enter it in my hand and enter that the said keeper was told by the second keeper of this office that the entry of an office's own ignorance is not a record, and that the keeper of this office agreed with the second keeper, and that the said entry has been amended, and enter the hour."

"Fourth hour," said Sarn Oyelaran, "and I want my name on it, because you are not going to be the one who is right twice in one afternoon and I am."

"Your name is on it," said Mara Vey. "Your name is on everything in this room and half of it you put there and I have been signing it for four years."

She wrote the amendment and Sarn Oyelaran read it back and initialled it, and then the keeper of the Bellweather Claims Archive put the two answers in a wrapper with a sheet over them, and the sheet said what it was, which is: *two answers to one paper, received on the first and the eighth of Fallowmonth, YR 312, under the fourth term; one of Bellweather-Living and one of a county that is not on any map in this country; held in the day-book and not in the press; not copied, and not to be copied out of this office, and the reason is entered in the day-book and is not the press's reason.*

And that last clause she wrote because a woman in a filing room in another country had refused twice to let a book be copied and had said why in trade's words, and because the reason was *a copy is a second thing that somebody can lose*, and because a keeper who has heard that sentence from a person who kept it should not need to be told twice.

Then she sat in the quiet room with the lamp on and the press shut and thought about the third line, which she had read eleven times and which was the reason the whole of this had happened.

*A man who fires at the kiln at Kilnholt was struck off the roll of persons of this county eleven months ago, under the county's own law, by the county's own officer, with a date. He has not gone anywhere. He is at his kiln. He cannot be found because a man who is struck cannot be asked anything, and so nobody asks him anything, and so there is nothing to find.*

Nine hundred holdings in this office and eight claim numbers in four years and a roll of eleven days of salt weights in a drawer in the counter, and every one of them is a thing a person was written down in, and not one of them is a man who is at his kiln and cannot be found.

She wrote one more line in the day-book against nobody, with the hour, and it is the shortest thing in the book and it is the line the second answer is read against in about nine months by a woman who has been doing this for four years and a half and who has never yet been wrong about a date.

*Fourth hour. I cannot answer the question in the fourth line, because the fourth line asks what a record owes a man who is in one and not in it, and I have held this office for four years and a half and I have two answers and no authority to give either of them away. I have entered the paper. I have entered the nine minutes I spent looking for a county that is not there. I have sent it to nobody. And I would like it noted, by whoever reads this, that the reason this office cannot answer the question is that this office has never once been asked it, and that a question nobody is asked is not a question this office has refused.*
