# Chapter 0100

The twenty-ninth of Greenmonth, YR 313, was a Monday and a market day and the lowest water of the year, and the fourth hour is the hour the river is in its bed and the deck is dry under the third bay and about forty men are on the Sedge Reach with stone in four carts, and a road warden of the Merefen bridge guild is standing on the deck of the third bay with a tool bag at his feet, counting load points.

The nose of the third pier is out of the water this morning for the first time since the sixth of Mudmonth, which is why there are four carts on a gravel approach instead of two, and nobody in this county has ever seen it out twice in one year and nobody in that yard has ever seen it at all, and nobody in this county has ever entered a reason why the low of the year falls on the twenty-ninth of Greenmonth, and nobody has ever asked.

He is not there as the hand of anything. He is on the roll, he has a vote, he will have a vote on a question that is not about him, he does not hold the crossing, he does not vote, and he said in a hall in Frostmonth that he would come when he was wanted, and he came on the twenty-fourth and has been on the deck every working morning since, and that is the whole of the office.

He counts load points because he counts load points. It is a habit and it is not a working and there is no book in this county that it goes in. He put four in the toe of the north pier and four in the toe of the third and two under the cutwater, and then he did it again to see whether he had got the same four, and he had, and he wrote nothing down, and the stone went down all morning.

**His left palm was warm at about the fourth hour and he did not need it.**

Nothing in the sound was his. The river at the low of the year makes almost no noise at the Sedge Reach, which is a thing every man in Merefen has said and nobody has ever written down, and the noise that morning was four carts on a gravel approach and a man shouting about a chain and about forty men and the bell four hundred yards up the road, which went at about the fourth hour and a half, on the Old Toll bridge, and was rung by other hands and not one of them was his.

On the near abutment there was a boy of sixteen who has been the steward's apprentice for two years and is still not good at it, holding one end of a line, and beside him a carpenter who is a hand and not a keeper and has a bar he is not using. They rang it because that is the arrangement. **The arrangement is not Aren's.** It has been a boy and a ferrywright since the twenty-fourth of Fallowmonth and a boy and a carpenter since the ninth of Hearthmonth, and there is a page at the head of the yard's book for the week of the twenty-ninth with the hour against it in a hand somebody would know, and nobody wrote a word in it about who rang the bell.

And at the far end of the line, four hundred miles off, on a coping on a river this county does not have, two pairs of hands struck what they had, which was not a bell and was not nothing, and the sound of it is in the fair copy because the clerk of that assizes copied out what a day in that county sounds like, and the clerk copied it because he was asked to, and what is in the copy is this:

*A tile-cutter of forty-one and the woman who keeps the road struck the coping at the fourth hour and a half, because that is the day the far end of the line keeps, and it is not a bell, and there is no bell in this county and there has never been one, and we have never had the word for what it is that they do in a market town in a fen on a Monday.*

**The other thing that happened on the deck that morning was that somebody had paid a man to look at the third pier, and it was the smallest thing anybody in this county has paid for in two years and it was the only one that anybody in either country has been glad of.**

The county's surveyor had paid the guild's man with the levels six pence, out of a surveyor's fund that is not a fund and is a box, for a written report on the scour at the third pier, and he had not entered it anywhere as a repair and had not told the bench and had not told the yard, and the guild's man with the levels had put it in his own book with the hour on it, which is a thing that happens in about four books in this county.

"You have been paid to look at it," said Aren Kest, "and you have been paid six pence, and I have been in this county eleven years and I have never seen a man paid for reading one of these and it is the best money I have ever heard of spent in this whole business."

"It is six pence," said the guild's man with the levels.

"It is six pence," said Aren Kest, "and I want it said out loud on a deck on the lowest water of the year that the county has paid a man to look at a hole under a bridge, and that is the whole of what a county did about it, and that the bill which went out of a room on Wednesday has a line on it with nothing in it, and that the line with nothing in it has been read by three people and the money has been found, and that nobody in this county can remember the last time that happened and I can, and it was about nine years ago, and I was not here."

"Nine years ago the fourth camp opened," said Tovan Kest, from the deck, without stopping, "and a bench of nine asked a chest twice and got no twice, and the third asking came in Frostmonth. **That is not the same thing and you will hear it said it is and it is not.**"

"I know it is not the same thing," said Aren Kest, "and I said it was not and I am not going to take it back, and there is a difference between a bench that asks and a man in a box that pays six pence, and the difference is that one of them happened in a room with a door on a lane and the other one happened because somebody was frightened, and the frightened one is the one that works."

Nobody argued with him about it, because it was the lowest water of the year and there were about forty men on a bridge and eleven tons of stone going down in four carts.

---

**And at about the seventh hour, on the deck, in a lull, he took his handbag off and opened it and there was a half tile in it, and it weighs a shade over ten ounces and there is nothing on it at all.**

"There are two of these in the world and I made both of them," he said, "and that is the whole of what I have got to say about it and I am going to say all of it because I have said part of it in a filing room and a filing room is where I say things properly.

"The first went north on the fourth of a month in that county's own year, in the bag of the woman who keeps the road at that end, on a day the line was kept, and I watched her put it in and I did not ask her anything about it and I went out of that county in about half an hour afterwards. **I have got no way of finding out whether it ever got here at all, and I am not going to ask, and the reason is in the notebook and the reason is that a man who asks whether an object arrived has told the other end that the object is worth arriving.** It is in a shed at the back of a kiln four hundred miles off, where the woman who fired that kiln is sixteen years in that trade and has never been asked a question in her life, and I think about it about once a week and I do not write it down.

"The second one is this one. I made it behind her kiln with a cold saw on the second day of that month, and it has a saw mark on the back of it that she has never seen and is never going to, and I made it because I could not think of a way to ask whether the first one had arrived. **A man who makes two of a thing to avoid asking a question has already decided what the answer is.** It went into a press two flights up a stair over a weigh-house on the afternoon of the second of this month with a claim number against nothing on it, because a thing with nothing on it has nothing anybody can take out of it, and a press is the only instrument in this case that cannot be served, and the woman who keeps that press weighed it on her landlord's stone, which is not a record, and it came to a shade over ten ounces, and she did not ask me where it had been.

He put it back in the bag and shut it, and the deck went on, and the bell had gone at the fourth hour and a half and nobody rang it twice.


---

**At about the fifth hour a list came down the line from the County of Ash, on a named day, in a fair hand, and it is a list, and a list can only be checked.**

Four hundred and six words. Four hundred and nine was the last one and this one is four hundred and six, and every line of it is a figure or a date or a trade, and a keeper of records can go down it line by line, and the whole of the point of it is that she did.

*And it is the county's answer to the fourth of its own terms, in a hand that is not a clerk's, and it is the first thing a county four hundred miles off has ever sent that anybody here can check.*

*The county has entered that two thousand nine hundred and six tiles have been fired on the roll of persons of a county of nine hundred and forty-one hearths, and that the county's own figure about itself is two thousand four hundred and fifty people, and that the county has never put the two figures in one line and is not going to, and that anybody who wants to do that arithmetic is welcome to come and do it on a bridge.*

**That is the county's own figure about itself and it is spoken as such and nobody in Merefen has touched it.**

*The county has struck the List of Persons to be Struck. It has not burned it. It has superseded it, in a room, on the twenty-fourth of a month this county does not use, in the county's own book, with the three hands named, and the magistrate's own words in it, and the physician's four pages read out, and Ash's dead remaining Ash's with no claim upon them and none to come.*

*The county is grinding back two hundred and fifty-four fired tiles by hand into clay and wedging it onto the bank, and the cost is on a board, and the board foots in pence, and the season is the standing water, and it is two years, and the first of it is now, and there are about thirteen men out of work in a county of nine hundred and forty-one hearths and the number is written down in a keeper's hand with the hour on it.*

*Four yards of the Ninebridge's parapet has been re-laid in tile in the last nine days by a man of fifty-two who wanted to be a mark and not a mouth, and nobody has read a single name out of a sheet in that county since the fourth day of a month this county does not use, and the county has entered that as a rule and not as a favour.*

*And a woman of about sixty, on three hearths, whose fourth column says wife, has written that she does not know whether this is a good idea, and that she is going to do it anyway, and that she would like somebody to write down that she said so.*

And then, in the middle of a column of prices, with nothing to mark it out and no rule above it and no rule below, and in the same hand as everything else, which is the hand of a man with a ledger who has been counting fires for nineteen years:

*And one man of this county has not been spoken to since the ninth week of a room at the assizes, and he is at a kiln, and the clerk of the roll does not know which kiln and neither does he, and the mark on his tile agrees with the entry and both are not either, and his name is in three books, and it is entered here as a number and not as a name because the second of the county's own terms is forty-one words long and the county has kept it, and because this county has no column for it, and because the county asks that the line be carried north as it stands.*

---

**She had read the first of them in fair hand on the first of this month, and it was a bill, and she had gone down it line by line and found nothing to argue with. This one was not a bill. It was an answer, and it was the answer to a term, and she knew it was the answer to a term before she had read two lines of it, because the first line of it is the county's own figure about itself and the second line of it is a word that is not in this county's calendar at all.**

She read it twice. The first time for what it said and the second time for whether it was true.

**It is a list, and that is the whole of the fourth of five terms, and a list can only be checked, and she checked it.**

She went down it with a rule. Nine hundred and forty-one hearths, and the county's own figure of two thousand four hundred and fifty people, and the number of tiles fired on the roll of persons, and the two hundred and fifty-four with the man's own floor under it and his own sentence that he would not go past it, and thirteen men, and nine cords of wood at a named price, and a day and a half of a wedging shed, and a rule with no office behind it, and a yard's arithmetic. Every figure had a trade or a term against it that she knew what it meant. Every one of them could be checked by a clerk in this room with nothing but that sheet and about four years.

**And it is the first document that has ever come into this office out of a place that has agreed to be checked, and the agreement is not in the document, and the agreement is the reason it can be checked at all, and it is the fourth of five terms printed in an office's hand on the back of a form in a shed four hundred miles off, and she has never read the back of that form and neither has the woman who keeps the road at that end, and neither is going to.**

She found nothing to argue with. She wrote nothing for an hour, and then she entered it in the day-book against no person with the hour on it, in the ordinary form, with a claim number against the office and against no person and no opinion, **because a figure is not a list, and this office has said that three times in four months to two different offices, and the third time is the day the county answered a term.**

And there was one line she could not put down, and she had a page for it, and it was not the page she had expected.

**It is a line in the middle of a column of prices with nothing to mark it out, and it is a number and not a name, and it says that the books are right about him.**

The books are right about him. That is the sentence. Not that he is missed, and not that he is struck, and not that he is owed anything, and not that anybody has done wrong. **Three books in three places and every one of them is right about a man who is alive, and the county has no column for the being alive, and the county has asked that the line be carried north as it stands, and it is the ninth time in four years that a record has come into this room about a person who cannot be found because he cannot be asked.**

And the difference is the same difference it has been every time, and it is the only difference there is, and it is not a difference in the county's handwriting: **the record is not asking her anything, and there is no instrument in this kingdom that can make it ask her anything, and the page that says so has been in a day-book over a weigh-house since the twenty-second of Fallowmonth, and it has got a number on it and not a name, and she wrote it, and it is four lines long, and the fourth of the four is the line the whole of this case is standing on without anybody knowing.**

She did not go and get it. She knew what was on it and she did not go and get it, and that is the same not asking in a different direction, and there is nobody in this room to see her not do it.

---

**And the last thing that happened on the twenty-ninth of Greenmonth, YR 313, happened in a room over a weigh-house at about the eighth hour, and it is an entry, and there is no more in it than an entry, and it is the last thing anybody in either country wrote on the twenty-ninth of Greenmonth.**

The day-book is the only book in that office that nobody can serve. She wrote in it with the date and the hour and the claim number against the office and against nobody, and there was no name on the line and there was no opinion on the line, and the pen was put down when it was done.

And then she turned the page back, to a page with one line on it, written in Fallowmonth, YR 312, at about the tenth hour, four lines in all, of which the fourth is the one the whole of this case is standing on without anybody knowing: *that this office is not going to ask him again.*

She looked at it for about a minute and a half with no lamp.

Then she made an entry, and it is the same entry, entered a second time, against nobody, with the hour on it, in a hand that has stopped asking, and it is the second time since Fallowmonth and the ninth entry of its kind in four years, and there is no reason on the line and there is nothing on the line but the hour:

*Twenty-ninth of Greenmonth, YR 313, a Monday, the eighth hour. A number, and a county, and an hour. Nothing else against it.*

*Entered, at the same hour, on the same page, against nobody: that the not asking has been entered on this page so many times this month that a keeper in nine years will count the entries instead of reading them, and that a keeper of records enters the not writing because the not writing is the part that is a decision, and that this is the last time this office will enter that sentence in a year that has needed it, and that the day this office wants to stop entering it is not going to be the day anything has changed, and that it is going to be the day nobody has asked anything for a long time, and that she will not know the day it happened.*

---

Four hundred miles off, in a room above a kiln, a man of fifty-eight who fired the tiles in that kiln last spring does not know which kiln he fires.

He is alive. He is employed. He has a hearth with a fire in it on the nights the county can see it from a road, and he has not been spoken to since the ninth week of a room at the assizes, and his name is in three books, and all three of them are right about him, and the books are in three places and no instrument in two countries will do one thing about it.

And the woman who reads for a living has written the whole of it down, with the hour on it, against nobody, in a day-book over a weigh-house, in a room with a door on a lane, four pence a year, and there is no name on the line and there is no opinion on the line, and the pen is down.
