# Chapter 0063

The room with the stove at the assizes at Kilnholt is the assizes' own room, and it has been the assizes' room since the assizes was put in Kilnholt, and there is a bench in it with three chairs in it and a table with a lamp on it and a clerk at the end of the table with a pen, and the magistrate of the assizes for the County of Ash is Colm Arreld, and he is fifty-four, and he was in the room for about nine minutes before Aren Kest understood that the man was afraid of the spring.

He understood it because of the almanac on the wall, which was a county thing and had the digging days marked in a different ink, and because of the two carts' worth of ash-wood standing in the corner that had not been in the corner in any of the other rooms he had been into in this county, and because a man who has hired carts by the quarter before he needs them is a man whose trade has stopped being steady, and not a man whose conscience is.

"You will want to know why you are in here," said Arreld. "I will not waste your morning. I have a fire in here that I am burning and I have a clerk and I have four lines of law on this table, and I have nobody to ask, and you came out of a road in this county nine days ago and the whole of Kilnholt knows it."

"Who should I be?"

"Anybody else," said Arreld. "You are not a magistrate and you are not on a jury and you are not in this county and there is no column in this county for what you are, and I am going to spend about an hour of your morning anyway, and I am going to do it because a man who has come from somewhere else has no reason to be polite about it, and I would rather be argued with by a stranger this month than by this room in eleven years, and that is the truth of why you are in here and I would like it written down that I have said it."

The clerk wrote it down. He was about thirty and he had the flat, unhurried hand of a man who has been the person in the room who is not talking, and he did not look up, and his name was not given and was not asked for.

---

"The law," said Arreld, "is four lines, and it is on this table, and it is signed by three magistrates, and the third of them has been dead since the eighth of the last rolling. I will read you the four lines because I have read them to this room nine times and I would like to hear how they sound to somebody who has not read them before."

He read them off the table and not off the paper, which meant he had them by heart, and a man who has four lines of his own county's law by heart and can say the truth about a law is rarer in this county than nine hundred and forty-one hearths.

*Where a list of persons to be struck from the count has been made under this act, and has remained unexpended for the space of one year, the assizes may cause the list to be burned in the market square at the sittings, in the presence of two magistrates and of such witnesses as the assizes shall call; and thereupon the striking of every person named in the list shall be thereby dissolved; and the clerk of the roll shall destroy the tiles of every person so named, and shall enter the destruction in the roll, and not the name.*

"Read the last four words again," said Aren Kest.

"The clerk of the roll shall destroy the tiles of every person so named, and shall enter the destruction in the roll, and not the name."

"There is no name in it."

"There is no name in it," said Arreld. "And I did not put that clause in and the man who is dead put that clause in, or did not, and it is in the law and I am not going to argue with a law in my own room on a working morning, and I will tell you what I think it is. I think it is mercy, and I will tell you why. If the clerk of the roll enters a name beside two hundred destructions then for ever after there is a page in this county that says these two hundred people were destroyed, and that page is a mark, and it is read. I would rather have a line that says two hundred tiles went on such a day. And I may be wrong and I have thought about it for a year, and I have not got anybody in this county to ask, because the one man in this county who would have an opinion about it is sixty-three and writes things down and puts them in a drawer."

"He has written it down."

"He has written it down," said Arreld, "and I have the copy in the next room, and I have read it, and I disagree with it, and I am going to tell you why I disagree with it and then I am going to ask you a question and I would like you to be the man I think you might be for about nine minutes."

---

"The list is called the List of Persons to be Struck," he said. "It is two hundred and six names and it is eleven months old and it is in a locked press in the room behind this one and the clerk of the roll has the only other key and she is a better keeper of it than I am a magistrate, and I will tell you that for nothing. The sittings of this assizes sit at the end of this rolling, and on the day they sit the list will be a year old, and the law will let me burn it, and after that day it will be two years old and a man may sit in a room in nine years and say there was no such list, and there would be nothing in the world to say him wrong."

"And you want to burn it."

"I want to burn it in the market square at the sittings, in daylight, with two magistrates on it and every person in this town who cares to stand in the square and watch," said Arreld, "and I have never been frightened of a public thing in my life and I am frightened of this, and I have made my peace with being frightened of it, and I would like somebody in this county to tell me that I am doing it for the right reason and I have looked round this county for two years and there is nobody to tell me and you are the first person out of this county who has ever come in here, and so I am going to ask you, and I am going to ask you properly, which is the first time in nine years I have asked anybody anything in this room with a law on the table: **what do you think?**"

He put his thumb on the table.

"And before you answer: the sittings sit at the end of this rolling. The kiln hands say the rolling is three weeks out. I say two. I have been wrong about this county's calendar twice in nine years and both times I sat in a market square in the rain waiting for a road, so take whichever of the two you would rather."

And that was the moment.

Aren Kest had been eleven years in rooms where men asked him what he thought. It was a thing he had got good at, the way a man gets good at a thing, and it had got him into four rooms in eleven months in a county four hundred miles off, and it had got him a bench seat and a wage refused in a hall in eleven seconds, and it was the first time anybody in this book had asked him for his opinion instead of a service, and he was aware of the whole of it, in a room with a stove, at about the ninth hour, in a county of clay.

"I would want to know what happens to the tile," he said.

"What tile?"

"The tile of a person whose name is on that list," he said. "A struck person has a tile. A fired tile with their name on it and their own hand on the back, and it is in a box in a room on the ground floor of this building, and it does not get destroyed when they are struck from the count, because the law destroys the tile when the list is burned, and not before. So for eleven months there are two hundred people who are not on the count and have a fired tile that says they are. And then you burn the list, and the striking is dissolved, and they are on the count again, and the roll is wrong, because the roll says there is a man at that hearth and there is a tile on a shelf and there is no name on either side of it. So the tiles go."

Arreld put both hands flat on the table.

"They go," he said.

"And then the hearths."

"And then the hearths," he said, "which is the part I have not slept properly over and I am going to give you the whole of it because I would rather a stranger carried the whole of it out of this room than a piece of it. A hearth is a fire. Two hundred and six of those two hundred and six names are in houses, and the houses have fires in them tonight and tomorrow night and for ever, and if you take the name out of the house and take the tile out of the shelf then in a year there is a fire in Kilnholt with nobody in the account of it, and a man in a chair by a fire is a thing that can be asked *why is that fire lit* and the answer is that it is lit because it has always been lit, and in nine years the fire will be there and the answer will have gone, and it will be a fire that nobody can give a reason for and nobody can be asked about, and every house that has a fire in it and no reason is a thing that this county has never had one of in four hundred years."

"That is not a worse thing than a name that cannot be read," he said.

"No," said Colm Arreld. "That is not a worse thing than a name that cannot be read. It is very much a better thing, and I have got a table of figures and I am going to read it to you, and I am going to read it before you answer me, because if you answer me first you will be arguing about a principle and I have got numbers and the numbers are the only honest part of this room."

---

He had the figures and he had kept them himself, in a hand that was not a clerk's hand, on four sheets of a copying book, and he said them out loud.

"In the four years before that list was made, forty-one people came off this county's roll with the softening. Eleven in the first year. Nine in the second. Twelve in the third. Nine in the fourth. That is the sickness and that is what it does to a county of this size and it is a terrible number and it is the ordinary number for a place this poor with this much clay in the water.

"In the eleven months since the list was made, a hundred and twelve people have come off this roll with the softening. And I have the months. The month the list was made, nine. The month after, eleven. And then it goes nine, thirteen, fifteen, seventeen, twenty-one, twenty-four. And I have read that column about forty times, because a man who reads a column forty times is looking for the place in it where it turns, and there is no place where it turns, and it does not turn, it goes up and up, and it has been four years that it goes up and up, and the winter before last was the worst winter this county has had and the winter before that was the second worst, and those two winters are why I am in a hurry, and a magistrate's calendar is a calendar of winters, and any man in this room who tells you a magistrate thinks in anything else has not sat in one."

He put the four sheets down and squared them.

"And here is the part I have said out loud to nobody, and I am saying it to you because you are a stranger and because if I do not say it in the next ten minutes I shall not say it this year. **The list is read.** The clerk of the roll reads it every morning, because there are two hundred and six names in it that somebody has to look at. The physician reads it. The man with the ledger reads it, because he is a man who counts and he cannot leave a column unread. I have asked all three of them to stop and I have asked them to stop in writing and I have no power to make any of them stop, and I have not told the rollkeeper that I have asked, because the rollkeeper is the only person in this county I trust and I was not going to put her in a room with that in it.

"So: every mark in this county that is read is on the roll, and the list is the hottest part of the roll, and a man who is on the list has his name read every morning in a room he is not in, and a struck person cannot be read, and a person who cannot be read cannot carry the softening, and I have worked that out in a night and a half and I could not make it come out any other way, and the instrument to do it is a fire in a market square, and it is the only instrument I have, and I have had it for a year and I have not been able to bring myself to use it, and if you tell me not to use it I want you to understand what you will be doing, which is that you will be telling a county with a fever in it to keep a list."

---

And then the stove ticked, because stoves tick, and he waited.

Aren Kest had five months of being the best reader of a room in a book about not doing that. He had a sentence ready. He had had a sentence ready since about the fourth minute, and the sentence was the one about a record a person can contradict, and the sentence about a thing written down, and the sentence about a bell-shaped seal going into a county file off a single stone, and about a room in a filing room over a weigh-house where a woman wrote down nine minutes because an entry of an office's own ignorance is the only fact in a book that nobody can ever check.

He looked at the four sheets with the columns on them and the last figure was twenty-four in a month, and the column went up and up and it did not turn, and there was no argument in the world that got past that column. The magistrate was right about the fever. Not right in the way a good man is right about a hard thing and is prepared to be argued out of it in a room, and not right the way a man with a case is right, but right the way a man is right who has read a column forty times and found no place in it where it turns.

And the reason he had nothing was not that the argument was bad. It was that he had spent five months being a man who is useful in a room, and he had never once been a man who was useless in a room, and there was no second set of skills for this, and the man who had come into the county on his first morning and had not found one in this county at all, and there was nothing in this room to look at.

He said, finally:

"I have nothing. I have had five minutes and I have nothing, and I am not going to say something to be the man in the room, because I have watched a man in a hall do that and I have been the man in the room and you would be able to tell the difference inside a minute and it would be worth nothing to you and it would be worth something to me, and there is a magistrate in a county four hundred miles off who spent four months of this year being sure he knew which end of what he was holding, and he was wrong about the date of it, and he has not told anybody yet, and I am not going to be that in your room."

"Then you have wasted my morning."

"I have not," said Aren Kest, "and I will tell you what I have got instead, and it is a trade answer and not a moral one, and I would like you to notice that I have noticed."

"Go on."

"You have told me the column goes up and up and it does not turn, and the column is the roll, and the list is the hottest part of the roll, and you want to take the hottest part of the roll out of the fire," he said. "And I have spent four days in a county where a man with a ledger has three thousand tiles in a shed and a number of people, and he has not put one of those two numbers against the other in nineteen years, and he counts fires. And you have a column of soft people and a list of two hundred and six names, and you have never put the two columns side by side either, and I am not saying that is the same mistake. I am saying it is a mistake of the same shape. **You have got a record and a disease and you have been looking for the disease in the record instead of asking who is reading the record, and the record has been read every morning for nineteen years, and the reading is not a thing anybody in this county ever chose.** So burning the list might not do what you want. It might take out the hottest line and leave the roll."

"The roll is the county," said Arreld.

"Yes," said Aren Kest. "That is why I have given you that and not the other thing. And the other thing is that I do not know, and the woman who keeps your roll does not know, and the man with the ledger does not know, and the physician has written it down and put it in a drawer and you have read it and you disagree with it. And I have been in this county five days and I am a sett-cutter from a fen and I have no standing in this room and the only standing I have got is that I am the only person you have ever asked in this room, and I would like to be rid of that, and I would like to be rid of it before somebody finds out that a stranger is the only man in a county that can be asked, because the first thing that happens then is that the county stops asking anybody at all."

Colm Arreld sat with his hands on the table for about nine seconds.

Then he said: "You are not going to tell me not to burn it."

"No."

"You thought about it."

"I thought about it for about four minutes and I had five and I am not going to pretend I thought about it for five," said Aren Kest. "And I want you to understand that I wanted to, and that wanting to is not a reason. If I tell this room not to burn that list, I am telling a county with a fever in it to keep a list, on my word, and I am the man who put the list there by arguing about it in front of witnesses — or I would be, in nine years, or a hundred, and I am the man who does that in a hat. I have been doing this for eleven years and I am not going to do it again in a hat."

"So there is nothing."

"There is one thing and it is not advice and you may throw it out," said Aren Kest. "It is a thing a man says when he cannot answer a question and does not want to have lied. The list is unexpended at eleven months. The law wants a year, and the year is up at the sittings, and you have told me twice now when the sittings sit. So the day is already chosen and it is not chosen by you, and a thing that is a year old is a thing that is about to be two years old, and two years is long enough for a man's column to turn. And yours has not turned in a year. I would not bet a tile on it turning inside a fortnight, and I would bet a tile on it turning in a year, and a betting house is not a court, and I would like a week."

He was not going to get it. He knew that before he had finished the sentence, and he had said it anyway, and that was new, and he noticed it, and he did not know what to do with it.

---

The clerk ruled the day's attendance line, and the four lines of law went back into the folder, and the folder went onto a shelf, and Aren Kest went out into a corridor where a woman with a box of tiles under her arm came past him going the other way and did not stop and did not look at him, and she is the clerk of the roll, and she is the best keeper of a list there has ever been in this county, and she is the one who would have to break two hundred and six tiles in a yard in a week, and she has not been asked whether she will.

He went down the steps and out into a street where the digging had started in earnest at both ends, and the carts were in the road, and a boy was shouting the price of ash-wood at a door, and a man in a leather apron on the far side of the street was on her knees at a kerb with a hand on a tile she had looked at nine thousand times, and she was not going to look up and she did not.

Four lines of county law, and three signatures on them, and the third signature was nine months old and the man who had put it there had been dead since the eighth of the last rolling, and nobody in that room had said one word about it, and the reason nobody had said one word about it is that a signature is a signature.

And a man who has been asked a question in a room, and has found out that the reason he was asked is that he is useful, and that the usefulness is the whole of the reason, walked up the street in a county that was counting its fires.
