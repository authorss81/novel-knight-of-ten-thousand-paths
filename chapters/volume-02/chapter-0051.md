# Chapter 0051

The first of Embermonth was a Wednesday and the county's market days were on Saturday and Thursday, and a woman came down Weir Street with a leather bag at about the seventh hour and stopped in the gateway of a yard on Weir Street, because the yard was the second door she had tried and the first one had been a filing room two flights up a stair that belongs to a man who weighs salt.

Nobody sent her to the yard. Sarn Oyelaran sent her down Weir Street with a boy of fourteen for a walk she could not be expected to do alone, and she asked at four doors before she asked at this one, and what she said at the fourth was: *I am not here about the object. I am here because there is a column on my form and I have to know what goes in it, and the office that owns the column is two hundred miles off and the keeper of the records that ought to answer it is two flights up a stair, and I have been up the stair and she has given me a fair copy of a page and no advice, in her own words, because she says advice is not a thing her office may give.*

The yard had four men in it and a churn propping the top hinge off the workshop door, and oak shavings four inches deep, and a factor's paving stone standing on end against the back wall that had been standing on end since Greenmonth and had about a fortnight left on its face.

"Step in or don't," said Tovan Kest, without looking up. "There's a thing on the bench that's hot."

She stepped in. She was thirty-three or thirty-four, in a plain coat with the hem of it damp, and she had a leather bag with a strap worn shiny on one side, and she put the bag on the bench beside the hot thing, which was a glue pot, and took out four sheets of printed paper and squared them on the bench, and squared them again, and then read out loud from the back of the fourth one in a voice that had been used to reading in rooms.

"Read it as it is printed," she said. "Not as it is explained."

The marginal instruction on the reverse of a form of the Office of Provincial Registration, in the office's own hand:

*Where no office of keeper is found in the county schedule, the person having knowledge shall be entered under the column headed Keeper, and the entry shall not be construed as the creation of an office.*

Nobody in the gateway said anything for about four seconds. A man of about sixty at the shave horse put his hand flat on the wood and left it there.

"That is not English," said the man at the shave horse. "That is four sentences wearing one coat."

"It is a standard instruction," said the woman. "It is printed under the office's hand at the foot of every form of the series, which is R.C., and there are four of them in the series, and the R stands for return and the C for contact, and I will not pretend to you that I know what any of that means because I am an aid to the register and not a person who designs forms."

"Then you are not the one to ask," said Aren Kest.

"I am the one who has to fill it in," she said. "That is closer to the one you want."

---

He had come down to the yard at the sixth hour to be in it, and he had been in the yard since the fifth because his brother had a thing on the bench that wanted doing at the low water and the low water was three weeks off and everybody in the street knew it and nobody had said so.

She put the form on the bench and turned it round and put her thumb on the fourth column.

*Object. Date. Person having knowledge. Keeper. How often.*

"There is no date on it," she said. "That is the whole of why I am standing in a yard in a fen county instead of sitting down. A form with no date on it does not have to be answered, and a thing that does not have to be answered does not have to be tried, and my office has found that out the expensive way. We have sent nineteen of these to this county in eleven months. Nineteen. We have had an answer to none of them. And I have read all nineteen, because that is part of my work, and every one of them came back with a figure in it."

"A figure," said Aren Kest.

"Eleven," said the woman. "The last one, in Fallowmonth, from the keeper over the weigh-house. Eleven. And a figure is not a list, and the office cannot act on a figure, and there is nothing at the end of a figure for a person to be hanged by, and everybody in the room where that was read out agreed that was a very clever thing for somebody to have done."

Nobody in the yard disagreed with her about the cleverness. That was the part he wrote down afterwards, in the notebook, with the hour on it: *four men in a yard and a woman with a bag and not one of us said the word craft, and all five of us knew.*

"So you have come to get a name," he said.

"I have come to get a date and a name, in that order, and I have come on my own time, and my office does not know I am here and will not have sent me, and I would like that entered somewhere by somebody with a book." She turned the sheet over again. "A date is easy. You can have a date. A name is the difficult half and I will tell you why, and then I will fill the column in and you can watch me do it, because you have not seen one of these filled in and I am told you are the person in this county everybody sends for."

"Because I have been in every room where this has been argued," he said. "That is not a qualification. It is a habit."

"Fill it," said Tovan Kest, from the bench, without turning round.

---

She filled it in with a pen she had to unscrew from somewhere, and she wrote slowly, and she did not ask him what to write and she did not ask him whether to write it, and the thing she wrote in the fourth column was **Aren Kest, road warden, in the roll of the Merefen bridge guild, and no other office** — and the second half of it, the *and no other office*, was not in the column, and she said so.

"There is a box under it," she said. "*State the office, if any.* There is no office. I am not going to write *none*, because a man who writes *none* has conceded that there ought to be one. I am going to write what you are, in the order you are in it, and the reader of the form can do what he likes with it."

"And you are aware," said Aren Kest, "that the instruction on the back of that sheet says the entry is not to be construed as the creation of an office."

"I am aware of it every working day of my life," said Sabin Dain, "because I write it out about forty times a year when a county schedule has to be certified, and I have asked twice, in writing, for it to be explained, and both times the answer I have received is that it is a standard form instruction and that standard form instructions are not explained. I have been in this office eleven years. I have never been given a reason for it. I would like a reason. I am telling you that I would like a reason, because a thing four generations of clerks have written down and nobody can say why is not a form instruction, it is a habit, and habits are what a county does to itself when nobody is looking."

She squared the four sheets on the bench again and put the pen away.

"My name is Sabin Dain. I am an aid to the register at the Office of Provincial Registration, and I am not a member of anything, and I have never been a member of anything, and if anybody ever tells you otherwise in a room you should ask them for the number of the list."

She said it the way a woman says a thing she has said before and does not enjoy saying.

"You fill in forms," he said.

"I fill in forms," said Sabin Dain. "And I will tell you what I want, since you have not asked me and I have come a long way. I want that no family in this country is ever again removed from a record by a name nobody entered. That is the whole of it. I do not care whose family. I do not care what they did. I want the name to be on the paper by the man who takes it off, in his own hand, with a date against it, and I want there to be a column on the form where he has to put it."

"That is a strange thing to want," said the man at the shave horse, "from an office."

"It is the only thing I have ever wanted," said Sabin Dain, "and I have been at it eleven years, and I will tell you how it goes. Every year the returns come back with fewer names in them and more figures, and every year nobody can be punished for anything, because a figure cannot be refused and a figure expires for want of a party. And in four years, when somebody in a good coat comes along and asks how a county can be governed without a name in it anywhere, there will be eleven months of my returns in a file saying that the officer asked this office for a name and this office did not get one. And on that day it will be somebody else's day and not mine, and I would like to be able to say that I asked."

She put the four sheets into the bag and did the strap.

"There is a dated one coming," she said. "Within the month. It will have a day on it in this corner and it will be the same form with the same five columns, and when it comes you can answer it or not answer it, and if you do not answer it that is a thing a court can do something about, which is more than it can do about anything else my office has sent to this county."

---

He wrote the whole of it down that evening in the room over the cooper, with the hour on it, and the notebook entry is the reason the reader knows the shape of the next eight months. It is in the flat hand, and it is four lines, and the first line is the one that is going to be checked against a form in Chapter 76 by a person who will not know to check it:

*First of Embermonth, YR 312, seventh hour, in a yard on Weir Street. A second R.C. 4 came back to the filing room and an officer of the office that printed it came down the road with it, unasked and unsent, and filled the fourth column in front of four men with what I am and the order I am in it in, and wrote under the box that there is no office, and said that the reason was that a man who writes none has conceded that there ought to be one.*

*And the instruction on the reverse is the private-keeper clause. I have had it in front of me now, in print, in an office's hand, on a paper with a man's name in a column, and I have had the phrase out of the Longlight returnable order and out of Lysa Fenn's finding and out of the back of Rell's own form for five months, and I have never once been able to say what it is for, and neither can she, and she has asked twice in writing and has been told that it is a standard form instruction.*

*And on the reverse, under the instruction, printed in the same hand in a smaller type, as a standard of measurement for the return: **a road held, three weeks.** Three weeks. It is the figure out of my own notebook and there is no office in this kingdom that can tell me whose figure it is, and the office that has printed it cannot say whose figure it is either, and I have looked at the foot of the sheet and there is nothing under it, and I am going to write the word down here once because I have never written it in a book anybody else will read and because in about eleven years somebody is going to want to know where a number first appeared on paper in this case: **Gatehouse.** It is a measurement standard and nothing else and nobody in this county has ever heard the word.*

*And the shape of it, which I can see and cannot prove and am putting down because a man who only writes down the things that are wrong is doing a kind of arithmetic that gets him into a court: this office has sent nineteen forms to this county and has had an answer to none, and every answer it has had is a figure, and a figure is not a list, and the reason a figure comes back is that a name in a form in a provincial office is a thing a man gets made out of. Mara Vey wrote that down in Greenmonth and she was right and she did it to keep a name out of a file. And it has cost this county nothing so far and it has cost the office nothing so far and the office has discovered that a figure cannot be refused. A dated form is coming. What a dated form can be refused with is a court, and a court is a thing this county has, and the answer to a dated form is a name in it, and I do not have to be a clever man to see what happens next: somebody with a good filing system is going to find out that the cheapest lawful way to act on a road it cannot find the owner of is to stop asking for names.*

He read that last paragraph back twice and did not soften it, and then he wrote one more line under it, which is the line he will read again in a kiln shed four hundred miles off, and which he does not know yet is the most useful sentence in the book:

*A thing that cannot be refused and cannot be tried is not a document. It is a road with nobody at the far end of it. I have been one of those. I have stood at this end of one twice and I did not know what I was looking at until a woman in a clay county asked me a question I could not answer.*
