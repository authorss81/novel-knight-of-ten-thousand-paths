# Chapter 0077

The fourth of Hearthmonth was a Friday and a market day, and the crossing out was at the fourth hour, and the whole of it took nineteen minutes at this end.

That is the first thing and it is the last thing and everything in between is the twenty-six days of a man's absence and the four months since and a rope of four fathoms.

Tovan Kest's rule: **the line is made up at the third hour, in the gateway, and it takes nine minutes, and every one of the nine goes in the yard's book with the date and the hour on it, because a line is a thing set and a thing set goes in a book.** The fourth of Hearthmonth was the fourth time he had made one up and the second time he had made one for a road that was not a crossing, and the splice at this end was cut in a yard in Fallowmonth out of a cooper's bin and it was the same splice, because there is one splice in that yard and it is the only one he owns.

The terms came out of the same drawer. They had arrived in a barrow on the ninth of Embermonth with a tile on them, six inches by four, fired grey clay, five lines pressed in a hand that is not a clerk's and not a die's, in three sizes of writing because the second line is forty-one words, cut with a broken piece of a saw. The tile had come back over a bridge four hundred miles off on a barrow in a market, and a woman in a leather apron had put it in a press in a room, and he had read it out loud in a gateway in front of four men, and the fourth line was the one that had gone into the yard's book because a thing a man reads out in a gateway is a thing set.

The toll-holder's clerk read the five lines out in the gateway at a quarter to the fourth hour, from a fair copy, at the reeve's request, in a voice that was doing a job and not a ceremony, and nobody in that yard had ever heard them before and two of the men at the drawknife could not follow the second line and did not try.

*1. The road is not to be made to carry a person.*
*2. No name is to be read out on the far side of the line by anybody from the north; and no name is to be written in the line's book at the north end.*
*3. The north is not to send anybody who can be asked to come and look.*
*4. A person who is struck may answer; and if a person who is struck answers, the person is restored.*
*5. The road is kept by a hand at each end and a witness at each end; and the witness at Ash's end is chosen by Ash.*

"Read the second one again," said the boy of fifteen, who rings the bell and who was standing in the gateway with his hand on the frame of the door.

The clerk read it again, and the yard was quiet in the way a yard is quiet, which is completely, and not one of the nine men in it said the word *name* out loud afterwards, because the second line is why a meeting on a market morning in a fen county is quiet: **there is a thing everybody in the yard wants to do, and the county wrote down that it is not to be done, and the county's word is the gate.**

---

The bench had sat at the fourth hour of the same morning, which is not usual, and the reason it had sat was a bill.

The bill is in the spring and it is not the ninth of Greenmonth; it is the twenty-fourth, and it wants the Sedge Reach shut a fortnight for the scour, and the bench knew about it in Frostmonth and had minuted that it knew. But the bench also had before it, on the ninth of Hearthmonth, one item of ordinary business with a date on it: that the road be kept, as it is kept, on this day, with a hand at this end and a witness at this end.

Eight hands and the reeve's, and the reeve voted, and the reeve had not voted on this guild's own matter in nine years, and on the ninth of Frostmonth she had used a casting vote twice, and on the fourth of Hearthmonth she used her hand like anybody's, and the minute says *the reeve voted* and the bench-clerk, who is a careful man of nineteen years' standing in a copyist's trade, entered the words *the reeve voted* without any comment and read them back, and nobody asked him to read them back.

Which is why Reeve Aldis Fenn was in the yard on Weir Street at about half past three with a sheet and a rule under her arm, and why she had a sheet and a rule, and why everybody in that gateway looked at her when the toll-holder's clerk read out the line about the witness.

She read it in about two seconds, standing up, the way she reads everything.

"Sign the third line," she said.

Bram Ottery signed it. He is forty-seven and unemployed and unrewarded and unpromoted and he has not been sorry for one hour of it and has not been thanked for one hour of it, and he signed with the same small neat upright hand that has signed nine hundred things, none of which is a document, because a watch sergeant signs a book.

The reeve watched him sign and then put the rule down on the bench-board.

"I am not going to sign the fourth line," she said, "and I want it minuted that I was asked to and that I said no, and I want the reason minuted, and I want the reason minuted in the words I am about to use and not in better ones, because if it is minuted in better ones then in nine years a man will read that a reeve of Merefen had a reason and it will not be my reason."

Nobody said anything.

"I am the reeve of this town. A reeve is the town's witness. That is what the word means and I have been it for nine years and I have wanted to vote on this guild's own matter for nine years and I have not, and **I will not stand on that line today, because a road this town does not own cannot have this town as its witness, and if it has, then in four years somebody will say the town stood there, and the town will be right and the road will be shut, and the road will have been shut by a signature and not by an argument, and an argument is a thing that can be had again on a different day by a different bench with a different temper in it.**"

It was the same sentence. Word for word, in a gateway on Weir Street, on the ninth of Embermonth, at eleven o'clock at night, with the market audible four streets off. She had said it then and Tovan Kest had written it down, and the man at the drawknife had said it was the best thing anybody in the town had said in a year, and she had said it was the first thing she had said in nine years that anybody had written down.

The man at the drawknife said nothing this time. It was the same sentence and hearing it twice is a different thing from hearing it once.

"Enter it whole," said Reeve Aldis Fenn. "And enter that I voted on it this morning with my hand, and that I am not going to stand on a line about a thing I have voted on, and that I have thought about that for five months and I have got to the bottom of it and the bottom of it is that a man may do one of the two and not the other and it took me nine years to find out which of the two I would rather do and it is not the one I thought when I started."

The bench-clerk entered it. It is in the guild's roll of this town in a copyist's hand and it is four lines and it is the whole of the reeve's nine years and she has never asked for it to be shortened.

"Then the line stands without you," said the toll-holder's clerk.

"The line has never wanted the reeve," said Reeve Aldis Fenn. "The line wants a hand and a witness, and neither of them is the town, and the whole reason I have been frightened of this since Mudmonth is that there is nothing in this arrangement that can be blamed on anybody afterwards. It is the best-made thing I have ever been near. **It is a road that cannot be shut by a signature because there is no signature on it that means anything.**" She picked the rule up. "I will hold the sheet."

---

At a quarter to the fourth hour the bell went.

The boy of fifteen rang it alone, on the near abutment, because the far end of that bell is four hundred yards up the road and there is no road in this county that connects to it, and there is no hand at the far end of that bell, and there never has been, and nobody in this town has ever asked why, because a bell is a thing a town rings on a day it has decided to ring it on. He leaned on the third stroke. The leaning is shorter and takes less time. Nobody said that the bell was cracked any more; it was not said in this town after the twenty-fourth of Fallowmonth and it was not said anywhere else either, but that is a different county's business.

The sill was under his feet the whole time. It is a dressed block and forty yards of tarred hemp through the abutment on a splice cut out of a cooper's bin, and the fourth course above it is open the whole depth of the joint the width of a hand, in register, and scarred, and it was left open, by a vote of nine men on the fourth of Frostmonth, on the casting vote of a reeve, over the objection of a man who keeps eels, so that a man standing on that deck could look down and see the mark. The stone was cold. There is no glass in this county and there will not be a third seat.

The line went out at the fourth hour, and Tovan Kest held this end of it standing in the gateway on Weir Street, and his brother took the other and went.

And then the yard stood there.

There is no gate at this end. There has never been a gate at this end; the arrangement is a line and two hands and a date and a witness at each end, and the gate is at the far end and the gate is five lines pressed in wet clay by a thumb in a county that is not on any map in this kingdom, and **nothing whatever happens at this end until the far end decides to open it, and the far end decides it on the day, in its own hour, in its own county's morning, and a county that has never refused anything in its life is going to take its time.**

It took about four minutes. Four minutes in a gateway in Weir Street in a hard month, with four men and a boy and a reeve with a sheet, and a rope that was doing nothing at all.

Tovan Kest, who had been saying nothing for about a minute and a half, said the one sentence in that yard that was not about a road, and he said it to his brother at the gate with his hand on the rope and his eyes on the far bank of the channel, and he said it in the flat voice he used for figures.

"The yard is a fortnight. It was a fortnight on Friday of last week and it is a fortnight today, and when you come back it will be a fortnight or it will not be, and if it is not then I will have had to put a man off, and it will be Tarrow, because Tarrow is the only one of the four with a barge and a barge can go to the county town. I will not be able to pay him and he will not be able to ask me, because that is not a thing a man asks. **I am telling you now so that on the day you come back and I say nothing about it, you will not think it was your brother.**"

"Understood," said Aren Kest.

"Good."

Then the line went, and it went out of a yard on Weir Street at the fourth hour of a market morning on the fourth of Hearthmonth, and there is a page at the head of the yard's book for the week of the fourth that has nine lines in it in Toban's own hand with the hour against each one, and it is the fourth time a page like that has been made in that book, and it says:

*Fourth of Hearthmonth. Third hour. Line made up, nine minutes, splice out of a bin, terms read out at the reeve's request, five lines, the second line read twice at the boy's asking. Fourth hour. The bell went at a quarter to, leaned on the third. The line went out at the fourth. Witness taken and signed. The reeve declined the fourth line and the reason is entered in the bench's roll and not in this book, because a reason is a thing believed and this book holds things set.*

---

He came up out of it on **clay steps**, and put his right foot back to find the edge, and there was no edge, the road behind him was the length of a cart and then it was the third step, and the weather was flat yellow light off a hill that stops at four hundred yards and everything in it the colour of a kiln, and the river made almost no noise and ran the colour of milk over a working ground, and the air was full of the smell of nine days of something being fired.

He had not been here since the first of Fallowmonth, and on that morning a woman had asked him one question and he had said no and she had said that was what the last one said too, and he had not gone through, and he had said out loud on a bridge in a market that she did not open it and she was right, and I am not the man who sends anybody, and it made no difference, which is how I know she was right.

It was the fourth of Hearthmonth and the road opened, and the road opened because the county's terms are the gate and the county's terms had been read out loud on this bridge on the twenty-third day of his last time here by a stranger in a hat, and because a county wrote down what it wanted and a town wrote down what it wanted, and the two of them did not have to want the same thing, and one of them had said *the road is not to be made to carry a person* and he had come on his own legs with nothing on him but a bag and a coat.

The hand of the Ninebridge was on the second span with a roll of tools at her hip and had been there since the second hour.

And beside her was a person he had never met, and he did not know the name, because it had been at the top of a piece of paper in a hand that is not a clerk's since the eighth of Embermonth, in the order it was given, and he had copied it into a notebook and had not used it once.

They did not say it. Nobody on this bridge said a name. The second of the five lines is forty-one words long and it is the reason a morning in a market town is quiet: **no name is to be read out on the far side of the line by anybody from the north, and no name is to be written in the line's book at the north end.** He had read that line out loud on the ninth of Embermonth in a gateway in a fen county in a market four hundred miles from here, in front of four men, and one of those four had wanted to write down the name at the top of the paper, and nobody had, and the not writing had gone into the yard's book as a fact and not as a want.

So the far end of the line was a woman with a set of tile-cutters' tools at her hip and a person the county had chosen, and neither of them said a word to him that was not about the road, and he did not ask either of them anything, and the two of them had been introduced to each other in about nine seconds by a person who introduced them by what they did.

"Up," said the hand of the Ninebridge. "You are on the boards at the low end of the rack in an hour. Bel's check round is the fourth row and if you go in the wrong place she will tell you and you will do what she tells you."

"How long."

"Nine months and six days," she said, "and then it is not yours to decide, and the north end is a bench and the north end is four hundred miles off, and if a thing goes wrong at your end then it is a bench's business and a bench can be wrong in a hall in front of witnesses, which is the only kind of wrong that this county has ever had and it has had nineteen years of it."

She looked at him for about two seconds.

"You stood on my kerb on the first morning," she said, "and I asked you one question and you answered it and I did not believe you and I was right not to and I have not asked you it again, and I am not going to now. Do you want to ask me one thing."

"Yes."

"Then ask it now and be quick, because there are about four hundred people in this market and two of them have noticed you and neither of us is a person anybody in this county has got a name for."

He asked it.

"How long has that thing been on your coping," he said.

"Forty-one years," said the hand of the Ninebridge, "and I have asked two men in this county what it is and one of them said his grandfather put it there and the other one said he did not know, and the man whose grandfather put it there has been dead since the eighth of the last rolling. **And I have never once asked anybody to take it down and I never will, and you have known what it is since the first morning and you did not say, and I did not ask, and that is the whole of what has happened between us in four months, and I would like it to go on being the whole of it.**"

"Agreed," said Aren Kest.

And she went to work, and the person the county chose went with her, and a market went on over a bridge with nine spans, and a road that opens was worth less in this book than a road that is refused, and about four hundred people in a county that had never once refused anything in nineteen years went to work on the fourth of Hearthmonth without one of them ringing a bell, because there is no bell in this county and there never has been one, and nobody there has ever had a reason to know it.
