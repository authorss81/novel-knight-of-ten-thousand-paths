# Chapter 0093

The ninth of Greenmonth was a Tuesday and it was a market day, and the county council sat at the fourth hour in a room over a court in the county town with the shutters up because the weather had turned, and about eleven people were in it, and the two things on the table were a returnable form with a figure in it and a resolution of a council, and neither of them was about a bridge.

Lysa Fenn had been a magistrate of this county for nine years and had never once sat in that room as a woman with a paper, and the difference was visible to everybody in about four minutes, and the clerk of the county council, who was fifty-eight and had been her clerk for twenty-two of the years, noticed it before anybody said anything and put a chair at the table for her without being asked, which is a thing a clerk does and cannot afterwards say he did not do.

The form came first.

It is a returnable form of the Office of Provincial Registration, of a series whose reverse nobody in this kingdom can explain, and it asks for a figure: **the number of persons in this county who have had knowledge of a declared object.** The first one, on the same series, was dated the twelfth of Greenmonth, YR 312, and was on her desk by the fifteenth of that month, and was returnable on or before the ninth of Frostmonth, and she let it expire in nine minutes **on the twenty-first of Wolfmonth, eighteen days before the day it was returnable**, in a room with the guild's own notice in front of her, and she did it by discovering that a returnable paper with no party in it expires, and a paper with nobody in default is not refused, and that construction is still the best piece of law she has ever done.

This is the second one. It was proposed in three lines on the thirteenth of Wolfmonth and it was returnable on the ninth of Greenmonth, and it had a space for a figure and no space for a name, and the space for a name is not there because a figure does not need one.

**The figure came back from a filing room in Merefen, and it is lawful, and it is a figure, and it has no name in it, and it is the most frightening document Lysa Fenn has ever had in her hand, and she has been frightened of four things in eleven years.**

---

**It is one thousand six hundred and nine.**

The return is signed by the keeper of the Bellweather Claims Archive, a woman of twenty-nine who has been in that office four years and a half, and it carries a claim number against the office and nothing against any person, and it says in the ordinary form that the declared object is that office and its records, and that the number of persons in this county who have had knowledge of it is one thousand six hundred and nine, and that the figure is derived from the office's own day-book for the four years and a half of its existence, and that a day-book records every person who comes through a door and the hour they come, and that the office has never had a reason to keep that as a figure and has had it for four years and a half.

There is a second paragraph and the second paragraph is the one Lysa Fenn read four times.

*That the office has no power to check the figure and has not attempted to check it, and that a count of persons is a count of persons and not a return of names, and that this office has in four years and a half entered every person who came through that door in a day-book in a form that cannot be served, and that this office has never once entered a person's name against a figure, and that it is not going to begin in the ninth month of a year in which it has been asked twice by a province.*

And there is a third paragraph, and the third paragraph is four words long, and Lysa Fenn read it and then sat back.

*Entered against no person. No opinion.*

---

**There was a man in that room who wanted a name, and he is a member of this council and has been a member of it for nineteen years, and he is a shipwright by trade, and he is not a fool, and the thing he said is in the minute because the clerk of the county council writes down what is said and not what is agreed.**

"Make them produce the book."

"Which book," said Lysa Fenn.

"The book the figure comes out of. They have told us this room in a letter in a plain hand that the figure is a count of people at a door, out of a day-book kept four years and a half. A council may require the production of a record. That is a power this council has had since before I was born and it has used it twice, both times about a bridge, and both times it got the book."

"That is correct," said Lysa Fenn. "You may require the production of a record."

"Then require it."

"I have," said Lysa Fenn, "and it is of no use whatsoever, and here is the reason, and I am going to give it to you in the order it happened, because the order is the whole of it.

"A day-book is not a file. A file is where a fact about a person goes, and a file can be produced, and a court can order a file, and anybody in this county may be shown a file. **A day-book is the only book in that office that nobody can serve.** It is not privileged and nobody has ever claimed it was. It is simply that the one line in it that matters is a line with a number against the office and nothing against any person, and the office does not pretend that line is about a person, and so there is nothing in the book to produce *about* anybody, and a council that requires the production of a book and gets a book with nobody in it has been given a paper and not a person.

"**And that is not a trick and it is not a lawyer's flourish. It is four and a half years of a woman refusing to make one kind of document so that when somebody came with a form she would have one kind of document to hand them, and I have just watched it work, and it has worked, and the figure on the table is the result of it, and I could not do a single thing about the figure.**"

"Then the council has a number and cannot use it," said the shipwright.

"The council has a number and cannot use it," said Lysa Fenn, "**and neither can anybody else, and that is not the same as there being no number, and in about four years somebody is going to find the difference.**"

---

The room took about nine minutes over it and then Lysa Fenn stopped it.

"Read the form's own construction," she said. "Not mine. Its. I read myself a construction on the twenty-first of Wolfmonth that took this court nine minutes and it was the best work of my life and I have not been able to improve on it, and I am not going to improve on it. I am going to use it again, and I am going to be frightened of using it again, and I am going to do it in front of you so that you can see a judge do it."

She put her finger on the middle of the form.

"A count of persons is a return of a figure. A figure is not a person. **No person is in default, because no person is named, and a thing against which nobody is in default is not refused and is not enforced, and therefore the instrument is spent at the moment it is answered, and there is no second date on it and no returnable term, and there is nothing in this kingdom that can require another answer to it.** I can say that in one sentence and I have been saying it since the twenty-first of Wolfmonth and it is the answer to every instrument this office or any other has ever put in front of me."

"Stop," said Marda Oakes, from the second chair on the left, and she was on her feet. "Say what that does, and then say it twice, because I want to be sure I have it."

"It destroys the count," said Lysa Fenn. "It has destroyed it. It went out of Merefen this morning and it can never be asked again and there is nobody to punish for it and nobody to appeal, and by Friday it will be a form in a press in a provincial town with a figure in it and no name attached to it and no way on earth to find out who made it, and that is the instrument finished, and it is the only instrument I have ever beaten, and I have been doing it by reading the other side's own paper back to it."

"And what else does it do."

**Lysa Fenn sat down, and then she stood up again, because she was not going to say the second half of it sitting down.**

"It does one more thing and I have been not saying it since the eighth hour of this morning and I am saying it now. Every argument I have ever had with an instrument of that office has been built on one fact, and the fact is that **this court is not a person.** A court is a room and nine men and a seal and a fee, and a court cannot be sued, and a court cannot be refused, and a court cannot be injured, and a court cannot be a party to anything, and that is why a notice nailed to a wall only stops people who read it, and I have been proud of that for nine years and I have said so out loud in a room to about four people.

"And in about four years somebody is going to make it a person. A corporation. A body with a door and a number that can be served and can sue and can be sued, and the day that happens every argument I have ever won comes apart, because the whole of them was that a thing which cannot be served cannot be reached. **And the first county in this kingdom to build one will not be a madman. It will be a woman of forty-five who has spent eleven years being unable to make a document do anything, who is right about the mechanism, and who will be quoted in nine years as the woman who saw it coming.**"

Nobody in the room said anything for long enough that the clerk of the county council put his pen down and waited to be told.

"That is not in a minute," said Marda Oakes.

"No," said Lysa Fenn. "It is not."

---

**And she said the other half of it, out loud, once, because she had decided in the last four seconds that she would, and because there were eleven people in a room and about four of them were owed the shape of the thing they had just voted on.**

"I have wanted to make the law useful to people who cannot afford a title or a patron since I was twenty-two," she said, "and I have been at it for nine years on a salary that the reeve's office has twice called a discouragement in a budget, and I have never once been appointed to anything, and my family has never once been appointed to anything, and I have done about four useful things.

"This is the fifth useful thing and it is a door with a number on it, and I did not build it and I would not have built it, and the woman who did build it wants to sue this county and is right to, and if anybody in this room thinks I have done this for the office I would like them to look at the deed first and then at the resolution, because the deed is thirteen words long and it does not mention the office at all, and the resolution is four hundred words long and it mentions the office in every line.

"**A thing that can be sued is a thing that can be shut, and I would like that written on the back of this form in somebody's hand, and I am not going to be the somebody, and I have asked for it three times.**"

The clerk of the county council said, "I have been asked three times and I am not going to write it on the back of a county form."

"Then write it in the book," said Lysa Fenn, "and the book is the minute, and the minute is the only book in this county that anybody has ever made anybody read."

---

**The second thing on the table was the resolution, and the clerk of the county council read it out in the flat voice of a man who reads, and he did not improve one word of anybody's sentence.**

It resolved that the Bellweather Claims Archive, being an office of this county that holds records relating to persons, be and is hereby made **a body corporate**, with a door, a number, and the capacity to sue and to be sued; and it carried, at the foot, a form with a printed space headed *Function of the office*, and the space had been filled in, in the founding's own words, and **the space above the ruled line had been left empty**, and the reason the space above the ruled line had been left empty is that on a form of that series the space above the ruled line is for the name of a thing and the space below it is for what the thing is for, and the form has no way of being wrong about that and everybody who fills one in in a hurry gets it the other way up.

What was in the filled space is the founding's own words, taken from the deed of four years and a half ago, and there are thirteen of them and they are the sentence a founder wrote in a room with a clerk in it: *for the keeping of what this county cannot be shown it has lost.*

"There is the whole of it," said Marda Oakes. "I asked for a number. I got a number. There is a number on that form and it is the number I asked for in a letter in Wolfmonth, and the office that filled it in did not know what I was doing and neither do I, and I have wanted this since the eighth of Embermonth and I have wanted it because I am right about the mechanism and I am going to say the mechanism now so that it is in a room."

She put her hand flat on the form.

"I have spent eleven years being unable to make a document do anything," she said. "A county council can write to a keeper of records. It can send a woman to a room with a paper. It can say *will you produce this* and get *no* written in a book in about nine months, and then what the council has is a woman who said no, and a woman who said no can be told she said no, and the county has no remedy at all, and it will find another thing to do.

A thing with a door and a number can be made to pay a man who has been damaged, and a county does not have to be right. It only has to have somebody in it that a court can write to. **I have wanted that for eleven years, and I am not going to say the rest of the sentence, and the rest of the sentence is the price, and somebody in this room knows the price and nobody is going to say it either.**"

The clerk of the county council wrote it down.

He read it back, and he read back the resolution and the two writs on the table and the finding of the fourth of Greenmonth, YR 312, clause by clause, and he did not improve one word of anybody's sentence, and he did not strike one word either, and at the foot of the minute, where the space is for the person who does not agree, he wrote *no dissent recorded*, and then he stopped, and asked whether anybody wished to dissent, and four people in the room looked at one another and nobody said the word, and he wrote the hour.

---

**And at about the sixth hour, on the way out, in a yard, Lysa Fenn said the sentence that nobody had said in the room, out loud, once, to one person, and it was the last thing she said that day and she has repeated it in a room twice since and both times she has said it exactly the same way.**

"**A body corporate can be sued,"** she said. "**A keeper can only be dismissed.**"

And Marda Oakes, who is right about the mechanism and wrong about the office and has been eleven years in a council that cannot make a document do anything, stood in a yard in the county town and did not answer it, and went to find the cart, because it was going to rain and there were eleven miles of bad road between that yard and Merefen and she had a Thursday in the market at the far end of it.
