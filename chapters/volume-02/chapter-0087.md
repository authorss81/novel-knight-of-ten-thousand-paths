# Chapter 0087

A woman of about sixty stood up in the long room at the assizes in the ninth month of the year and said the sentence that closed an argument nobody in that county had started, and the sentence was this:

**"We did not lose our register. We lost our refusal. A register is what you have instead."**

She is on the roll. She has been on three hearths in this county and has buried two men and the fourth column of her tile says *wife*, which is not a trade, and there is no column in the County of Ash for a person whose trade is being a person, and she is the second entry in nineteen years in that office against a person by name that is not a removal, the first being a kiln-worker of fifty-eight who asked to be entered by somebody who was not him while he was still able to ask.

Nobody asked her name. She gave it into the room, once, when the clerk asked, and the clerk wrote it, and it went into a page headed with a number that nobody had ever put at the top of a page before, and that is all that happened to it.

---

The magistrate called the room on purpose, and he put the reason on the table in three sentences and then he went and stood at the back of it and did not sit down for about two hours, and a magistrate who stands at the back of a room of fifty people in his own county in the ninth month of a year is doing a thing that is minuted and that he is not minuting.

"Colm Arreld called it and the reason he called it is in the book, and here it is in his own words and I am reading it because he asked me to read it and not to summarise it."

*I call this room because we are about to make a roll that is kept in three places, and two of the three are in this room, and the third is a letter, and a county that has made a thing like that in the ninth month ought to be able to say in one sentence what it is making it out of. We have four pages of a physician's finding in a drawer and a list of two hundred and six names in a press that is a year old, and a county of nine hundred and forty-one hearths, and a man off a road who has been here eight months. I want the room to say the sentence. That is all I have asked for and I have asked it in a room and not in a corridor because the last time I asked a question in a corridor it cost this county a fortnight and me a fortnight, and I have learned one thing in nine years and it is that everything I have ever done in a corridor was worse.*

Nobody in that room said the sentence for about an hour and a half.

The argument they had instead was, and this is exactly what the argument was, and it is worth printing because it is the shape of the thing the whole volume is about: **they could not agree on a word for what had happened to them.**

They had plenty of words. They had *list* and *book* and *roll* and *column* and *stamp* and *the year of the taking* and *the year the register went*, and about eleven men said those words in about forty minutes and every one of them was describing a procedure and not an event, and at no point in forty minutes did anybody in that room say a word for *a thing that was done to this county by somebody and could not be refused*.

And a man at the loading end, who is the loudest man in a kiln and is not the loudest man in that room, put his hands flat on the table and said:

"You have not got one. That is what I have been listening to for about an hour. **Forty minutes and eleven words and not one of them is a word for a thing that was done to us.** Every one of them is a word for a book. And a county that cannot name a thing that was done to it is going to get it done to it again, and it is going to call it a list when it happens, because that is the only word it has got, and that is how you get a roll instead of a word."

"I did not lose a book," said the woman of about sixty. "I want to be exact, because I have had four of them in my house and I buried two men and I have buried a sister and I know what a thing taken out of a house is. **We did not lose our register. We lost our refusal.** A man came with something and this county had a thing it could have said, and it did not say it, and the thing it could have said was a no, and there is no word for a no in this county that does not also mean lost, and so we did not say it, and then the register was gone and we have spent nineteen years building a roll.

"A register is what you have instead. That is all it is. It is the thing you build with your hands for nineteen years so that you will not have to say no to anybody ever again, and it has not worked, and every single one of us in this room knows it has not worked, and about four hundred and nine of us have been put back on it in the last eleven weeks and it is the best thing that has happened in this county since I was a young woman and it is still the wrong shape and I am going to say why it is the wrong shape, and I am not going and I have not got a trade for it."

Nobody said anything for about eleven seconds.

"Say it," said the man at the loading end.

"I am not going to. I have said the shape of it and I have said it in the only words I have got and I am a woman of sixty and I have never in my life had a better sentence than that one and it is about a roll, and I am going to sit down."

---

The hand of the Ninebridge said the other one, and she said it standing, and she said it about four minutes in, in the middle of an hour and a half, and it is the sentence the whole of this volume's road is built on and it is the first time in this volume that it has been said in a room by the person it belongs to, and about nine people in that room had heard it in some form and none of them had heard it in that room.

"A hand answers for a road and a hand can be asked. Those are two halves and everybody always puts them together and they do not go together.

"A hand that answers for a road is a thing. You have got one. It is me and it is a good word and it means I keep four hundred yards of tiled kerb and a man can turn a stone over with a heel and read who cut it and when, and that is the only non-argument in this county and I have wanted it for nine years.

"**A hand can be asked is the other half, and I have been the hand of the Ninebridge for nine years and nobody has ever asked me one single question about it.** Not the magistrate, not the rollkeeper, not the physician, not the bench, not a man off a road. Nine years. Nobody in this county has ever had anything they wanted to know about this bridge, because there is nothing they could do with the answer. **A hand that cannot be asked is not a hand. It is a person holding something.**

"And you have not got a word for the person who answers, and you have not got a word for the person who asks, and it is not because this county is poor or cut off or behind a river. It is because there has never been anything in this county that could be asked. Nothing was ever refused here. Nothing came here with a paper. Nothing was ever served. So there was nothing to be answerable for, and so nobody had to answer, and so nobody learned how, and so when a thing came that needed a person to stand up in front of it and say a word, this county had nine hundred and forty-one hearths and about a thousand and a half people who could read and not one person in it who had ever said the word out loud.

"**That is why there has never been a hand here that had to answer. Not because we are better than other counties. Because nothing has ever been put in front of us.**"

Somebody at the back said, "We have been the hand that does not answer for nine years then."

"Yes," said the hand of the Ninebridge, "and about four hundred and nine people have been through a room in eleven weeks, and a girl of nineteen has read about four hundred names out loud four times a day for eleven weeks, and a magistrate has read out the argument of a man in a city that is not on any map, and a physician has been read to in this county for the first time in four years, and every one of those things is somebody standing up in front of something and saying a word.

"So I am not going to tell anybody in this room that we have no word for a refusal. **We have had four hundred and nine of them since the sixth month. We have had them in this room, at this table, in these two hours. What we do not have is a word for what we lost, and that is not the same thing, and I have watched about forty people in this room tonight get them the wrong way round in their heads, and the two are so different that the difference is the whole of what any of us has done for nine years.**"

---

The physician's four pages were read out again, and four pages of them were read out, and this time nobody misunderstood them.

That is the sentence the eighth month and the ninth month of this year have bought, and it was not bought by a stranger and it was not bought by a physician, and there is nobody in this volume who is going to be thanked for it.

Hesper Lound read the four pages himself, at about the third hour, in a room of about fifty people, in a county where the same four pages were read out loud once in four years in the twelfth month to a man with a sett-cutter's bag, and were misunderstood twice in the same hour, cleverly by the keeper of the roll and kindly by the magistrate.

He was not misunderstood on the second reading. Nobody argued with him. Nobody misunderstood him. A man at the back of the room said, about the second page, "That is the reading," and the physician said, "Yes," and the man at the back said, "Then we have been doing it since the sixth month four times a day," and about eleven people in that room said nothing, and one of them was the keeper of the roll.

"You have been doing it since the sixth month," said Hesper Lound. "And I want the room to hear what I am going to say and I want it entered and I want the clerk to write it as I say it and not tidy it. **I wrote that in a drawer for four years and I was right and I was not listened to, and I have spent four years being the only person in this county who knew what the marks were doing, and the man off the road read it out in this room in the twelfth month and put it in my hands in front of four people, and I have got exactly one thing to say about that, which is that the county is not to say that a stranger found it.**

"The county found it. **A county that has been made into marks for nineteen years and has spent four months being read four times a day cannot hear the second page of a finding without understanding it, and that is not a thing about me. That is a thing about what you have been doing for eleven weeks.** I have been in this county nineteen years and I have never once been understood in about four seconds, and the reason is that in four years I have never once had anything to be right about that the room was also doing.

"I am sixty-three and I have had a drawer in a table for four years and I have had it out for four months and I have not put it back, and it is on this table, and anybody in this room may pick it up."

Nobody picked it up.

---

Which left the thing that has been standing in the middle of this county since the fifth month, and which nobody has ever been able to say out loud, and which the cord-cutter said out loud in about forty seconds without being asked and then sat down.

He is a cord-cutter. He is fifty-one and he has been in this county forty years and he has no other trade and there is no column in this county for a man who cuts cord, and the fourth column of his tile says *cord-cutter* and that is a trade and it is a good one and it is not a thing anybody could explain in a hall in a fen county.

"In the fifth month," he said, "about two hundred of us was asked one time each, in our own sheds, by me and by the ash-driver, in about a minute and a half a house. The question was *are you on this list.* That was all of it. Nobody asked anybody what they wanted. The answers went in my own book in my own hand and I shut the book, and it is in a box in my shed under the bench, and it is not in this room, and it is not in the assizes, and it is not in the roll, and it is not going anywhere, and nobody has ever asked me for it and nobody ever will.

"And I have not read it. **I have not read that book since the fifth month and it is in a box under a bench in my shed and I am not going to read it and if anybody in this county wants it they can come and get it off me and I will hand it over, and nobody has come, and a woman came in the eighth month and asked me if I still had it and I said yes, and she said nothing else and went out, and I would like that in the book here tonight, and the clerk can put it in, because in about nine years somebody is going to write that the answers were collected and they are going to want to know where they went and the answer is that they went in a box in a shed and a woman asked a man in a doorway and said nothing else and went out.**"

Somebody said, "Why did she not ask for it?"

And the cord-cutter said, "I do not know and I have thought about it for a month and I have got to the bottom of it and the bottom of it is that she did not ask for it because she wanted to know it was there." And then he said, in a slightly different voice:

"And I will tell the other thing, because this room is going to have it out of me by morning and I would rather have it out of me now. **The physician says a mark that is written down and not kept anywhere is a mark that gets read more often and not less, and he said that in this room in the fifth month and he was right and I have not been able to put it down.**

"We wrote two hundred answers in two hundred books. That is two hundred marks, in two hundred houses, in the hand of the man who asked, and every one of those books is going to be read at that man's table, and then it is going to be read in that man's house, and then it is going to be read by whoever is in the next room, and I have been in about nine of those houses since the fifth month and I can tell you that the answer *yes* has already been said out loud in a kitchen in this county, twice, and I was not there for either and I heard about both.

"So the physician is right. **And the physician's cure is three copies in three hands and three places, and I have been in a hall tonight with a woman of sixty who has told us we built a register instead of saying no, and I would like somebody to explain to me, in this county, in a shed, at a cost, what the difference is between a thing a man writes in his own hand in his own house and a thing a woman presses into a board with her own thumb.**

"I have been asked twice since the fifth month whether the books are a good thing and both times by people who had not read the third page. I have not got an answer and I have had four months to get one."

Nobody in that room answered him, and the chapter is the whole of that not being answered, because the physician's objection was made in the fifth month by a man in a room with a stove in it, and it was right then, and it is right now, and about four hundred and nine people have been through a room since then and the fastest mark in the County of Ash is a list of restorations read aloud four times a day by a girl of nineteen, and **the physician is a sixty-three-year-old man who has been right about this county for four years and has had four pages in a drawer and about a week to think, and he is not going to be right about it in a hall tonight, and nobody is going to make him, and the one piece of civic work in this volume that nobody has ever written down is two hundred books in two hundred sheds and it is the best thing anybody in this county has ever done and it may be a fire.**

---

They read out the minutes at about the sixth hour, because the clerk reads the minutes, and there were about nine of them out of the long arguments, and the last one is the reason the room is not going to be quoted anywhere, and the magistrate read it out himself and said it was his.

*I am asked to minute the sense of this room. I have been in this room four hours. I am not able to minute the sense of this room and I will not minute a resolution of it, and the reason is that this is the fifth year I have sat in a chair in this county and I have had four arguments in front of me in it and three of them were about a list and one of them was about a bench, and I am not going to sit here and take a fifth and call it the sense of a room of fifty people on a wet evening in the ninth month, and if anybody in this county wants to know what we have agreed tonight they may have it out of me one at a time, in this room, with the clerk writing, and it will take until the next low water and there is nothing else in this county I have to do.*

The clerk wrote that down and read it back and nobody asked him to read it back.

And then the man at the loading end said, "Colm Arreld, that was the best minute anybody has ever entered in that book and you did not take a vote and I want to say why I voted for you being a magistrate nine years ago, and it is that, and you have not noticed it for nine years and you were not going to."

And Colm Arreld said, "Then I have had nine good years and I did not notice one of them, and I am going to notice it now, and I am going to go and stand at the back of the room again, because I have been standing at the back of it for two hours and a man in this county who has said what I have just said should stand at the back and let about fifty people look at him."

And he did.
