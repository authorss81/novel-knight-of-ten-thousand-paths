# Chapter 0089

The county's day in the ninth month was not a battle. It was a morning in a long room with a stove in it, three signatures, two columns on a board, a price of tile, a vote that was lost, and about two hundred and fifty-four tiles broken up and wedged back by thirteen men who had not gone back, and none of it took a single thing off any of the eleven who were in the room.

Pernel Quist refused for two days.

She refused in the ordinary way, which is that she said nothing on the first day and came at the ordinary hour on the second and asked for the sheet back to see whether the third hand was still a letter, and it was, and she read it, and she put it face down on the table and squared it, which is a thing a keeper does.

"It is a letter," she said.

"It is," said Colm Arreld.

"Then I have said what I said in the twenty-second week and I have said it once and I am not going to say it twice to a room." She squared the sheet again. "I said I would not sign a thing with a letter in it. I said it in a long room with about nine people in it and the clerk wrote it down and read it back and I corrected a word. **I have been right about refusing twice in nine years and both times I was wrong, and I have said that in a book in a corridor and in this room, and I am not going to have the day I said it put in a minute as though saying it had been work.**"

Nobody said anything for about nine seconds.

"Then here is the rest of it and it is four sentences and I have had two days," said Pernel Quist. "**A roll that can be wrong is not a weaker roll. It is a roll that has to be read twice, and a roll that has to be read twice cannot be read by one man in a morning.**

"Nineteen years I have kept a roll that cannot be read twice. I have kept it in one room in one building with a box under the table, and it is the only thing in this county that does not move, and I have been proud of that every day of nineteen years, and about four hundred people of this county have gone through a room in nine days because of it, and one of them was a man of fifty-eight who does not know which kiln he works in and is on this roll correctly, and the roll is right about him, and it was right about him in the ninth week of the rite as well, and it is going to be right about him in nine years.

"A roll that cannot be read twice cannot survive a person. **And this county has just had a person in it for nine days and I have been sitting in a room with a box under the table while it happened, and I have been right, and my being right is the reason.**"

She put her hand flat on the sheet.

"The rule is that a person is restored when two of the three agree and removed when two of the three agree otherwise, and the third is allowed to be wrong. That is a physician's fourth page, it is four years old, it was in a drawer, and I have had it copied out twice and I have disagreed with it for four years on the grounds that a system which permits an error is not a system. I was wrong about the third hand in the twenty-second week and I was not wrong about the anchor and I am going to have both of those in the book in my own hand and I am not going to have one of them tidied into the other."

"Then you will sign it," said the magistrate.

"I will sign it," said Pernel Quist, "and I will sign it with a letter in it, and I would like the minute to say that the third hand is a letter, and that it is a letter because a bench in this county lost a vote about it on the day before yesterday, and that I have signed a roll whose third hand is a letter on the ground that the rule permits the third to be wrong, and that a rule which permits the third to be wrong is the only reason I have been able to put my name on it at all."

The clerk wrote all of it in a flat unhurried hand and read it back and she corrected one word and the word she corrected was *weaker*.

---

The bench argued about the price of tile for about an hour and won a reduction and lost a vote, and both of those went in the same minute and neither was the important part of the day.

The reduction: the price of a tile to a household goes down a farthing, and it goes down because about four hundred and nine people have been put back on a roll in nine days and every one of them needs one, and the fourth column has never been cheaper to fill in this county, and the bench does not put that in the minute and the reason it does not put that in the minute is the reason a bench in this county has never once in nineteen years put anything of its own reasons in a minute, which is that a reason is a thing believed and a minute is a thing set.

The vote: whether to send a man of this county to a city four hundred miles off to fetch eleven sheets out of a press in a room with a door on a lane, so that the third hand of the roll in three hands can be a shelf instead of a letter.

**Lost.** Four for, three against, and the clerk of the roll did not vote and said so, and the reason she did not vote is in the minute and is the volume's whole subject in a keeper's mouth: she is one of the three hands and a hand that votes on whether it is a hand is not a hand.

And the reasons given against were three and all three were trade.

The second: **the fifth of this county's own terms says the road is kept by a hand at each end and a witness at each end, and there is no witness at this end, and a man cannot walk out of a county he cannot be fetched out of.** He would have to go on the road, and the road is a line and two hands and a date and a witness at each end, and the witness at Ash's end was a person the county chose and that person was struck in the eighth month and the county did not replace him in time, and that is nine days ago, and nobody in this room is going to stand on that bridge and pretend the two facts are unrelated.

The third, from the man at the loading end, and it is in the minute whole:

"**A promised hand is not a hand, and a promised hand that costs a man his season is not a promised hand, it is a list.** We have had a list. I have been in a kiln shed with a list of about two hundred on it for four years and I have watched what it does, and a list is what a county makes when it cannot pay a man for a day, and we have spent nineteen years learning that a county which cannot pay a man for a day does not beg, it makes a list, and a promised hand four hundred miles off that costs one of thirteen men a season is the same list with a nicer name on it, and I am not going to be the man who gives this county that."

And the minute ends, in the clerk's hand, in one line: *The bench has resolved that no man shall be sent. The bench has entered that it is unable to say whether the third hand is a hand, and that it has resolved not to find out this year.*

---

**The kilns reopened on a schedule of two thirds.**

The one at the north village, which had fired out a half-charged rolling in the twelfth month of the long middle and had nine days of wages for a kiln that will not be paid for nine days, went to two thirds on a schedule in a book with the hour against it. The one at the east village, which was not charged and was left, was not re-lit, and that was in the book in one line, and the reason was in one line, and the reason was that there is no wood and the wood is a season.

The other two came to two thirds together. About forty men had gone out in the twelfth month. The two live kilns took twenty-seven of them in the fifteenth week. **About thirteen did not go back, and the thirteen are thirteen, and the number is written down in the county's own book in a keeper's hand with the date and the hour on it, and the hand that wrote it did not write one word of an opinion, and thirteen men out of a county of nine hundred and forty-one hearths is the only figure in this county that everybody has agreed on without an argument and it is agreed because it is the ugliest one.**

---

And then, in the ninth month, in a shed at the back of the Kilnholt kiln, two hundred and fifty-four fired tiles were broken up and the clay wedged back.

The man with the ledger got the number and it is worth saying exactly how, because he is the only man in this county who counts and he counts fires and not himself and has been doing it for nineteen years, and for the first time in nineteen years he was asked to count something that was not a fire.

"There is a figure in this county of four hundred and fifty-six," he said. "It was said out loud in a kiln shed in the first month of a stranger's time here, by me, because a stranger asked me a question, and a room of nine people stopped what it was doing, and the man who asked it worked it out in a minute and a half before I got to it. It has not been said since. It is a floor of a surplus and I do not know where the rest of it is and neither does he and he has written that down in a book of his own.

"**I have not used it. I have not worked back from it. I have not divided it by anything and I have not multiplied it by anything and I have not entered it on this slate, and I have been asked three times today whether my number is the same as that number and the answer is no, and the reason the answer is no is that that one is every tile over the number of people and this one is a count of tiles with a second hand on the back of them, and one of those is a thing about a shortage and the other one is a thing about a person.**

"I got this one by walking the walls with a lamp and turning tiles over. There is a hand on the back of a tile and it belongs to the person. A soft tile can be re-pressed by anybody with a thumb and a wet cloth. A fired tile cannot be re-pressed. So a person who has been fired twice has **two tiles with the same hand on the back of them, and the two of them are in two different kilns, and the county has never counted them because the county counts fires and a fire is a kiln and a kiln is on a wall.**

"I got to two hundred and fifty-four."

The boy with the slate had it on the slate and the ash-driver stood and looked at it.

"Two hundred and fifty-four of what," said the ash-driver.

"Of tiles I have walked round and turned over," said the man with the ledger. "Two kilns and one village and about half of the wall of the third. **It is a floor and I will not go past it, and there is a stack in the back of the Kilnholt shed that I have not opened and I am not going to open it this month because I have a date in the fourth of this month and I am not opening a stack in the fourth of a month.** And it is not the same number as the four hundred and fifty-six and I have said so three times today and I would like the clerk to enter that I have said it three times."

---

The cost was put on a board in the ash-driver's hand and the boy with the slate copied it, and it is a cost and not a speech, and it foots.

*Two hundred and fifty-four fired tiles, broken by hand. There is no mill in this county and there is not going to be one; a tile is six inches by four and about two fingers thick and a man breaks one in about four seconds and there is no way round that and the county has looked for one for about a week.*
*Thirteen men, five days, at five pence the day: three hundred and twenty-five pence, six marks three shillings and one penny.*
*Wood for the proof rolling, nine cords, at the kiln gate, two shillings and sixpence the cord: two hundred and seventy pence, five marks two shillings and sixpence.*
*The whole of it: five hundred and ninety-five pence, twelve marks one shilling and seven pence.*

And under it, in the same hand, and the man at the cutting bench was not at the board and said afterwards that the two lines were not to be added and that he would hear about it if they were:

*It is being done in the standing water. It is being done now. The Kiln is up and the banks are under and there is no clay to dig and no wood worth burning and about thirteen men have not gone back since the twelfth month, and this is the only season in this county in which a man who is out of work and a room with a floor are both available at the same time.*

*It will be two years. There are two hundred and fifty-four and we have not got the rest of them and we are not going to get the rest of them this year. The county will do what it can find and the county will do it in this season and in the next one, and there is a bench, and there is a cost, and the cost is on this board.*

And the bench entered the cost in the county's own book, in one line, and it is the only line in the minute of that day with a figure against it that was not a signature: *the cost of the un-firing, being twelve marks one shilling and seven pence, and two years, and the first of it in the standing water.*

---

There is no month in this county that a clerk in another country could write down, and the county has said so out loud twice in a book, and anybody in Merefen who reads a list of restorations in a fair hand in a month or two is going to be told a season by a county that counts from its first firing and marks two low waters a year, and the season this county calls the standing water is the time between the second low water and the next first firing, when the Kiln is up over the working ground and nothing can be dug and nothing can be burned, and it is the dead season, and it is the only season in which a county can afford to do something that costs nothing but time and thirteen men's hands.

That is the permanent, slow, unglamorous, expensive act, and it is the only thing anybody in this county did in the ninth month that will still be true in nine years, and there is no speech about it and nobody is going to make one.

Aren Kest watched two hundred and fifty-four fired tiles go into a hopper in the shed at the back of the Kilnholt kiln on the fourth of the ninth month, and the clay go back onto the bank in a wedging shed, and he watched a man of about fifty-two put his thumb into wet clay and press round a mark and not on it, about forty times an hour, for five days.

It is the motion.

Not the work. The motion. A hand setting a thing true against a thing out of true — a hand under a deck a finger's width out, a thumb in a dish, a clamp bar. A woman in a filing room over a salt merchant in a fen county put a hundred and iron clamps straight a year for four years and one night in a room on her own with nobody watching straightened one that had been out of true for a year and had been left that way on purpose, and a man with a garboard in his hand did it on the third slip of a ferry yard in a city four hundred miles off, and it has now been done four hundred miles off again by a man of fifty-eight with a hod in his hands and a roll of tools at his hip.

**None of them has said one word about it to any of the others and none of them is going to.**

He did not tell the hand of the Ninebridge, because a sentence about a clamp is a sentence that goes into a book, and there is a woman in a book in a fen who has entered three times this year that the not writing is the part that is a decision. And she is four hundred miles off in a county that is on every map in this kingdom, and he is four hundred miles off in a county that is on none of them, and the reason neither of them is going to say anything about it is not modesty and it is not the volume's argument. It is that a clamp, a garboard and a thumb in a dish are the same movement, and a movement is not a fact, and a fact goes in a book and a movement does not, and if you put a movement in a book then a man in nine years can ask you what it was for and you will have to answer, and the answer is that it is the same thing done three times by three people who never met, and that is not an answer anybody in this county can use.

---

And at about the fourth hour of the same day, in the long room at the assizes, with the stove in it and about nine people in it and a girl of nineteen copying the roll at the end of the table, Colm Arreld stopped what he was doing and said:

"Loam."

She put her pen down.

"I want to say one thing to you and I am going to say it once and I am going to say it in a room and I am not going to write it in a minute, and the reason I am not going to write it in a minute is that a minute is a thing set and this is a thing I want to have said out loud in a room with about nine people in it so that about nine people heard it."

"The clerk said—"

"I do not care what the clerk said and I have been the magistrate of this county for nine years and I do not care what a clerk of mine says to a girl of nineteen in a corridor, and I have had it twice in the last nine weeks and I have let it stand both times because there are about nine things in this county I should have dealt with before the wages of an apprentice."

The room had gone completely quiet, and the kiln, four hundred yards off, made almost no noise, as it always does.

"You read a list of restorations out loud four times a day for eleven weeks, in a room in this county, and nobody had ever asked you to and nobody had told you that was a job you had, and you took it off a man on the third morning without a word being said about it and you got better at it every week for eleven weeks and you read two hundred and thirty-one names with their hearths and their trades attached at the speed of a woman reading out a row of things she has copied four hundred times and you did not get one wrong.

"**You were right to read it.**"

Fennie Loam did not say anything for about four seconds.

"Do I write that down?" she said.

"No," said Colm Arreld.

"Because I have a copy to give in and a copy has to have a name at the top of it and everything I hand in has to have somebody's authority on it, and I have had a magistrate say a thing to me in a room twice in nine weeks and both times it was not in a minute, and I would like to know now which of the two is the one I am allowed to write on a copy."

"You are allowed to write *the magistrate said so in a room and he is not minuting it*," said Colm Arreld, "and you are not allowed to write one word more than that, and if anybody in nine years asks you why there is no minute of it you will say that a minute is a thing set and I said it out loud, and that will be true, and it will be the whole of it, and you will be able to hand that copy in."

"Right," said Fennie Loam, and picked her pen up, and went back to the roll, and about four seconds afterwards she said, without looking up, "Thank you, magistrate," and he said, "That is the second time this year and I am not going to make a habit of it," and nobody in the room laughed, and about four of them did not go home for about a quarter of an hour.

And then the girl of nineteen went on copying the roll of persons of a county of nine hundred and forty-one hearths, in a room with a stove in it, in the ninth month, on an ordinary working morning, and she was right, and she was told so once, and it was not in a minute, and the minute has three signatures on it and the third hand is a letter, and the price of a tile has gone down a farthing, and two hundred and fifty-four fired tiles went into a hopper that afternoon and the clay went back onto a bank, and about thirteen men out of a county of nine hundred and forty-one hearths stayed out, and the number is written down.
