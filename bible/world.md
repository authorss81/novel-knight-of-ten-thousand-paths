# World Bible: Neyra

## World shape and tone

Neyra is a late-medieval, river-and-road world with hand tools, horse transport, water-powered mills, brass clocks, and small enchanted industries. Its magic is practical enough to mend a lock, warm a greenhouse, or hold a bridge against a flood, but never so convenient that ordinary labor disappears. The social center is a network of roads, bridges, markets, oath-stones, and local assemblies rather than a single imperial capital.

The story’s visual language is **weathered material made briefly luminous**: wet stone, toll-bell bronze, black river water, chalk dust, salt, lamp smoke, and pale seams in the air when a working fails. The world is lived-in and imperfect. People argue over tolls, inherit bad bridges, bury failed crops, and celebrate when a road stays open.

Neyra is not secretly modern. There are no firearms, mass printing, or instant communication. News travels by courier, barge, signal bell, and the slower routes of the Gatehouse. A competent rider can cover a region in weeks; a kingdom can still be divided by a washed-out bridge.

## Geography

### Merefen

A fen-side market town and Aren’s starting home. It sits where the Sable River divides into reed channels. Merchants bring peat, fish, salt, and glass, while local bridge crews repair the raised road. Merefen is prosperous enough to have opinions, poor enough that a closed road causes real hardship. Its people know Aren as a competent repairman before they know him as a disgraced captain.

### Bellweather and Alder Vale

Bellweather was a river city built around a massive arched bridge, a bell foundry, and a public archive. The Bellweather Sundering erased the city, the bridge, and seven nearby villages from the physical world. The name Bellweather survives in old songs, blank maps, and a river channel that locals avoid after dark. The path continuation called **Bellweather-Living** has a functioning bridge and a different political history.

Alder Vale is the fertile basin around the old city. It is the Crown’s most politically important breadbasket and therefore the first place officials prefer to call a natural loss.

### The Hearth Marches

Rolling farm country divided into household fields, communal woods, and seasonal oath-stones. Farmers use small workings to improve seed memory and keep wells from silting up. The Marches are politically fractured between hereditary landlords, tenant leagues, and traveling bridge crews. Their first volume-level conflict is whether to sacrifice a village’s old boundary oath to keep a shared river alive.

### The Glass Steppe

A high, wind-scoured plateau where volcanic glass grows in long blades and the sun makes shadows look like roads. Glasswrights cut lenses, bridge pins, heat jars, and oath-glass. They are practical engineers who distrust spells that cannot be measured, repaired, or sold. Their workshops are some of the safest anchors in the known world, but their control of anchor-making gives them disproportionate political power.

### Morrow Abbey and the Gatehouse

Morrow Abbey stands on a dry island between the river branches. Its public library preserves charters, survey books, and the earliest surviving road records. Beneath it is the Gatehouse, a buried archive of possible continuations built into the abbey’s foundations. The Gatehouse is not a god, a dungeon, or a friendly machine. It is an old civic structure whose rules were damaged by repeated use.

### The Morrow Coast

A chain of salt ports where the Sable River meets the sea. Tide roads, ferry cooperatives, and shipwright families trade glass, grain, and preserved fruit. Coastal communities are the first to organize a direct council with the Bellweather Archive, because they have the strongest practical reason to communicate across paths without surrendering local control.

### The Salt Verge and the Sable Fen

The eastern roads end in salt flats and reed wetlands. The Fen is not empty: it contains small communities living around floating bridge-piles, old ferry rights, and channels that shift after magical storms. The Salt Verge hides several sealed Gatehouse anchors. Later volumes reveal that one of the culled paths has left traces in both regions.

## History

### Before the First Quiet

Early magic was understood as **oathcraft**: a person, household, guild, or ruler made a bounded promise to a material or social reality. Oaths gave shape to cause and effect, which is why a promise about a bridge could hold a bridge and a promise about succession could distort who was considered legitimate.

The earliest oaths were local and specific: keep a ford open, honor a boundary at harvest, bury a name in a field. A powerful court eventually tried to bind every subject to one succession, one harvest, and one definition of the Crown. The resulting conflict is called the **First Quiet** in later records because the legal language disappeared rather than the consequences.

### The Gatehouse and the Quiet Assembly

The Quiet Assembly was a loose council of river communities, road crews, archivists, and oath-smiths. It built the Gatehouse after seeing the First Quiet spread across several valleys. Its founding principle was simple and politically dangerous:

> A living possibility may not be closed by force while a conscious life inside it remains.

The Gatehouse was meant to let communities compare possible continuations, negotiate trade and refuge, and preserve names when a local catastrophe was disputed. It was not designed to grant one hero authority over every world. Its original consent rule required a living anchor and, for opening or maintaining a deliberate contact, the agreement of at least two independent custodians. A damaged legacy stitch can produce Aren’s first uncontrolled crossing, but that bypass is a fault in the old system rather than a normal entry method.

### The Lantern Council

The Crown of Neyra later created the **Lantern Council** to judge oaths, certify succession, and maintain the public road network. The Council is not a secret evil order. It supplied courts, bridge standards, grain reserves, and the first reliable map of the western marches. Its institutional habit, however, is to label one approved account as the only lawful history.

The Council altered the Gatehouse’s consent rule by degrees. First it treated a missing witness as a dead witness. Then it treated an unrepresented path as a failed path. Finally it authorized selective severing whenever a continuation was judged likely to cause instability. The **Culling Order** is the name used by the people who carried out these severances; the Council calls them emergency closures.

### The Unwritten Wound

The Unwritten Wound is a spreading zone where promises, laws, and cause-and-effect stop agreeing with one another. It is not a sentient monster. Its symptoms include a road that reaches two destinations, a person who cannot be remembered by two witnesses at once, a bridge that is intact in one map and missing in another, and legal decisions that reverse when a different clerk reads them.

The Wound grows wherever a living path is forcibly closed or when a major oath is used as a one-way command. It is a consequence of historical coercion, not a convenient monster that can be killed. The final plan contains it at a visible wound and builds institutions that prevent new forced closures.

### The Bellweather Sundering

Eleven years before the opening, the Lantern Council identified the first severe cluster of the Wound around Bellweather. A succession dispute, a contaminated oath-stone, and a Gatehouse resonance had made local memories incompatible. Marshal Edric Hunn authorized a full severance of the city and its surrounding villages. The official account blames Captain Aren’s failed bridge defense and calls the event a magical fever.

The truth is more damaging: Edric knew the cull would kill everyone inside the severing radius. He was convinced that the alternative would let the Wound consume several kingdoms. Aren was sent to hold the bridge, was not told the true purpose of the operation, and failed to reach the inner city before the cut. He is guilty of bad judgment and obedience, but he did not author the order. The path of living Bellweather is a continuation that the cull was meant to erase before it could become an independent witness.

## Factions and institutions

- **The Crown of Neyra:** a federation of river provinces under a ceremonial monarch. It funds roads and stores grain, but cannot control every local assembly.
- **Lantern Council:** oath judges, archive keepers, and royal road administrators. Some members want repair; many defend the official history because admitting a living cull occurred would destabilize succession and trade.
- **The Bellweather Archive:** a small public records office led by Mara Vey. It preserves charters, damage reports, and erased names rather than weapons.
- **Roadwardens:** local bridge and road workers. Aren’s former order is now split between Crown loyalists, independent crews, and people who remember what Bellweather paid.
- **The Quiet Assembly:** a dispersed guild of old path-keepers, oath-smiths, and bridge engineers. Its surviving records are incomplete and often contradictory.
- **The Culling Order:** Edric’s enforcement network. It seals anchors, confiscates path journals, and calls forced severance an emergency measure.
- **The Glasswright Cantons:** independent workshops that manufacture anchor-glass and maintain the most stable crossings. They are not automatically good; they want their craft protected from Crown control.
- **Path communities:** societies living inside continuations. They are not a single faction, and their representatives may disagree sharply about contact with the main world.

## Everyday life and economy

Oathlight is used for practical things: keeping a well from poisoning, warming a seedbed through a cold night, sealing a wine vault, making a bridge marker glow for a few hours, or guiding a night ferry. The most common working requires a visible anchor and a real counterweight. People know the formulas, but formulas do not guarantee a good decision.

Road work is political. A bridge repaired for a merchant route can deprive a village of its toll income. A ferry guild can keep a town alive while a new road makes it obsolete. Officials publish safety rules that favor central control because central control is easier to inspect than a thousand local compromises.

## Path worlds

A path is a full continuation with its own weather, institutions, family trees, and local memory. The following are planned major path families, not disposable levels:

### Bellweather-Living

The city survived because a bridge guild refused the Council’s cull. Its people live under a hereditary debt that makes every public decision expensive. The first path arc proves that the erased citizens are conscious and that their history is not a reward Aren can claim.

### The Ninefold Winter

A frozen river valley where nine generations have kept a civic promise to keep the hearth fires burning. The local council can end the winter by breaking the promise, but the choice will redistribute heat and food unevenly. Aren must learn that a path can reject his preferred solution.

### The Glasswright Cantons of Lume

A path where glasswrights control the weather by shaping a public lattice. Their system is stable only while every craftsperson is represented. A faction begins removing inexperienced workers from the lattice, and the resulting fracture threatens the entire region.

### The Quiet Orchard

A prosperous agricultural continuation with no recorded war. Its citizens have arranged a pleasant life by suppressing dissent and refusing to record certain kinds of loss. The apparent safety is a warning about a world managed into silence, not a paradise to be imposed elsewhere.

### The Hollow Country

A path emptied by an earlier cull. It contains ruined towns, unanswered bells, and the physical residue of histories that no living witness can confirm. It cannot be reset. Aren can learn what a forced closing leaves behind, but he cannot bring its missing people back merely by finding the right door.

### The Morrow Coast Commons

A coastal path where several towns share a living tide road. It offers the first durable model for voluntary inter-path trade, but its system depends on public records and will be captured by the Council if the Archive refuses to share custody.

## Path life and ethics

People inside a path have subjective continuity. They do not begin empty at each visit, and they do not reset when Aren leaves. They may discover that their world differs from a story they were told, but the story is not automatically false; it is evidence shaped by their local history. Repeated crossings can leave traces: a shared song, a road that recognizes Aren, a person who dreams of a bridge that does not exist in his own country.

Aren has no right to reveal a path’s secrets to the main world merely because revelation would help his case. Local custodians decide what can cross the boundary. A path can refuse to communicate, close its doors, or ask him to leave. Those decisions are plot consequences, not inconveniences.

## Visual and sensory anchors

- Wet bridge stone that holds a footprint after rain.
- Bronze toll bells with hairline seams filled by dull red oathlight.
- Reeds cut in straight lines where a road used to be.
- Paper that turns to gray pulp when a record is intentionally severed.
- The smell of hot glass, river mud, lamp oil, and turned earth.
- A path opening as a fold in rain, not a glowing videogame doorway.
- The Gatehouse voice arriving as one brief line in Aren’s own perception, often when he is alone or touching an anchor.

## World rules that must remain true

1. The Gatehouse cannot create a path from nothing; it can open an existing continuation when a living anchor and a remembered hinge remain.
2. A path cannot be reset while conscious life exists inside it.
3. Closing a route is not the same as destroying the route. People may continue to live there without Aren.
4. Magic is local, bounded, and paid for. It cannot solve an entire social problem without an anchor, a method, and a human consequence.
5. Every path has ordinary people and work that matter even when the central mystery moves elsewhere.
6. History is politically controlled, but a falsified record can still contain real facts.
7. The Unwritten Wound is a consequence of forced history, not a conveniently inserted final monster.
