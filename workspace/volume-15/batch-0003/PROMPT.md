## 4. What the block must contain

**The gift, and it is the broom.** **The broom on that east rise is about nine acres and it comes on about the fourth of Rainmonth and it is the only broom within a day's walk**, and a young man of about nineteen said on a hill in the middle of Thawmonth that he would not come up again until the fourth of Rainmonth, and **this block is the first time in about four and a half years that anybody in nine counties goes up that lane for the thing the lane is actually for.** **He goes on his own business and nobody sends him and nobody thanks him.** The broom is on a rise **about four hundred yards up the east rise and about two hundred yards below the top of the hill, and getting to it from the low end of that line of nine stones means going past the stones.** **NOBODY CROSSES THE NINE STONES AND NOTHING IS SHOWN OF THE FAR SIDE. Write the block so that the whole of its finding is on this side.**

**The gift, second half: the road that stops being made.** **Nobody in nine counties can say where the kerb stops.** A beekeeper saw it stop on the twelfth of Thawmonth and about nine hundred yards further on there was a line in the grass and then nine stones. **A road that goes between two places and not off a hill is a road that goes between two places and both of them are under the grass, and about four people in nine counties have said that and about five have said that a road that goes across a shoulder and not off it is a road that goes to a place that is not there any more, and those two sets have never spoken to the other.** **This block's job is to put the two accounts of that road in front of each other and to give the finding to the trade that has the standing to give it**, which is a man who lays stone on the low side of a hill for about nineteen years and has never once laid stone on the flat, or a man who keeps four miles of made road under contract, or the young man of about nineteen, who says in his own order and not anybody's order what is on the flat and how long it has been going and what it is for.

**The midpoint reversal, in the series row's own words and nothing more.** **Aren Kest's pathmark offers a route that would restore his missing childhood, and taking it would expose and destabilise every path still connected to him. HE DOES NOT TAKE IT. **The ordinary half, and it is long.** Rainmonth coming in; a drove on Thursdays; the hives and about three frames of brood where there were nine, and the frames coming back; nine yards of a wall at the head of the made road and a man who has been rebuilding it alone since the second of Mudmonth; the gap, which is four feet high and about nine yards long; the market street on a Saturday; the weigh-house and about four hundred and nine parish rolls a month; the room over the weigh-house; and about four hundred people who have heard four things and have not acted on them. **And the broom itself, cut and carried and made into something, because a thing that is found is not a thing that is used and this manuscript is about the difference.**

## 5. The ending of the block

**A completed beat and a new question.** The road is walked, the broom is cut, and the finding is on the ground and it is about keeping, not about arriving. **The block's last page is not the shoulder, not the stones and not the broom: it is the fact that a thing about four and a half years old is now being said out loud in two trades in the same market street, and that nobody in nine counties has a way of holding a finding once it is out of somebody's mouth and in nobody's hand.** Nobody thanks anybody. The reader is left with a question that a man with no standing cannot put, for the same reason he did not put the last one.

## 7. The house prose, and the four mechanical rules that are not taste

**The voice is in Chapters 0714, 0716, 0718 and 0720.** Read them before writing a sentence and match them: short declaratives, a flat cadence, a great many plain negatives, the census sentence where who-understood is the point, and physical work, weather and distance everywhere else.

1. **Zero em dashes, zero en dashes, zero semicolons, zero exclamation marks, zero single asterisks and therefore no italics, zero straight double quotes and zero curly apostrophes.** Speech takes curly doubles. Apostrophes are straight. Per-line bold parity even on every line. Every file ends in a newline.
2. **One footed money column per chapter**: ten items, priced 1 to 41, a mark at forty-eight pence in every conversion, and the coda that names the figures in the chapter that are not in it. **THE CEILING IS 332 PENCE AND IT IS ARITHMETIC: with ten distinct prices drawn from 1 to 41 the largest achievable total is 332, and no column may carry a printed total above it.** **The barred numerals are 14, 20, 26, 33, 38 and 41 and none of them may be a price, an over-figure or a printed total.** Foot the column four ways and check the conversion separately from the sum.
3. **`about` density.** Volume 15 Batch 0002 ran 22.26 to 27.67 per 1,000 with no page at or above 28.00, and Volume 14's last block ran 23.00 to 27.69 with none. **Aim for 22 to 27 and no page at or above 28.00. Keep the census sentence for the two or three beats in a chapter where who understood is the point and write the rest as action, weather or silence.** Chapter length: Volume 15 Batch 0002 ran at a mean of 3,397 and its minimum chapter is 3,125. **Do not write below 3,000.**
4. **Every count attached to a printed speech is footed off the printed words beside it**, and where a claim cannot be counted off the words printed next to it, the claim is cut rather than left wrong. This is the single most repeated finding in this manuscript's reviews.
